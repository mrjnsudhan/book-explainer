The ONE Thing,
Explained

Gary Keller built Keller Williams into one of the biggest property firms in the world, and he says the secret was not doing more but doing less. The ONE Thing argues that extraordinary results come from finding the single most important thing, doing it first, and letting it knock over the rest like a line of dominoes. This guide takes all 18 chapters one at a time, with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

Part zero · the idea

The ONE Thing

Before the lies and the method, Keller sets out the idea itself: extraordinary results come from going small, lining up one thing after another like dominoes, and the clue is everywhere you look at success.

The idea behind this part

Going small is not thinking small. It means ignoring all the things you could do so you can do the one thing you should.

Chapter 1 · Why going small wins

The ONE Thing

In short

When you want the biggest results, you need to narrow your focus to the one thing that matters most. Everything else can wait.

The big ideas

  • Curly's secret. In the 1991 film City Slickers, an old cowboy tells a stressed city man that the secret of life is one thing. Just one. You stick to that and the rest doesn't mean much. What is that one thing? That's what you have to find out.
  • Going small. Most people try to do too much and end up with a pile of half-finished work. Keller argues for the opposite: work out what matters most and give it your full attention.
  • Two rabbits. An old proverb says that if you chase two rabbits, you will catch neither. Split attention splits results.
  • It applies everywhere. The idea is not only for work. It works for your health, your relationships and your money too, as long as you ask what matters most in each area.
From the book

Keller says that when he looked back over his own career, his best results always came after he narrowed his focus, and his worst came when he spread himself across too many things at once. The same pattern showed up when he coached others. The people who got ahead were not doing more. They were doing less, and doing it better.

In plain words

If you want something extraordinary, you cannot give it a fraction of your attention. Pick the one thing that matters most and protect it. The rest is not banned, it just waits its turn.

Chapter 2 · Small wins add up, in order

The Domino Effect

In short

A single domino can knock over one about half again as big. Line up the right things in order and small efforts grow into huge results.

The big ideas

  • The physics. In 1983, the physicist Lorne Whitehead showed that a domino can topple another about 50 percent larger than itself. That growth compounds quickly.
  • From two inches to the moon. Start with a two-inch domino. By the 10th you are at the height of a tall American football quarterback. The 18th would rival the Leaning Tower of Pisa, the 23rd would top the Eiffel Tower, the 31st would beat Mount Everest by nearly 3,000 feet, and the 57th would almost reach the moon.
  • One at a time. Extraordinary success is sequential, not simultaneous. You do the right thing, then the next right thing. Each one makes the next one possible.
  • Find the lead domino. The job is to find the first domino in the line, the one that sets the rest in motion, and push it over today.
From the book

Keller uses Whitehead's experiment because it shows something we underrate: geometric growth. One domino knocking over another of the same size gets you nowhere fast. One knocking over one a bit bigger, again and again, gets you somewhere astonishing. Skills, knowledge and savings grow the same way when you build them in order.

In plain words

Don't try to knock over every domino at once. Find the first one in the line and push it. When it falls, it gives you the push you need for the next, larger one.

Chapter 3 · Look closely at anyone who wins big

Success Leaves Clues

In short

Look at any big success, a company or a person, and you'll find a ONE Thing at its heart: one product, one skill, one person who made the difference.

The big ideas

  • Companies. Coors is known for beer, KFC for the Colonel's secret recipe, Starbucks for coffee and Google for search. Each has a single product or service that made it and still carries it.
  • One at a time. Even companies famous for many products tend to have one that matters most at any given moment, and their attention moves from one big hit to the next.
  • People. Behind many successful people there is one passion, one skill or one person who changed everything. Bill Gates turned a teenage fascination with computers into a skill that let him co-found Microsoft.
  • Your turn. If you look honestly at your own wins, you will usually find the same pattern: a time when one thing had your full attention.
From the book

Keller asks readers to treat other people's success as evidence. If the same pattern shows up again and again, in brands, founders, athletes and artists, it is probably not luck. The clue is that something narrow and specific sits at the centre, and everything else grew out of it.

In plain words

When you study people and businesses that did very well, don't be dazzled by everything they do now. Look for the one thing they did first and best. That's the clue you can copy.

Part one · the six lies

The Lies

Six beliefs sound sensible and quietly wreck our focus: that everything matters equally, that we can multitask, that we need iron discipline, that willpower is always there, that life should be balanced, and that big is dangerous.

The idea behind this part

Each lie spreads your effort thinner. Unlearn them, and the path to a single priority clears.

Lie 1 · The first lie: all tasks are equal

Everything Matters Equally

In short

Things don't matter equally. A to-do list treats them as if they do. A success list ranks them and finds the few that matter most, and then the one.

The big ideas

  • To-do lists lie. A to-do list is a list of things you could do, not a plan of what you should do. Crossing off easy items feels productive, but it often keeps you busy rather than effective.
  • The 80/20 rule. In the 1890s the economist Vilfredo Pareto noticed that about 20 percent of the people in Italy owned about 80 percent of the land. Later, the quality expert Joseph Juran named the wider pattern: a small share of causes produces most of the effects.
  • Go extreme. Keller says don't stop at 20 percent. Take the vital 20 percent of your list, then the vital 20 percent of that, and keep going until you are left with one thing.
  • A success list. Rank by results, not by urgency. The question is not what's next, but what matters most.
From the book

Keller quotes Andrew Carnegie's advice: put all your eggs in one basket, and then watch that basket. His point is that busyness is not the same as progress. The person who does fewer things, chosen well, beats the person who does more things, chosen by habit.

In plain words

Your list will always be longer than your day. Instead of working through it top to bottom, rank it by what actually moves results, and keep cutting until one thing is left. Do that first.

Next: Multitasking →
Lie 2 · The second lie: you can do two things at once

Multitasking

In short

You can do two things at once, like walk and talk, but you cannot focus on two things at once. What feels like multitasking is fast switching, and every switch costs time and quality.

The big ideas

  • A computer word. Multitasking was a term for computers that seemed to run several jobs at once. Even they don't: a single processor switches quickly between tasks.
  • Switching costs. Each time you switch, your brain has to reorient: remember where you were, reload the rules and get back up to speed. Those small costs add up.
  • Lost time. Keller cites research suggesting office workers lose more than a quarter of the workday to interruptions and the time it takes to recover from them.
  • Worse at everything. A 2009 Stanford study led by Clifford Nass found that heavy multitaskers were worse at filtering out irrelevant information and at switching tasks than people who rarely multitasked.
From the book

Keller points out that a juggler is not juggling three balls at once. They are catching and tossing one ball at a time, very quickly. Our attention works the same way. The more balls we try to keep in the air, the less attention each one gets, and the more often one falls.

In plain words

When something matters, give it your undivided attention. Close the tabs, silence the phone, and work on one thing until you reach a natural stopping point.

Next: A disciplined life →
Lie 3 · The third lie: you must be disciplined all the time

A Disciplined Life

In short

You don't need to be a disciplined person. You need enough selected discipline to build the right habit. Once it's a habit, it takes far less effort to keep going.

The big ideas

  • Selected discipline. Successful people aren't disciplined about everything. They use discipline on a few things, for long enough to turn them into habits.
  • Habits carry you. Once a behaviour is a habit, it runs on its own. Discipline gets you started; habit keeps you going.
  • About 66 days. A 2009 study at University College London led by Phillippa Lally found that new habits took 66 days on average to become automatic, with a wide range from 18 to 254 days.
  • One at a time. Build one habit, then the next. Stacking them in order is the domino effect applied to your behaviour.
From the book

Keller gives the example of the swimmer Michael Phelps, who as a boy was restless and struggled to focus. He channelled his energy into swimming and trained relentlessly, day after day for years. His discipline was not spread across his whole life. It was aimed at one thing, until training was simply what he did.

In plain words

You don't have to become a different, iron-willed person. Pick one habit that matters, push yourself to do it every day for about two months, and it will start to run itself. Then pick the next one.

Lie 4 · The fourth lie: willpower is unlimited

Willpower Is Always on Will-Call

In short

Willpower is not always there when you need it. It fades as the day goes on and as you make decisions, so do your most important work early, before it runs low.

The big ideas

  • Like a phone battery. Keller describes willpower as something that starts the day charged and drains as you use it, then recharges with rest and food.
  • The brain is hungry. Your brain makes up about 2 percent of your body weight but uses around a fifth of your energy. Hard thinking and self-control are tiring.
  • The marshmallow test. In Walter Mischel's studies, children who could wait for a second treat used tricks such as looking away rather than sheer grit. Managing your situation beats relying on willpower.
  • Time your ONE Thing. Schedule your most important task for when your willpower is highest, which for most people is the morning.
From the book

Keller describes an experiment by Roy Baumeister in which hungry students sat by a plate of warm cookies. Some could eat them; others had to eat radishes and resist. Afterwards, the radish eaters gave up much sooner on an impossible puzzle. Keller's point: self-control in one place can leave less of it for the next thing.

Worth knowing

the idea that willpower is a limited fuel that runs out, called ego depletion, has been hard to repeat. A large replication in 2016 across more than 20 labs found little or no effect. The practical advice still holds for many people, because fatigue, hunger and decision overload are real, but the battery is a metaphor, not a proven law.

In plain words

Don't save your most important work for the end of the day, when you're tired and have made a hundred little choices. Do it first, and put your day's easy tasks afterwards.

Next: A balanced life →
Lie 5 · The fifth lie: you should balance everything

A Balanced Life

In short

Extraordinary results need time and focus, which means some areas of life will get less attention for a while. Don't chase balance; counterbalance, and never neglect what you can't get back.

The big ideas

  • The middle is a myth. Trying to give every part of your life equal time means nothing gets enough to be extraordinary. Balance sounds healthy but it usually means mediocre.
  • Counterbalance. Keller suggests thinking of a tightrope walker: you lean one way, then correct. You can go out of balance, as long as you come back.
  • Long at work. At work, it's fine to go long on your ONE Thing for weeks or months, then counterbalance with time off and attention elsewhere.
  • Short at home. In your personal life, keep the swings short. Family, friends, health and your own wellbeing can't be left alone for long.
From the book

Keller's picture is of someone balancing on a high wire. They are never perfectly still. They lean, feel the pull and lean back. The skill is not in being still but in knowing which way you're leaning and how long you can stay there before something important falls.

In plain words

It's fine to throw yourself into something for a season. Just know you're doing it, keep coming back to the people and the health you depend on, and don't let the swing go on so long that something breaks.

Next: Big is bad →
Lie 6 · The sixth lie: big goals are dangerous

Big Is Bad

In short

Thinking big is not reckless. A big goal forces you to ask better questions and find better ways, while a small goal lets you get by with what you already do.

The big ideas

  • Fear of big. People avoid big goals because they fear failure, pressure or looking foolish. So they set goals they already know how to hit.
  • Big changes the question. How do I get a bit more? gets you a slightly busier version of today. How could I double it? forces a whole new plan.
  • Aim past the target. Keller's advice: if 10 is a good goal, aim for 20 and plan for it. You may land short of 20, but you'll likely pass 10.
  • Growth mindset. Thinking big works best with the belief that your ability can grow, so setbacks are lessons rather than proof you aren't good enough.
From the book

Keller asks his readers to imagine the life they'd have if they set the bar higher. His argument is simple: no one ever hit a target they never aimed at. The path that a big goal demands is different from the path a small one demands, and the big path often turns out to be the better one.

In plain words

Ask yourself what you'd aim for if you weren't afraid, then plan as if you meant it. A bigger goal makes you look for better ways to work, and even falling short of it can leave you far ahead.

Part two · the simple path

The Truth

The book's tool is one question, asked often: What's the ONE Thing I can do such that by doing it everything else will be easier or unnecessary? This part shows how to ask it, make it a habit and get great answers to it.

The idea behind this part

A great question is big and specific. A great answer is found by researching what the best have done, then going one step further.

Chapter 10 · One question to find your ONE Thing

The Focusing Question

In short

Ask: What's the ONE Thing I can do such that by doing it everything else will be easier or unnecessary? Use it for the big picture and for right now.

The big ideas

  • The question. The full wording is: What's the ONE Thing I can do such that by doing it everything else will be easier or unnecessary? Every part does work.
  • What's the ONE Thing? Asks for a single action, not a list. It forces you to choose.
  • I can do. Makes it about action you can take yourself, not something you hope will happen.
  • Easier or unnecessary. This is the test of a lead domino: once it falls, the next steps get easier, or disappear altogether.
  • Two sizes. The big-picture version is What's my ONE Thing? for your life and career. The small-focus version is What's my ONE Thing right now? for the next hour.
From the book

Keller calls the focusing question the most important habit in the book. It is how you find the lead domino. He suggests asking it about every area of life, from your health to your money, and about every time frame, from someday to this very moment.

In plain words

Whenever you're unsure what to do, ask the focusing question. It turns a cloud of possible tasks into one clear next action.

Chapter 11 · Make the question automatic

The Success Habit

In short

The focusing question only works if you ask it. Make it a daily habit: ask it every morning, act on the answer, and keep it in front of you until it runs on its own.

The big ideas

  • Understand and believe it. First accept that focusing on one thing really does lead to bigger results. You won't build a habit you don't believe in.
  • Use it every day. Ask the question each morning, first thing, and let the answer shape your day.
  • Make it a habit. Keep asking until it's automatic. Building a habit takes about 66 days, so plan for two months, not two days.
  • Leave reminders. Put the question somewhere you'll see it. Keller and his team even used signs reading Until my ONE Thing is done, everything else is a distraction.
  • Recruit support. Tell the people around you what you're doing. They can protect your time and hold you to it.
From the book

Keller describes how the people around him started using the question with each other: in meetings, in check-ins and at the end of the day. Once a group asks it together, it stops being a technique and becomes part of how they work.

In plain words

Knowing the question doesn't help unless you ask it. Put it where you'll see it each morning, ask it before anything else, and keep going until you don't need the reminder.

Next: The path to great answers →
Chapter 12 · Big and specific questions, researched answers

The Path to Great Answers

In short

A great question is big and specific. A great answer comes from research: find what the best people have done, then go one step further.

The big ideas

  • Four kinds of question. Questions can be small or big, and broad or specific. Small and broad gives you nothing to act on. Small and specific is easy but limited. Big and broad leaves too many options. Big and specific is the great question.
  • An example. How can I sell more books? is small and broad. How can I double my book sales in six months? is big and specific, and it tells you what success looks like and by when.
  • Three kinds of answer. A doable answer is within your reach already. A stretch answer is at the edge of your ability and needs research and practice. A possibility answer is beyond what you know and needs you to learn and innovate.
  • Benchmark and trend. To find a possibility answer, study the best results anyone has achieved, look at where things are heading, and then push past that.
From the book

Keller argues that most people settle for doable answers because they are comfortable. But the answers that change things usually sit just outside what you already know. You find them by looking at people who've already gone furthest, and treating their best as your starting point.

In plain words

Ask questions that are big enough to stretch you and specific enough to act on. Then don't stop at the first answer you can think of. Find out what the best have done and see if you can go past it.

Part three · living it

Extraordinary Results

Purpose sets your direction, priority turns it into a ONE Thing for right now, and productivity is the time block where you actually do it. Then come the three commitments, the four thieves to guard against, and the journey.

The idea behind this part

Purpose, priority and productivity are an iceberg: what people see is your results, but the bulk of it is underneath.

Chapter 13 · Purpose sets the direction

Live with Purpose

In short

Purpose is the reason behind your ONE Thing. Things and money give short bursts of happiness that fade. Meaning and achievement give a deeper, more lasting kind.

The big ideas

  • The iceberg. Keller pictures purpose, priority and productivity as an iceberg. People see your productivity; underneath, holding it up, are your priority and, deepest of all, your purpose.
  • Happiness on the way. Happiness happens on the way to fulfilment. Chasing happiness directly tends to fail; pursuing something meaningful brings it along.
  • Fleeting and lasting. Keller cites the psychologist Martin Seligman: pleasure fades quickly, while engagement, relationships, meaning and achievement give a longer-lasting sense of wellbeing.
  • Find your big why. Ask what you'd want your life to stand for. You don't need a perfect answer; pick a direction and refine it as you go.
From the book

Keller notes that people who chase money for its own sake often find that it doesn't satisfy them for long. Money and things are useful, but they are tools for living a purpose, not the purpose itself. When your work connects to something you care about, the effort starts to pay you back.

In plain words

Know why you're doing what you do. A clear purpose makes it much easier to pick your ONE Thing, and it turns the hard days into steps toward something rather than just effort.

Next: Live by priority →
Chapter 14 · Goal-setting to the now

Live by Priority

In short

Connect today to someday. Start with your biggest goal, then work down through five years, one year, a month, a week and today, to the ONE Thing you should do right now.

The big ideas

  • One priority. The word priority was singular for centuries. Only recently did people start talking about many priorities. Keller wants it singular again.
  • We discount the future. People value a reward now far more than a bigger one later. A goal that's years away feels unreal, so we drift toward what's easy today.
  • Goal-setting to the now. The fix is to link each level to the next: my someday goal, so my five-year goal, so my one-year goal, then this month, this week, today, and right now.
  • Write it down. Keller cites research by Gail Matthews finding that people who wrote their goals down were about 40 percent more likely to achieve them.
From the book

Keller's ladder is a chain of focusing questions. Each answer becomes the context for the next, smaller question. By the time you reach right now, the one thing on your desk is directly connected to the life you want, which makes it far easier to choose it over the distractions.

In plain words

Your goals for today should be small pieces of your goals for this year, which should be pieces of your someday goal. Write the chain down, and the thing in front of you will never feel random.

Next: Live for productivity →
Chapter 15 · Time block your ONE Thing

Live for Productivity

In short

The most productive people block time for their ONE Thing and protect it. Schedule time off first, then your ONE Thing for about four hours a day, then time to plan.

The big ideas

  • Time blocking. Put an appointment in your calendar for your ONE Thing, and treat it as seriously as a meeting with your most important client.
  • Three blocks, in order. Block your time off first, then your ONE Thing, then a short planning time each week to review your goals and set the next week's blocks.
  • Four hours. Keller recommends about four hours a day on your ONE Thing, ideally early, before the day fills up.
  • Maker and manager. Paul Graham, co-founder of Y Combinator, wrote that makers need long uninterrupted stretches, while managers live in one-hour slots. He moved his meetings to the end of the day to protect his maker time.
  • Protect the block. Find a place you won't be disturbed, have what you need to hand, switch off distractions, and tell others why you're unavailable. If you have to move the block, reschedule it the same day.
From the book

Keller suggests making a calendar that only you can override, then defending it. When someone asks for time during your block, the honest answer is I have an appointment, and it's true. If something urgent truly can't wait, deal with it and then put the block back the same day.

In plain words

Decide in advance when you'll do your most important work, put it in the calendar, and guard it like a meeting you can't miss. Four focused hours a day is what moves the dominoes.

Next: The three commitments →
Chapter 16 · Mastery, a better approach, accountability

The Three Commitments

In short

To get the most out of your time block, commit to three things: follow the path of mastery, move from your usual approach to a better one, and own your results with the accountability cycle.

The big ideas

  • The path of mastery. Mastery is a lifelong path, not a finish line. Research often quoted says expert performance takes around 10,000 hours of deliberate practice, about three hours a day for ten years.
  • From E to P. Keller contrasts an entrepreneurial approach, doing things the way that comes naturally, with a purposeful approach, looking for the best possible way. When you hit a ceiling, the answer is not more effort but a new method.
  • The accountability cycle. Accountable people take the results they get, own them, look for solutions and act on them. Victims deny, blame others and wait.
  • Get a coach. Keller argues that almost every top performer has a coach, someone who sees what you can't and holds you to the standard.
From the book

Keller describes how people often reach a level and stall. They've maxed out what their natural approach can do. Trying harder at the same thing only gets you a little higher. The breakthrough comes from asking what the best in the world do differently, learning it, and climbing a new curve.

In plain words

Keep getting better at your ONE Thing, change your method when it stops working rather than just working harder, and take ownership of your results, good or bad.

Chapter 17 · What steals your productivity

The Four Thieves

In short

Four thieves will try to steal your time block: the inability to say no, the fear of chaos, poor health habits, and an environment that doesn't support your goals.

The big ideas

  • Not saying no. Every yes to something else is a no to your ONE Thing. Keller puts it this way: one yes must be defended over time by a thousand nos.
  • Fear of chaos. When you focus on one thing, other things will pile up and get messy. Accept that some chaos is the price of extraordinary results.
  • Poor health habits. You can't sustain focus on little sleep, bad food and no exercise. Keller suggests a routine for energy: meditate, eat well, exercise, spend time with loved ones, and get enough sleep.
  • An unsupportive environment. The people around you and your physical surroundings either help or hurt. Make sure both point toward your goal.
From the book

Keller lists the thieves because people often blame themselves for losing focus, when the real problem is that something keeps walking off with their time. Once you can name the thief, you can lock the door: say no more often, let small fires burn, look after your body, and shape your surroundings.

In plain words

Expect the four thieves, and plan for them. Practise saying no, let unimportant things stay untidy, protect your energy, and shape the people and places around you so they support your ONE Thing.

Next: The journey →
Chapter 18 · Living with no regrets

The Journey

In short

The ONE Thing is a journey made of one step after another. At the end of life, people rarely regret what they did as much as what they never tried.

The big ideas

  • Start now. You can't do everything at once, but you can do the first thing today. Then the next one.
  • Regret. Keller points to stories from palliative care nurse Bronnie Ware, whose patients most often regretted not living a life true to themselves, working too much, not expressing their feelings, losing touch with friends and not letting themselves be happier.
  • Inaction hurts longer. Psychologists Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec found that in the short term people regret things they did, but over a lifetime they regret the things they didn't do much more.
  • Live large. Ask the focusing question about the life you want, then knock over the first domino. Keller closes with a reminder that the dominoes add up to a life.
From the book

Keller ends the book by bringing it back to the domino line. Every great life is built the same way as every great business: one thing at a time, in the right order, each one making the next possible. The only way to fail at it is to never start.

In plain words

Don't wait until you have everything figured out. Pick your ONE Thing, start today, and keep knocking over the next domino. The regret you'll feel most is for the line you never began.
