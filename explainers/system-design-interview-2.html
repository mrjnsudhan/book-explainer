In a system design interview you get 45 minutes and a vague prompt like "design Google Maps". System Design Interview, Volume 2 works through thirteen of the hardest prompts, from finding restaurants near you to running a stock exchange, using the same four steps every time: pin down the scope, sketch the big picture, dig into the hard parts, and wrap up. The book in one line

Part one · location

Where Things Are

The book opens with three systems built around a place on Earth: a search for businesses near you, a feature that shows which friends are close by right now, and a full map with directions. They share one hard problem. A database is good at finding rows by a key, but "near me" is not a key, so each design has to turn two-dimensional distance into something a computer can look up fast.

The idea behind this part

Location problems become easy once you divide the map into cells with names, because then "nearby" is just "in the same cell or the next one".

Chapter 1 · Find the restaurants near me

Proximity Service

In short

Design the search behind "restaurants within 1 km", like Yelp. The trick is a geospatial index that splits the map into named cells, so finding nearby businesses means reading a few cells instead of scanning the whole world.

The big ideas

  • Read-heavy and simple to scale. With 100 million daily users and 200 million businesses, searches run at about 5,000 per second, while business owners rarely change their listings. A stateless location service behind a load balancer, plus read replicas of the database, handles that load.
  • Why not just compare latitude and longitude? A query like "latitude between A and B and longitude between C and D" has to intersect two huge ranges, and an ordinary index on each column can't do that efficiently. You need an index built for two dimensions.
  • Geohash. Geohash repeatedly halves the world, alternating longitude and latitude, and writes the result as a short base-32 string. Each extra character makes the cell smaller: 4 characters is about 39 by 20 km, 5 is about 5 by 5 km, 6 is about 1.2 km by 600 m. Nearby places usually share a prefix, so a search reads your cell and its eight neighbours to cover the edges.
  • Quadtrees and S2. A quadtree keeps splitting a square into four until each leaf holds at most about 100 businesses, so dense cities get tiny cells and the ocean stays one big one. The book estimates the whole tree fits in under 2 GB of memory. Google's S2 maps the sphere onto a Hilbert curve and is used at Google and Tinder.
Walking the four steps

Step one: ask how far users search (up to 20 km), whether results update while they walk (no), and how fast an owner's edits must appear (the next day is fine). Step two: split the system into a location-based service that answers searches and a business service that handles edits, with a primary database and read replicas. Step three: compare geospatial indexes, choose geohash or a quadtree, and cache results keyed by geohash cell. Step four: mention filtering by opening hours or category, and serving several regions from data centres close to users.

In plain words

When people ask for things near them, don't scan everything. Cut the map into cells with names, store each business under its cell, and look in a handful of cells around the user.

Chapter 2 · Who is close to me right now

Nearby Friends

In short

Design a feature that shows which of your friends are within 5 miles, updating as everyone moves. Unlike chapter 1, the data changes every few seconds, so the design is about pushing fresh locations to exactly the right people.

The big ideas

  • Locations go stale fast. With 10 million users online at once, each sending their position every 30 seconds, the system receives about 334,000 location updates per second. Nothing here is read-heavy; it's a constant stream of small writes that must reach friends quickly.
  • WebSockets keep the line open. Each phone holds a persistent WebSocket connection to a server, so the server can push a friend's new position the moment it arrives. These servers are stateful, which makes deploying and scaling them more careful work than for ordinary web servers.
  • Redis pub/sub as the switchboard. Every user gets a channel. When you move, your server publishes to your channel; each online friend's WebSocket handler is subscribed to it, checks the distance, and forwards the update only if you're within range. The latest location also sits in a Redis cache with a time-to-live, so inactive users fade out.
  • The fan-out is the cost. With about 400 friends each and 10 percent of them online, those 334,000 updates become roughly 14 million pushes a second. The book sizes the pub/sub cluster by CPU, not memory: at about 100,000 pushes per server, that's well over a hundred servers, spread with consistent hashing.
Walking the four steps

Step one: agree that "nearby" means within 5 miles, positions refresh every 30 seconds, and a short delay is fine. Step two: phones connect to WebSocket servers; a location cache stores each user's latest position; a history database (Cassandra suits the write load) keeps the trail; Redis pub/sub links each user to their friends. Step three: work out the fan-out, size and shard the pub/sub cluster, and handle adding or removing friends and users with thousands of friends. Step four: extend it to "nearby random people" by giving each geohash cell its own channel.

In plain words

If data changes every few seconds, don't make people ask for it. Keep a connection open, give every user a channel, and push each update only to the friends who are listening.

Next: Google Maps →
Chapter 3 · Tiles, routes and arrival times

Google Maps

In short

Design a simplified Google Maps with three jobs: draw the map, find the best route, and keep the arrival time honest as traffic changes. Each job uses a different trick: pre-drawn tiles on a CDN, a road graph cut into tiles, and a stream of live locations.

The big ideas

  • The map is pre-drawn tiles. The world is cut into 256 by 256 pixel images at 21 zoom levels, and each level has four times as many tiles as the one before. The book estimates about 4.4 trillion tiles at the closest zoom, roughly 100 PB for all levels before compressing the oceans and forests, so tiles are generated ahead of time and served from a CDN.
  • Location updates arrive in batches. A billion daily users navigating about 35 minutes a week would send millions of updates a second if each phone reported every second. Instead phones record every second but send a batch every 15 seconds, cutting the load to about 200,000 requests a second.
  • Routing on a tiled graph. Roads become a graph of intersections and segments. The full graph is too big for one machine, so it's cut into routing tiles, with three layers: small tiles of local streets, bigger ones of arterial roads and huge ones of highways. A route search like A* only loads the tiles it needs and hops up to highways for long trips.
  • ETA and rerouting. Live locations stream through Kafka into a traffic service, and machine learning combines current and historical traffic to predict arrival times. When conditions change, the navigation service finds which active users are affected and pushes new routes to them.
Walking the four steps

Step one: agree on the three features (location updates, navigation with arrival times, map rendering) and estimate storage for tiles and traffic for updates. Step two: a location service writes batched positions to a database like Cassandra and onto Kafka; a navigation service calls a geocoder, a route planner and a ranker; tiles come from a CDN using URLs built from geohashes. Step three: dig into the routing tile hierarchy, the ETA service and adaptive rerouting, and how updates reach phones through WebSockets. Step four: mention multi-stop routes and features like avoiding tolls.

In plain words

A map app is three systems wearing one coat: a giant cache of pictures, a graph search over roads, and a live traffic feed. Pre-compute what you can, split what's too big, and stream what changes.

Part two · streams

Data in Motion

The next three systems never stop receiving data: a message queue that passes events between services, a monitoring system that swallows millions of metrics a second, and a pipeline that counts ad clicks for billing. The common thread is the log, an append-only list of events that many readers can replay at their own pace.

The idea behind this part

Write everything to a durable log first, then process it. The log is what lets fast producers and slow consumers live together without losing anything.

Chapter 4 · A log that many can read

Distributed Message Queue

In short

Design something like Kafka: producers write messages to topics, consumers read them later, and nothing is lost when machines fail. The heart of it is a partitioned, replicated, append-only log on disk.

The big ideas

  • Topics, partitions, offsets. A topic is split into partitions spread across brokers. Each partition is an ordered log where every message gets an increasing offset. Messages with the same key go to the same partition, so their order is kept.
  • Sequential disk is fast. Brokers append messages to a write-ahead log split into segments and rely on the operating system's page cache. Writing and reading disk sequentially, in batches, is far faster than random access, which is why a message queue can keep weeks of data cheaply.
  • Consumer groups pull. Consumers in a group share a topic: each partition is read by exactly one consumer in the group, so adding consumers adds throughput, up to the number of partitions. Consumers pull at their own pace and commit their offset, and a coordinator rebalances partitions when members join or leave.
  • Replication and acks. Each partition has a leader and followers; followers that are caught up form the in-sync replica set. A producer chooses how long to wait: ack=0 doesn't wait, ack=1 waits for the leader, ack=all waits for every in-sync replica. That choice, plus how consumers commit offsets, decides at-most-once, at-least-once or exactly-once delivery.
Walking the four steps

Step one: settle on message size (kilobytes), ordering, retention (two weeks), throughput and the delivery guarantee users can choose. Step two: producers, brokers holding partitions, consumer groups, and storage for messages, consumer offsets and cluster metadata, with something like ZooKeeper coordinating. Step three: the on-disk log format, batching, the pull model and rebalancing, replication with in-sync replicas and acks, and scaling each part. Step four: extras such as message filtering by tag and delayed or scheduled messages.

In plain words

A message queue is a durable, shared to-do list. Split it into partitions so many machines can work in parallel, keep copies so a crash loses nothing, and let each reader track where it got to.

Chapter 5 · Watching ten million numbers

Metrics Monitoring and Alerting System

In short

Design an internal system like Datadog or Prometheus that collects CPU, memory and request numbers from every server, stores them as time series, draws dashboards and pages someone when things go wrong.

The big ideas

  • Ten million metrics. The book imagines 1,000 server pools of 100 machines, each reporting about 100 metrics, so roughly 10 million series. The load is constant heavy writing; reads are spiky, from dashboards and alert checks.
  • A time-series database. Each data point is a metric name, a set of labels, a timestamp and a value. A general database struggles with this pattern, so the book uses a purpose-built store such as InfluxDB or Prometheus, tuned for writes and queries over time ranges.
  • Pull or push. In a pull model, collectors scrape each server's metrics endpoint, which makes it easy to tell when a server is down. In a push model, agents on each server send metrics, which works better through firewalls and for short-lived jobs. Large systems often put Kafka between collectors and the database to absorb bursts.
  • Keep less, for longer. Raw data is kept for 7 days, one-minute summaries for 30 days and one-hour summaries for a year. Downsampling, compact encodings for timestamps and cold storage keep the bill sane, while an alert manager reads rules from config files, removes duplicates and sends alerts to email, text, PagerDuty or webhooks.
Walking the four steps

Step one: ask what's being monitored (infrastructure, not business metrics), how many machines, how long to keep data and at what resolution, and which alert channels. Step two: sketch data collection, a transport layer, the time-series store, a query service, alerting and visualization. Step three: pull vs push, scaling the pipeline with Kafka, where to aggregate, storage tricks, and building the alerting and dashboards (or buying Grafana). Step four: revisit the trade-offs and mention that off-the-shelf tools often win here.

In plain words

Monitoring is a firehose of tiny numbers. Store them in a database built for time, keep detail only while it's useful, and write alert rules that wait long enough to be sure.

Next: Ad click counting →
Chapter 6 · Counting clicks you get paid for

Ad Click Event Aggregation

In short

Design the pipeline that counts a billion ad clicks a day, per ad and per minute, and finds the most-clicked ads. Because advertisers are billed on these numbers, the counts must be correct, not just fast.

The big ideas

  • The numbers. A billion clicks a day is about 10,000 a second on average and 50,000 at peak, over 2 million ads. The system must answer two queries: clicks for an ad in the last few minutes, and the top 100 ads in the past minute, with filters such as country.
  • A streaming pipeline. Raw click logs flow into Kafka, an aggregation service counts them using a MapReduce-style graph of map, aggregate and reduce nodes, and results flow through a second Kafka topic into a database for queries. Raw data is kept too, so numbers can be recalculated if a bug is found.
  • Event time, not arrival time. A click can reach the system seconds or minutes late. Counting by when the click happened (event time) is accurate but means waiting for stragglers. A watermark sets how long to wait: longer catches more late clicks but adds delay. Counts are made in windows: tumbling one-minute windows for per-ad counts, sliding windows for the top ads.
  • Exactly once and reconciliation. The book uses one streaming path (the Kappa architecture) rather than separate batch and stream systems. To avoid double counting after a crash, the aggregator saves its position in Kafka together with its results, and a nightly batch job reconciles the streaming totals against raw data.
Walking the four steps

Step one: confirm the queries, the volume, the acceptable delay (a few minutes), and that correctness matters because of billing. Step two: define the query API, store raw and aggregated data, and connect log watchers, Kafka, the aggregation service and the databases. Step three: choose event time with watermarks, pick window types, guarantee exactly-once processing, handle hot ads, recover from failures with snapshots, and reconcile. Step four: monitor the pipeline and mention alternatives like a hosted service.

In plain words

When counts mean money, count by when things happened, wait a moment for the stragglers, and check the totals again later from the raw data.

Part three · state

State at Scale

Four systems that hold data people care about: hotel rooms that must never be sold twice, a billion mailboxes, a storage service for a hundred petabytes of files, and a leaderboard that must rank millions of players in real time. Each one picks the right storage for the job and then fights a different enemy: races, size, disk failures or slow sorting.

The idea behind this part

Pick the data store by its access pattern, then decide what you will give up. Every design here trades a little of something to get a lot of something else.

Chapter 7 · Never sell the last room twice

Hotel Reservation System

In short

Design the booking system for a hotel chain like Marriott. The traffic is modest, but two guests must never get the same room, the same click must never book twice, and the hotel wants to overbook a little on purpose.

The big ideas

  • Small numbers, strict rules. With 5,000 hotels and a million rooms, about 70 percent full and three-night stays, the book estimates around 240,000 bookings a day, only about 3 a second. A relational database with ACID transactions is the natural choice.
  • Book a room type, not a room. Guests reserve "a king room" for given dates, and the specific room is assigned at check-in. Inventory is a table with one row per hotel, room type and date, holding the total rooms and how many are reserved. The hotel can allow about 10 percent overbooking.
  • Two ways to double-book. The same user can click Book twice, which an idempotency key fixes: the reservation ID is created before submitting and is the primary key, so the second insert fails. Or two users can race for the last room, which needs concurrency control.
  • Locks, versions or constraints. Pessimistic locking (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE) makes the second buyer wait. Optimistic locking adds a version number and rejects a write if the row changed since it was read. A database constraint refuses any update where reserved exceeds total. The book prefers optimistic locking or constraints when contention is low, which it usually is.
Walking the four steps

Step one: agree on the scale, booking by room type, overbooking and price changes per day. Step two: design the API and data model (hotel, room type inventory, reservation) and split services for hotels, rates, reservations, payments and management behind a gateway. Step three: handle concurrency with idempotency and locking, scale to booking.com size by sharding on hotel ID and caching inventory in Redis, and keep reservation and inventory data consistent, ideally in one database rather than a saga across services. Step four: recap the choices.

In plain words

When two people want the last of something, don't trust what you read a moment ago. Either lock it, check it hasn't changed when you write, or let the database refuse an impossible number.

Chapter 8 · A billion inboxes

Distributed Email Service

In short

Design Gmail-scale email: send and receive, folders, search, and keeping spam out of the inbox. The old one-server mail designs don't scale, so the book rebuilds email around web servers, real-time servers and a custom metadata store.

The big ideas

  • Huge storage, steady traffic. A billion users sending about 10 emails a day is around 100,000 sends a second. Receiving 40 a day at about 50 KB of metadata each comes to roughly 730 PB a year, and attachments add even more, so storage dominates the design.
  • The protocols. SMTP moves mail between servers; POP and IMAP let clients fetch it; modern webmail uses HTTP. A sender finds the recipient's mail server through a DNS MX record.
  • Store by mailbox. Email metadata is partitioned by user ID, so one user's mail lives together, and sorted by a time-based email ID. Read and unread mail go into separate tables to make "show unread" fast, attachments go to object storage, and recent mail is cached. A single primary per mailbox favours consistency over availability.
  • Getting into the inbox. Sending is easy; being trusted is hard. Use dedicated IP addresses, warm them up slowly over weeks, separate marketing from transactional mail, process bounces and complaints quickly, ban spammers, and sign mail with SPF, DKIM and DMARC so receivers can check it really came from you.
Walking the four steps

Step one: confirm the features (send, receive, fetch, folders, search, spam filtering) and estimate the traffic and storage. Step two: walk through how traditional mail servers work and why they don't scale, then propose webmail, web servers, real-time servers on WebSockets, a metadata database, an attachment store, a cache and a search store, with separate sending and receiving flows through queues. Step three: choose the metadata store and data model, keep threads together with message headers, handle consistency, deliverability and search. Step four: scaling across regions and other extras.

In plain words

Email at scale is a storage problem dressed as a messaging problem. Keep each mailbox together, separate what people search for from what they just read, and earn the trust of other mail servers slowly.

Chapter 9 · A hundred petabytes that never vanish

S3-like Object Storage

In short

Design a storage service like Amazon S3: put files (objects) into buckets, get them back by name, and make sure that almost nothing is ever lost, even as disks fail every day.

The big ideas

  • Block, file and object. Block storage gives raw disk blocks to a server; file storage adds folders and is shared over a network; object storage gives up editing in place for nearly unlimited scale and low cost. Objects are written once and replaced whole, and are addressed by bucket and name.
  • Split metadata from data. Names, sizes and permissions are small and change often; the bytes are big and never change. So a metadata service (a sharded database) keeps the first, and a data store keeps the second. In the data store, a placement service tracks which data nodes are alive and where copies go, and a routing service sends reads and writes to them.
  • Pack small objects together. Millions of tiny files would waste disk blocks and overwhelm the file system. Data nodes append objects to one large file until it reaches a few gigabytes, then seal it as read-only, and keep a small local table of which object sits at which offset.
  • Durability: copies or codes. Three copies on different failure domains give about six nines of durability for 200 percent extra space. Erasure coding (for example 8 data chunks plus 4 parity chunks) survives any 4 failures, reaches about eleven nines, and costs only 50 percent extra, but reads touch more machines. Checksums catch silent corruption.
Walking the four steps

Step one: agree on bucket and object operations, versioning, large uploads, 100 PB of data, six nines of durability and four of availability. Step two: explain storage types and terms, then sketch the API service, identity and access, the metadata store and the data store, with upload and download flows. Step three: data routing and placement, replication, packing small objects, durability with failure domains and erasure coding, checksums, the metadata schema, listing objects, versioning, multipart uploads and garbage collection. Step four: recap and point out what was left out.

In plain words

To store files forever, keep the small, changing facts about them apart from the big, unchanging bytes. Then spread the bytes so that losing several machines at once still loses nothing.

Chapter 10 · Your rank, right now

Real-time Gaming Leaderboard

In short

Design a monthly leaderboard for a mobile game: show the top 10 players and your own rank, updated the moment you win. A sorted set in Redis does almost all the work.

The big ideas

  • Modest load, hard query. With 5 million daily players finishing about 10 matches each, scores change about 500 times a second, peaking around 2,500. The hard part is not the writes but answering "what is my rank?" among millions, instantly.
  • Why not SQL? A relational database can sort by score, but ranking millions of rows for every request means scanning and sorting again and again. It works for small games and falls over in real time at scale.
  • Redis sorted sets. A sorted set keeps members ordered by score using a skip list, a linked list with express lanes. Adding points (ZINCRBY), fetching the top 10 (ZREVRANGE) and finding a player's rank (ZREVRANK) all take logarithmic time. One leaderboard per month is one key, and 25 million players need well under a gigabyte.
  • When one Redis isn't enough. At 100 times the scale, shard by score range (each shard holds a band of scores, so finding the top is easy) or by hash (Redis Cluster spreads players evenly, but top 10 needs a scatter-gather across shards). Ties can be broken by who reached the score first. A NoSQL table like DynamoDB with sharded writes is another option.
Walking the four steps

Step one: confirm how points are scored, how many players, that the board resets monthly, and that ties and real-time updates matter. Step two: the game service calls a leaderboard service after each win; scores go to Redis and to a database for history; clients ask the leaderboard service for the top 10 and their own position. Step three: compare SQL with Redis sorted sets, size the memory, plan persistence and replicas, then scale out with sharding or switch to a NoSQL design. Step four: tie-breaking and recovering from a Redis failure.

In plain words

When you need "who's on top" and "where am I" in real time, keep the data already sorted. A sorted set answers both in a handful of steps, however many players there are.

Part four · money

Moving Money

The book ends with the systems where a bug costs real money: a payment service that talks to card networks, a digital wallet that moves balances a million times a second, and a stock exchange where microseconds decide who gets the trade. Correctness comes first, and speed is earned without giving it up.

The idea behind this part

With money, every action must happen exactly once and be explainable afterwards. Idempotency, double-entry ledgers and replayable event logs are how you get there.

Chapter 11 · Charge once, exactly once

Payment System

In short

Design the payment backend for an online shop like Amazon. The volume is low, but every charge must happen exactly once, every cent must be accounted for, and the system must keep working when banks and networks misbehave.

The big ideas

  • Correctness over speed. A million transactions a day is only about 10 a second. The challenge is never charging twice, never losing a payment, and being able to prove what happened, even when a call to a payment provider times out.
  • Pay-in and pay-out. When you buy, money flows in: a payment service records the event, a payment executor sends each order to a payment service provider (PSP) such as Stripe, which talks to card networks. A ledger records every movement in double-entry form, where each debit has a matching credit, and a wallet tracks what each seller is owed. Pay-out sends money to sellers later.
  • Hosted pages and webhooks. To avoid handling card numbers (and the PCI rules that come with them), the shop shows the PSP's hosted payment page. The PSP tells the shop the result through a webhook, and some payments take hours or days to finish, so the system must track pending states.
  • Retries plus idempotency. Retries with exponential backoff give at-least-once delivery; an idempotency key, often sent in a header and enforced with a unique key, gives at-most-once. Together they make exactly-once. Failures that keep failing go to a dead-letter queue, and nightly reconciliation compares the ledger with the PSP's settlement file.
Walking the four steps

Step one: pin down the scope (pay-in for an e-commerce site, using a third-party PSP, card payments), the volume, and the need for reliability, reconciliation and fault tolerance. Step two: sketch the pay-in flow through the payment service, executor, PSP, ledger and wallet, then the API and data model, and the pay-out flow. Step three: PSP integration and hosted pages, reconciliation, handling slow payments, sync versus async communication, failed payments, exactly-once delivery, consistency and security. Step four: monitoring, alerting, debugging tools, currency exchange and cash payments.

In plain words

Networks fail in the worst place: after the money moved but before you heard back. Retry so nothing is lost, and send a key with every request so a retry can never charge twice.

Chapter 12 · A million transfers a second

Digital Wallet

In short

Design the balance-transfer engine behind a wallet like PayPal's: move money between accounts a million times a second, never lose a cent, and be able to rebuild any balance at any moment in the past.

The big ideas

  • A million transactions a second. Each transfer is two operations, a debit and a credit, so the system really needs two million a second. If one database node handles about 1,000 a second, that's about 2,000 nodes, so the data must be sharded, and a transfer will often span two shards.
  • Atomic across shards. In-memory sharding with Redis is fast but can't make a debit on one shard and a credit on another happen together. Distributed transactions can: two-phase commit locks both sides and blocks if the coordinator fails; Try-Confirm/Cancel uses separate, reversible steps at the application level; a Saga runs steps in order and undoes them with compensating actions if one fails.
  • Event sourcing. Auditors want to know not just the balance but how it got there. Event sourcing stores every validated change as an immutable event; the balance is just the result of replaying the events. The rules that turn a command ("send $5") into an event must be deterministic, so replaying always gives the same state. Reads can come from separate, derived views (CQRS).
  • Fast and reliable. To go fast, keep the command and event lists in local files read through mmap, keep state in a local store such as RocksDB, and take snapshots so replays start from a checkpoint. To survive failures, replicate the event list with the Raft consensus algorithm, and coordinate transfers across Raft groups with a Saga or TC/C.
Walking the four steps

Step one: agree on balance transfers only, one million transactions a second, high reliability, transactional guarantees and reproducibility. Step two: estimate the node count, then compare in-memory sharding, distributed transactions (2PC, TC/C, Saga) and event sourcing. Step three: make event sourcing fast with local files, mmap and snapshots, make it reliable with Raft, and distribute it across groups with Saga or TC/C coordination. Step four: recap the progression from simple sharding to distributed event sourcing.

In plain words

Instead of overwriting a balance, write down every change that caused it. Then the balance is easy to recompute, any past moment can be replayed, and an auditor can see exactly why.

Chapter 13 · Where microseconds decide

Stock Exchange

In short

Design an electronic stock exchange that takes buy and sell orders, matches them fairly, and publishes prices, all with latency measured in microseconds. The surprising answer is to put the critical path on one machine.

The big ideas

  • Orders, not users. The book designs for 100 symbols and a billion orders a day. Spread across six and a half trading hours that's about 43,000 orders a second, with peaks around five times higher, and the latency that matters most is at the 99th percentile.
  • The critical path. An order passes through the client gateway (checks and rate limits), the order manager (risk checks and the trader's wallet), the sequencer (which stamps every order and every result with a sequence number), and the matching engine. Market data and reporting sit off the critical path.
  • The order book. For each symbol, the matching engine keeps buy orders and sell orders by price level, and orders at the same price in time order. A new order matches against the best opposite price first, oldest order first. Lists and maps make adding, cancelling and matching constant-time.
  • Fewer hops, one thread. Every network hop costs hundreds of microseconds, so the book puts the gateway-to-engine path on a single server. Components talk through shared memory with mmap, each runs a single-threaded loop pinned to a CPU core, and the whole thing is an event-sourced, deterministic log that a warm standby, or a Raft group, can replay to take over instantly.
Walking the four steps

Step one: agree on the scope (stocks only, limit orders, cancel, real-time market data, risk checks), the volume and the latency target. Step two: trace the trading flow, market data flow and reporting flow, then define the API, the order book and candlestick data. Step three: cut latency with one server, mmap and pinned threads; use event sourcing; make it fault tolerant with hot-warm engines and Raft; talk about determinism, market data multicast for fairness, colocation and DDoS protection. Step four: note that some exchanges now run in the cloud and use automated market makers.

In plain words

An exchange is a fair queue that has to be extremely fast. Number everything, match by price and then by time, and keep the critical path on one machine so nothing waits on the network.
