A chapter-by-chapter guide to Brené Brown's book on brave work, tough conversations and whole hearts

Dare to Lead,
Explained

Brené Brown asked senior leaders around the world one question: what has to change for people to lead well in a complex, fast-moving world? The answer kept coming back the same: we need braver leaders and more courageous cultures. Her research found that courage isn't a personality trait. It is four sets of skills you can learn, practise and see: rumbling with vulnerability, living into your values, braving trust, and learning to rise. This guide covers every section of the book, with a lab in each one where you can try the skill.

Introduction · chapter 1

Brave Leaders and Courage Cultures

Before any skills, Brown sets out what the book is for. Workplaces don't mostly fail for lack of strategy or talent. They fail because people avoid the uncomfortable parts of working together: the hard conversation, the honest feedback, the risky idea, the admission that they don't know.

The idea behind this part

Who we are is how we lead. Courage at work starts as something personal, and spreads as something shared.

Chapter 1 · The introduction

Brave Leaders and Courage Cultures

In short

A leader is anyone who takes responsibility for finding the potential in people and processes, and has the nerve to develop it. Title doesn't matter. What does matter is courage, and Brown's research shows courage can be taught, practised and measured.

The big ideas

  • Leadership isn't a job title. Brown's definition covers the intern who helps a new starter as much as the chief executive. If you take responsibility for bringing out what people and systems could be, you're leading.
  • The world needs braver leaders. When Brown's team interviewed around 150 senior leaders, the answers converged: in a world of fast change and tough problems, what's missing is courage, and cultures where courage is normal.
  • Ten habits hold teams back. The research named the same behaviours again and again: avoiding tough conversations, managing bad behaviour instead of the fears behind it, low trust, too few smart risks, getting stuck after setbacks, too much shame and blame, dodging hard talks about inclusion, rushing to fixes, values that are slogans, and perfectionism that stops people learning.
  • Courage is four skill sets. Rumbling with vulnerability, living into your values, braving trust and learning to rise. They are teachable, they can be observed, and they can be measured. The first one underpins all the others.
  • Daring leadership serves people. It is about taking care of others, not about protecting your own image. Leaders who need to be the smartest person in the room or to look in control end up working against their teams.
From the book

The man in the arena

Brown's work keeps returning to a passage from Roosevelt's 1910 speech "Citizenship in a Republic": it isn't the critic who counts, but the person actually in the arena who strives, errs and, if they fail, at least fails while daring greatly. She read it at a low point after a talk of hers went viral and drew a flood of cruel comments, and decided that she would only take feedback from people who were also in the arena, risking something.

In plain words

Most work problems are people dodging discomfort. Leading means walking into the awkward conversation instead of around it, and helping everyone else do the same.

Try it this week

Pick the one habit you ticked that costs your team the most. Name it out loud at your next meeting, and ask what it would take to change it.

"I think we avoid hard conversations here. Can we talk about that?"

Part one · chapters 2 to 6

Rumbling with Vulnerability

The biggest part of the book, and the foundation for the rest. Brown defines vulnerability as the emotion we feel in moments of uncertainty, risk and emotional exposure. Every brave act involves it, so you can't build courage without learning to stay in that feeling instead of running from it. A "rumble" is her word for a conversation where people commit to lean into that discomfort, stay curious and listen as hard as they talk.

The idea behind this part

There is no courage without vulnerability. If you can't name a brave act that didn't involve risk and uncertainty, you've found the rule.

Chapter 2 · Section one

The Moment and the Myths

In short

Vulnerability is not weakness. It's the feeling that comes with every brave act. Six common myths stop people from engaging with it, and you need to know whose voices count while you're in the arena.

The big ideas

  • No courage without vulnerability. Brown has asked thousands of people, including special forces soldiers, to describe an act of courage that didn't involve uncertainty, risk or exposure. Nobody has managed one.
  • Six myths keep us armoured. That vulnerability is weakness; that you can opt out of it; that you can go it alone; that systems can engineer the discomfort away; that trust has to come first; and that vulnerability means oversharing. Each is tested below.
  • Vulnerability is not disclosure. Pouring out personal details to people who haven't earned them is not courage. Vulnerability without boundaries is closer to desperation, or a way to grab attention.
  • Choose whose feedback counts. If someone isn't also in the arena, risking failure, their criticism doesn't get a vote. That doesn't mean ignoring all feedback: it means taking it from people who are brave alongside you.
  • Your square squad. Write the names of the people whose opinions really matter to you on a one-inch square of paper. They should be people who love you because of your flaws and vulnerabilities, not in spite of them. If the list doesn't fit, it's too long.
Six myths · tap one to see what's true
In plain words

Feeling exposed when you try something hard isn't a sign you're doing it wrong. It's what bravery feels like from the inside. And not everyone who has an opinion about your effort has earned the right to shape it.

Try it this week

Before you read the comments on something you made, decide in advance whose feedback you'll take seriously. Look at your square first.

"Are they in the arena with me, or in the cheap seats?"

Chapter 3 · Section two

The Call to Courage

In short

The thing that blocks courage isn't fear, it's the armour we put on to avoid feeling fear. Daring leaders believe people can be brave and give them the clarity to do it. The rule of thumb: clear is kind, unclear is unkind.

The big ideas

  • Fear isn't the problem; armour is. Almost every leader Brown spoke to admitted to feeling afraid. The ones who struggled were those who protected themselves from the feeling by shutting down, controlling or blaming.
  • Clear is kind. Unclear is unkind. Feeding people half-truths to make them feel better, or not telling a colleague what's expected and then being annoyed when they miss it, feels nice in the moment and is unkind in the end.
  • Paint done. Before handing work over, describe what finished looks like: the purpose, the result, the format, the deadline. Teams lose huge amounts of time and goodwill to work that was never clearly defined.
  • TASC. When you hand off a task, check who owns it (Task), whether they are Accountable for it, whether they have the Skills, and whether they have the Capacity right now. Missing any one of them sets someone up to fail.
  • Turn and learn. When estimating how long work will take, everyone writes a guess on a sticky note and turns it over at the same time. It stops junior people from simply echoing the most senior estimate.
  • Permission slips. At the start of a hard meeting, each person writes what they give themselves permission to feel or do: "to ask questions", "to be confused", "to be excited about something not yet finished". Saying it out loud makes it easier.
In plain words

Being vague to avoid an awkward moment just moves the awkwardness to later, when it's bigger. Say what you need, say what done looks like, and say it kindly.

Try it this week

The next time you hand off a piece of work, ask the other person to describe back to you what finished looks like before they start.

"Can you paint done for me, so I know we're picturing the same thing?"

Chapter 4 · Section three

The Armory

In short

We each pick up armour as children to protect ourselves from hurt, and keep wearing it into adulthood. At work it shows up in sixteen recognisable ways. For each kind of armoured leadership, Brown names the daring alternative.

The big ideas

  • Armour made sense once. It was how we got through childhood hurts, school, early jobs. The trouble is that it keeps out connection and learning as well as pain, and it is heavy to carry.
  • Perfectionism is armour, not high standards. Healthy striving asks "how can I improve?" Perfectionism asks "what will they think?" It is about avoiding blame and shame, and it makes people afraid to try.
  • Knowing versus learning. Armoured leaders need to be right. Daring leaders want to get it right, which means asking questions, admitting what they don't know and changing their minds.
  • Power over versus power with. Using power to control people through fear is armour. Daring leaders share power: power with others, power to make a difference, and power within, the confidence that doesn't need to dominate.
  • Leading from hurt or from heart. When leaders haven't dealt with their own pain, they tend to pass it on. The work of taking off armour starts with knowing what you are protecting.
In plain words

The things we do to look strong at work, like always having the answer, staying cynical, being busy, are often ways of not being seen. The braver move is usually the more human one.

Try it this week

Pick the one piece of armour you reach for most. Notice the moment you put it on, and ask what you were protecting.

"What am I afraid will happen if I take this off?"

Chapter 5 · Section four

Shame and Empathy

In short

Shame is the painful belief that we are flawed and so unworthy of belonging. It is everywhere at work, and it kills learning. Empathy is its antidote, and it's a skill: connecting to the feeling underneath someone's experience, not to the experience itself.

The big ideas

  • Shame is "I am bad". Guilt is "I did something bad". Guilt can be useful: it compares what we did with what we value, and pushes us to fix it. Shame attacks who we are, and tends to make people hide, lash out or give up.
  • Shame at work has a look. Perfectionism, favouritism, gossip, back-channelling, comparison, tying worth to productivity, bullying, blaming, teasing and cover-ups are all signs of it.
  • Three shame shields. When we feel shame we tend to move away (hide, go silent), move toward (people-please, appease), or move against (attack, use shame on others). Knowing your default helps you catch it.
  • Empathy is a set of skills. Drawing on the nursing researcher Theresa Wiseman, Brown lists them: see the world as others see it, stay out of judgement, understand their feelings, tell them you understand, and pay attention. You don't need to have had the same experience.
  • Empathy misses. Most of us mean well and still miss: we feel sorry for people from above, gasp in horror, one-up their story, look for a silver lining, rush to fix it, or show we're let down. Rarely can a response make something better; what helps is connection.
  • Self-compassion first. Using Kristin Neff's research, Brown argues we need to treat ourselves with the same kindness, and remember that everyone struggles. Leaders who are brutal with themselves are rarely gentle with others.
In plain words

When someone tells you something painful, they don't need you to make it better. They need to not feel alone with it. "Me too" and "that sounds hard" do more than any advice.

Try it this week

When someone tells you about a bad day, hold back the fix for one full minute. Just reflect back what you hear them feeling.

"That sounds really hard. I'm glad you told me."

Chapter 6 · Section five

Curiosity and Grounded Confidence

In short

Grounded confidence is not bravado. It is rumble skills plus curiosity plus practice: the messy, ongoing work of learning and unlearning. Curiosity is itself a brave act, because asking a question admits you don't already know.

The big ideas

  • Grounded confidence has a formula. Rumble skills, plus curiosity, plus practice. It looks less like certainty and more like a person willing to stay with a hard question until they understand it.
  • Curiosity is uncomfortable. It means admitting a gap and being open to what you find. Many people stop being curious as adults because school and work reward having answers, not asking questions.
  • You need a little knowledge to wonder. Brown draws on George Loewenstein's information-gap idea: curiosity switches on when we notice a gap between what we know and what we want to know. Too little knowledge and there's nothing to ask; too much certainty and we stop looking.
  • Rumble starters. Simple phrases open a curious conversation: "The story I'm making up is…", "Tell me more", "Help me understand", "I'm curious about", "Walk me through", "What does done look like?"
  • Fix the problem you actually have. Teams under pressure jump to solutions. Daring leaders spend longer defining the problem, because the wrong problem solved well is still a waste.
In plain words

Confidence that comes from never being questioned is brittle. Confidence that comes from having asked hard questions, been wrong and learned is the kind that holds.

Try it this week

In your next disagreement, ask two genuine questions before you make your point.

"Help me understand how you're seeing this."

Part two · chapter 7

Living into Our Values

Values are what we believe matters most, the guide we lean on when things get hard. Most organisations have values on the wall; few teach the behaviours that go with them. Brown's point is simple and demanding: if you can't name your values and say what they look like in practice, you can't live into them.

The idea behind this part

We have to do more than profess our values. We have to practise them, especially when it's hard.

Chapter 7 · Part two

Living into Our Values

In short

Pick one or two core values, not ten. Turn each into three or four behaviours you can actually see, plus the "slippery" behaviours that show you've drifted. Then use them to steer hard moments like giving feedback.

The big ideas

  • If everything is important, nothing is. Brown asks people to choose just one or two core values. Most other values on a long list turn out to flow from those two.
  • From slogans to behaviours. For each value, write what it looks like when you're living it, what it looks like when you're not, and an example of a time you did. "Courage" might mean "I say the hard thing in the meeting, not in the car park afterwards".
  • Values hold us in the arena. When criticism or fear hits, values are the thing you check yourself against. The question becomes "did I act on what I believe?", not "did everyone approve?"
  • Know your values to know others'. Brown recommends sharing your two values with colleagues and asking about theirs. It explains a lot of friction, and helps you support each other.
  • Feedback is a values test. Brown's engaged feedback checklist starts with: I'm ready when I'm ready to sit next to you rather than across from you, and to put the problem in front of us rather than between us.
In plain words

A value you can't describe in behaviour is a wish. Choose two things you really stand for, and decide what they look like on a Tuesday afternoon.

Try it this week

Tell one colleague your two values and ask for theirs. Then ask how you can support each other in living them.

"What does support look like for you around that value?"

Part three · chapter 8

BRAVING Trust

Everyone agrees trust matters, and almost nobody knows how to talk about it. Telling a colleague "I don't trust you" is so loaded that we just avoid them instead. Brown's research breaks trust into seven elements, so you can talk about the specific part that's missing.

The idea behind this part

Trust is built in small moments, not grand gestures, and it can be named, discussed and repaired.

Chapter 8 · Part three

BRAVING Trust

In short

Trust is choosing to risk making something you value vulnerable to another person's actions. It's built like marbles going into a jar: one small moment at a time. BRAVING names its seven parts: Boundaries, Reliability, Accountability, Vault, Integrity, Nonjudgement and Generosity.

The big ideas

  • A working definition. Brown borrows Charles Feltman's: trust is choosing to make something you value vulnerable to someone else's actions. Distrust is deciding that what matters to you isn't safe with that person in this situation.
  • The marble jar. When Brown's daughter was hurt that friends had shared her secret, Brown described trust as a jar of marbles: people earn a marble each time they show up for you in a small way. Trust builds slowly and can be lost quickly.
  • The seven elements. Boundaries: you respect mine and ask when unsure. Reliability: you do what you say, again and again. Accountability: you own mistakes and make amends. Vault: you keep what isn't yours to share. Integrity: you choose courage over comfort. Nonjudgement: we can both ask for help. Generosity: you assume the best about my words and actions.
  • The vault cuts both ways. When someone shares gossip about others with you, it can feel like closeness. It's actually a sign they'll share yours. Bonding over a common enemy is counterfeit trust.
  • Asking for help builds trust. Leaders tend to trust people who ask for help more than people who never do. If you never ask, they can't count on you to raise your hand when it really matters.
  • Self-trust uses the same seven. After a failure, people often lose trust in themselves. Running through BRAVING on yourself shows which part to rebuild.
In plain words

People don't trust you because of one big gesture. They trust you because you remembered, kept quiet, owned up, turned up, and gave them the benefit of the doubt, over and over.

Try it this week

Think of a working relationship that feels shaky. Instead of "I don't trust you", find the specific element that's missing and talk about that.

"I'd like to talk about reliability between us. Here's what I've noticed."

Part four · chapter 9

Learning to Rise

If you're brave often enough, you will fall. The last skill set is about getting back up: noticing you've been knocked down, getting honest about the story you're telling yourself, and writing a better ending.

The idea behind this part

A team that knows how to get up again can afford to take risks.

Chapter 9 · Part four

Learning to Rise

In short

Rising takes three steps. The reckoning: notice you're hooked by an emotion and get curious about it. The rumble: own the story you're making up, and check it against the facts. The revolution: write a new ending and live it.

The big ideas

  • The reckoning starts with your body. Hurt shows up as a racing heart, a hot face, a tight chest. Brown teaches tactical breathing, a technique used by soldiers and police: breathe in for four, hold for four, out for four, hold for four.
  • We offload hurt in predictable ways. Chandeliering (keeping it in until it explodes at the wrong person), bouncing it ("I don't care anyway"), numbing it, stockpiling it, and hiding behind "I'm fine". All of them postpone the work.
  • Our brains love a story, true or not. When something hurts, the brain rushes to explain it, and rewards us for any story with an ending. That first explanation is usually self-protective and often wrong.
  • Write the shitty first draft. Brown's phrase, borrowed from the writer Anne Lamott. Get your first, unfiltered story on paper: "The story I'm making up is…". Then look for the conspiracies (stories built on little data) and confabulations (things we sincerely believe but made up).
  • Rumble with three questions. What more do I need to learn about the situation? About the other people? About myself? The answers turn a story that protects you into one that's true.
  • The revolution. Owning the story means you get to write the ending. That's the change that sticks: from a person who was hurt to a person who learned something.
In plain words

When something stings, your mind writes a story fast, and usually casts you as the victim or the fool. Write it down, then go find the parts you made up.

Try it this week

When a colleague's reaction stings, start the next conversation with your first draft, said lightly.

"The story I'm making up is that you were annoyed with me. Is that right?"
