A chapter-by-chapter guide to Eric Jorgenson's book of Naval Ravikant's ideas

The Almanack of Naval Ravikant,
Explained

Naval Ravikant, the investor behind AngelList, never wrote a book. For over a decade he thought out loud in tweets, essays and podcasts, and in 2020 Eric Jorgenson gathered the best of it into The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, a short guide to two things most people want and few are taught: how to get rich without getting lucky, and how to be happy whatever happens. The first half says wealth comes from specific knowledge, accountability and leverage, applied with good judgment and patience. The second half says happiness is a skill you learn, through presence, fewer desires, good habits and acceptance. This guide covers Naval's story and all 36 chapters, each with an illustration and a lab where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

Build wealth by owning what you know and multiplying it with leverage; build happiness by wanting less and noticing more.

Part I · wealth

Building Wealth

The first half of the book is about money, and it opens with the idea that made Naval famous outside Silicon Valley: you can get rich without getting lucky. Wealth, he says, isn't a reward for hours worked. It comes from owning things that keep earning, and you get there by finding knowledge only you have, taking responsibility for the results, and multiplying your effort with leverage. These twelve chapters start with Naval's own story and then walk through that recipe, ending with luck and patience.

The idea behind this part

Wealth comes from owning things that earn while you sleep, built from specific knowledge, accountability and leverage, and given time.

Introduction · Eric's note and a timeline of Naval's life

Now, Here Is Naval in His Own Words

In short

Before the ideas, the book introduces the man. Naval Ravikant was born in India, grew up with little money in New York, and became one of Silicon Valley's best-known founders and angel investors. Eric Jorgenson, who had followed his tweets and podcasts for years, gathered his scattered thinking into one place so you could read it in an afternoon.

The big ideas

  • An almanack, not a memoir. Naval didn't write this book. Jorgenson collected his words from tweets, essays and interviews and arranged them by theme, then Naval reviewed it. It reads like a notebook of principles.
  • Started with nothing in particular. Naval moved to New York as a boy with his mother and brother and spent long hours in the public library. He credits reading with nearly everything that followed.
  • A builder and an investor. He co-founded Epinions in 1999 and AngelList in 2010, and was an early investor in companies such as Twitter and Uber.
  • The tweetstorm that started it. In 2018 he posted a long thread called "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)". It spread widely, and much of the wealth half of the book expands on it.
From the book

The book's opening pages lay out a short timeline. Naval was born in New Delhi in 1974 and moved to New York around the age of nine. His mother raised him and his brother largely on her own while working and studying, and he describes himself as a latchkey kid who found a second home in the library. He got into Stuyvesant, one of the city's selective public high schools, then studied computer science and economics at Dartmouth. In Silicon Valley he co-founded Epinions, later started the blog Venture Hacks with Babak Nivi, and turned it into AngelList, a platform for startups to raise money. By the time of his 2018 tweetstorm he had invested in hundreds of companies. Jorgenson's note says he made the book because Naval's ideas had helped him so much and were scattered across a decade of the internet.

Worth knowing

The book is unusual: it's sold in shops but also offered free as an ebook and PDF on its own website. Everything in it is Naval's words selected and edited by Jorgenson, so it captures his thinking up to about 2020.

Try it this week

Write your own one-paragraph timeline: where you started, two or three turning points, and what you had to work with at each. Notice which turning points came from things you read or learned.

In plain words

Naval is a self-made founder and investor who learned most of what he knows from reading. This book is his scattered advice on getting rich and being happy, collected into one short volume by a fan.

Chapter 2 · Wealth, money and status

Understand How Wealth Is Created

In short

Naval separates three things people blur together. Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep: a business, code, a book, property. Money is how we move wealth around. Status is your rank in a social pecking order. You get rich by creating and owning wealth, and you won't get there by renting out your time.

The big ideas

  • Wealth earns while you sleep. A factory, an app, a song or a rental flat keeps producing value when you're not working. A salary stops when you stop.
  • Wealth creation isn't zero-sum. Building something people want makes the pie bigger. Status games are zero-sum: someone has to lose for you to win, which is why they make people so unpleasant.
  • Renting your time has a ceiling. Your hourly rate times your hours is capped by the hours in a week. To get past it you need ownership: a share of what you produce.
  • Give society what it wants but doesn't know how to get. Your job is to find something people want that isn't yet easy to get, and deliver it at scale.
From the book

The chapter grows out of Naval's 2018 tweetstorm. He opens by saying he wants to be clear about the goal: wealth, not money and not status. He points out that people who play status games often attack wealth creation, because they can win status by tearing others down, while wealth only grows by making new things. He describes the basic trade of a working life, where you sell your time for money, and argues that nobody ever got truly rich that way. Owners of businesses, products and intellectual property can earn while they sleep. The aim, as he puts it, is to reach a point where you have enough to stop worrying and can spend your time how you like.

Worth knowing

Naval's examples lean toward technology founders, where owning a successful product can make you very rich. Most startups fail, and owning a small piece of something that fails is worth nothing. The principle (own, don't only rent) holds; the scale of the outcome is never guaranteed.

Try it this week

List everything you own that earns money without your hours: savings, a side product, a share of a business, writing that people still read. If the list is empty, pick one small thing you could start owning this month.

In plain words

Being rich means owning things that make money without you. A wage, however high, stops when you do, so put some of your time into building or owning something.

Chapter 3 · Knowledge that can't be trained

Find and Build Specific Knowledge

In short

Specific knowledge is the kind that can't be taught in a classroom, because it grew out of your own curiosity, talents and experience. If society can train you to do something, it can train someone else and replace you. What feels like play to you but looks like work to others is a clue to where your specific knowledge lies.

The big ideas

  • It's found, not taught. You build specific knowledge by following genuine curiosity, often since childhood. It looks obvious to you and mysterious to others.
  • It's often on the edge of technology. New fields haven't been turned into courses yet, so the people who learned by doing hold knowledge nobody else can easily copy.
  • Learn to sell and learn to build. Naval's shortcut: if you can both make things and persuade people to want them, you're very hard to replace.
  • Combine your oddities. The mix matters. Being good at two or three unusual things together makes you one of a kind.
From the book

Naval describes his own specific knowledge as a mix that grew from childhood: reading everything, an obsession with science and technology, and a knack for explaining and persuading. None of it came from a job title. He suggests looking back at what you did as a teenager when no one was watching, the things that came naturally and made time vanish. He also warns that specific knowledge can't be found by asking what's hot. If you follow the crowd into a popular field, you'll be competing with everyone who did the same. If you follow your own odd curiosity far enough, you end up somewhere with very little competition.

Try it this week

Ask three people who know you well: "What do I do that seems easy for me but hard for others?" Write down the answers that overlap.

In plain words

The knowledge that makes you valuable is the stuff nobody can teach you in a course, because it came from your own curiosity. Follow what you love learning, and combine a few unusual skills.

Next: Long-term games →
Chapter 4 · Compound interest in relationships

Play Long-Term Games with Long-Term People

In short

All the returns in life, Naval says, come from compound interest: in wealth, knowledge and relationships. Compounding needs time, so pick an industry, a set of skills and a set of people you can stay with for decades. Trust built over many rounds lets you do bigger things faster, with less friction.

The big ideas

  • Repeated games reward honesty. In a one-off deal, cheating can pay. With the same people for twenty years, it never does, so long-term partners act differently.
  • Trust compounds. Each round with someone reliable makes the next one easier and bigger. After years, a handshake can replace a contract.
  • Choose people with integrity. Your partners' character matters more than their talent, because you'll be tied to their choices for a long time.
  • Switching resets the clock. Every time you change field, job or partners you start compounding again from zero.
From the book

Naval points out how many big Silicon Valley deals happen on little more than trust. Investors and founders who have worked together for years can agree on terms quickly, because each knows the other will behave well. He contrasts that with people who move from scheme to scheme, cutting corners each time, and never build up the trust that lets things move fast. His advice is to look for people who are playing the same long game, check their integrity in small things, and then stay with them. Over time, the people who do this find that deals come to them.

Try it this week

Make a list of the five people you'd most like to be working with in twenty years. Do one thing this week that makes you more trustworthy to one of them.

In plain words

Pick work and people you can stick with for a long time. Trust grows with every round and makes everything easier, while hopping around keeps resetting you to the start.

Next: Accountability →
Chapter 5 · Put your name on it

Take on Accountability

In short

Accountability means taking the risk of putting your name to something, so you get the credit when it works and the blame when it doesn't. Society rewards people who take that risk with leverage and pay. Hiding inside a group feels safer, but it also hides you from the upside.

The big ideas

  • Your name is your leverage. People trust and back someone who stands behind the result. A known name attracts customers, capital and talent.
  • Failure costs less than it feels. In the modern world, a public failure is usually survivable. You can try again, and people mostly forget.
  • Accountability gets you equity. People who take responsibility for outcomes get paid for outcomes, which is where ownership comes from.
  • Build a brand around yourself. Over time, your name becomes associated with quality and honesty, which compounds like any other asset.
From the book

Naval compares the modern world with older times, when failing publicly could mean losing everything, and argues that today the downside of putting your name on something is far smaller than people fear. He gives the example of entrepreneurs and creators who are the face of their company: they carry the risk of looking foolish, but they also become the person investors, customers and talented people want to back. He's careful to say this cuts both ways. If you take credit, you must take blame too, honestly and quickly. People who stay anonymous inside organisations rarely get rich, because nobody can tell what they contributed.

Try it this week

Pick one piece of work you're doing now and attach your name to it publicly: present it yourself, write it up under your name, or tell your manager it's yours to own.

In plain words

Take responsibility for results and put your name on your work. It's riskier, but it's how people come to trust and back you, and failure costs less than you think.

Next: Equity →
Chapter 6 · Own a piece

Build or Buy Equity in a Business

In short

To get rich you need ownership. Without a share of the business, your pay is tied to your inputs, like hours. With equity, it's tied to outputs: what the whole thing becomes. So Naval's advice is to own a piece of what you work on, by starting it, joining early, or buying in.

The big ideas

  • Inputs versus outputs. A salary pays for your time. Equity pays for the result, which can be worth far more than any time you put in.
  • Ownership is how founders and early staff get rich. Almost every big fortune Naval mentions came from owning part of a business.
  • You can buy equity too. Investing in businesses, through shares or funds, lets your money own a piece of what others build.
  • Equity is risky. Most early-stage shares end up worth little. Ownership is a bet, which is why it pays more when it works.
Naval's example

Naval learned how much equity matters the hard way. He co-founded Epinions, a product-review site, in 1999. In 2003 it merged with DealTime to become Shopping.com, which went public and was bought by eBay in 2005 for several hundred million dollars. Naval and some other early people later said the restructuring had left their founder shares worth very little while investors did well, and in 2005 he sued. The case was settled privately. He has said the experience taught him to understand exactly what he owns, and it shaped his later work on AngelList, which was built to make startup investing more open and fair to founders.

Worth knowing

The Epinions story is told from Naval's side; the investors disputed his claims and the settlement terms were never made public. The lesson he draws, read the terms of what you own, holds either way.

Try it this week

Look at what you work on and ask: do I own any part of the outcome? If not, find one small way to own a piece, from employee shares to an index fund to a side project that's fully yours.

In plain words

A wage pays for your time; owning part of a business pays for the result. To build real wealth, own a piece of something, knowing that ownership is a bet.

Next: Leverage →
Chapter 7 · Labour, capital, code and media

Find a Position of Leverage

In short

Leverage multiplies what your judgment and effort can do. Naval names three kinds. Labour (people working for you) and capital (money) are the old forms, and both need someone's permission. Code and media are new: they cost almost nothing to copy, need nobody's permission, and can reach millions while you sleep.

The big ideas

  • Labour is the oldest and messiest. Managing people multiplies your output but brings coordination, politics and the need for someone to follow you.
  • Capital scales well but needs trust. Managing money multiplies your judgment cleanly, but someone has to give you the money first.
  • Code and media are permissionless. Anyone can write software, a book, a video or a podcast, and every copy is free. This is the leverage of the new rich.
  • Pick a job with uncapped leverage. Some work scales with no ceiling (writing, software, investing). Some is tied to hours (most services). Choose the first where you can.
From the book

Naval borrows Archimedes' line about moving the world with a long enough lever and a place to stand. In his telling, the history of wealth is the history of leverage. Kings and generals had armies of people. Industrialists and financiers had capital. Today a teenager with a laptop can write an app used by millions or record a podcast heard around the world, and nobody has to approve it. He points to programmers, writers and creators as the clearest examples, and says the most valuable thing you can do is learn to use the leverage that's open to everyone: code and media.

Try it this week

Make one thing this week that can work without you: a short guide, a script that automates a task, a recorded explanation. Share it where people can find it.

In plain words

Leverage is what multiplies your effort. People and money need permission, but code and media don't, and every copy is free. Learn to use them.

Chapter 8 · Leverage multiplies your calls

Get Paid for Your Judgment

In short

Once you have leverage, what you're really paid for is your judgment: how good your decisions are. With a big lever, being right 75 percent of the time instead of 70 can be worth an enormous amount. That's why people with proven judgment are paid so much, and why building a track record matters.

The big ideas

  • Effort matters less with leverage. Two people working equally hard can see wildly different results when one is pointing a big lever in a slightly better direction.
  • A small edge becomes a fortune. Multiply a tiny advantage in judgment by a large enough lever and it outweighs everything else.
  • Judgment shows over time. A record of good calls, especially public ones, is how people come to trust your judgment and hand you more leverage.
  • Bad judgment is multiplied too. Leverage cuts both ways. That's why the next section of the book is all about building judgment.
From the book

Naval uses Warren Buffett as the clearest case. Buffett doesn't work more hours than anyone else; much of his day is reading and thinking. But his decisions move enormous amounts of capital, so each good call is worth a great deal, and people trust him because of a decades-long public record. Naval contrasts this with the idea of being paid for hours: nobody pays Buffett by the hour. The same logic, he says, explains why chief executives of large companies are paid so much. Their decisions are levered across thousands of people and billions of dollars, so a slightly better leader is worth far more.

Try it this week

Write down three predictions about your work or field for the next three months, with how sure you are. Check them at the end. That's the start of a judgment record.

In plain words

With leverage, you're paid for how good your decisions are, not how hard you work. Small improvements in judgment get multiplied into big results, for better or worse.

Next: Prioritise and focus →
Chapter 9 · An aspirational hourly rate

Prioritize and Focus

In short

Your time is your scarcest asset, so value it as if you were already the person you want to become. Naval suggests setting an aspirational hourly rate, deliberately high, and refusing to spend time on anything that saves less than that. Say no often, protect long stretches of focus, and work hard on the few things that matter.

The big ideas

  • Set a rate and enforce it. If a chore saves you less per hour than your aspirational rate, outsource it, drop it or accept the cost. Don't argue over small sums.
  • Busy is not the same as productive. Meetings, errands and small favours fill the week while the important work waits.
  • Work like a lion, not a cow. Naval prefers intense sprints on what matters followed by real rest, rather than steady grazing through busywork.
  • Say no to almost everything. The only way to protect focus is to turn down most requests, politely and early.
Naval's example

Naval says that when he was young and not yet wealthy, he picked an hourly rate that seemed absurdly high for him at the time, around $5,000 an hour, and used it to make decisions. If a task such as returning a faulty item, arguing over a bill or running a small errand would save less than that, he'd pay someone else or simply let it go. He admits it made him look extravagant, even a little ridiculous, to friends. But it kept his best hours for the work that could actually change his life: building, learning and thinking.

Try it this week

Pick an aspirational hourly rate, write it on a note, and find one recurring chore this week that saves you less than it. Drop it, outsource it, or batch it.

In plain words

Value your time as if you were already where you want to be. Stop spending hours on small savings and spend them on the few things that will change your life.

Next: Work that feels like play →
Chapter 10 · Nobody can compete with you on being you

Find Work That Feels Like Play

In short

If your work feels like play to you, you'll do it longer and better than people who are forcing themselves, and you'll be doing something nobody can copy exactly. Naval's line is that no one can compete with you on being you. Authenticity is the best escape from competition.

The big ideas

  • Play beats grind over the long run. People who enjoy the work put in years of extra hours without noticing, and that compounds into mastery.
  • Escape competition through authenticity. If you're doing what everyone else does, you're competing with all of them. If you're being yourself, there's only one of you.
  • Productise yourself. Naval's phrase for finding what you uniquely do, then giving it accountability and leverage so it scales.
  • Follow your curiosity, not the trend. The crowd's direction is crowded. Your own interests lead somewhere less contested.
From the book

Naval suggests a test: when you're doing the work, does time disappear? Does it feel like something you'd do on a weekend anyway? He observes that the most successful people he's met in technology were often obsessed long before it paid. They tinkered, wrote, coded or traded because they couldn't stop. Meanwhile, people who chose a field because it was hot spent their energy forcing themselves, and were up against everyone else who'd made the same choice. His conclusion is to keep searching until you find work that feels like play, and then combine it with leverage.

Try it this week

List three activities where you lose track of time. For each, write one way it could be useful to other people. Spend an hour this week on the most promising one.

In plain words

Do the work that feels like play to you. You'll keep at it longer than people who force themselves, and nobody can out-compete you at being you.

Chapter 11 · Four kinds of luck

How to Get Lucky

In short

Naval describes four kinds of luck. The first is blind luck, which just happens. The second comes from hustling: the more you move, the more you bump into. The third comes from preparation: you notice chances others miss. The fourth, and best, comes from building a unique character and reputation, so luck seeks you out.

The big ideas

  • Blind luck you can't control. Being born in the right place or winning a draw is real, but there's nothing to do about it.
  • Luck from motion. Energy, curiosity and meeting people stir things up so that more opportunities come your way.
  • Luck from awareness. Skill and knowledge let you spot the valuable thing in what others pass over.
  • Luck that finds you. When you're known as the best at something specific, the right opportunities come looking for you. This is the kind to aim for.
From the book

The four kinds of luck come from a 1978 book on medical discovery, Chase, Chance, and Creativity, by the neurologist James Austin, and were popularised by the investor Marc Andreessen in a 2007 blog post; Naval builds on them. His favourite is the fourth. His example is someone who's known as the world's best deep-sea diver: when a treasure hunter finds a wreck, that person gets the call and a share of the treasure. You can't plan the shipwreck, but you can become the person who's called. That's why specific knowledge, accountability and reputation keep coming back in the book: they turn luck into something closer to destiny.

Try it this week

Write one sentence finishing "I want to be the person people call when they need...". Then do one public thing this week, a post, a demo, a talk, that makes you more known for it.

In plain words

You can't control blind luck, but you can make more of it by moving, by being prepared to spot it, and above all by becoming so good at something specific that luck comes looking for you.

Next: Be patient →
Chapter 12 · Impatient with actions, patient with results

Be Patient

In short

Even if you do everything right, getting rich takes a long time, often a decade or more. Naval's rule is to be impatient with actions, doing things quickly and decisively, and patient with results. Compounding looks like nothing for years, and the people who quit early never see its steep part.

The big ideas

  • Compounding is back-loaded. Most of the gain from compounding arrives at the end. Early years look flat.
  • Act fast, wait long. Move quickly on what's in front of you, but don't judge the outcome until the long game has had time to play out.
  • Most people quit in the flat part. If you keep going through the dull years, you meet much less competition later.
  • No shortcuts to the end. Schemes to get rich quickly usually sacrifice the trust and reputation that compound.
From the book

Naval notes that even for him, with a good education, a network and good timing, it took many years of hard work for things to pay off. He describes looking at very successful people and seeing only the end of the curve, not the long flat stretch before it. The book pairs this with a theme from the long-term games chapter: if you're playing with good people, in a field you love, the waiting stops feeling like waiting. You're doing the work anyway. The trap, he says, is impatience with results, which makes people quit or cut corners just before the curve turns up.

Try it this week

Pick one long-term effort you've been judging too early. Write down the date ten years from now and what it could look like if you kept going. Put the next action in your calendar today.

In plain words

Big results take a long time, and most of the payoff comes late. Move fast on what you can do today, and be patient with how long it takes to pay off.

Part I · wealth, continued

Building Judgment

Once you have leverage, the quality of your decisions matters more than the amount of your effort. A slightly better call, multiplied a million times, is worth a fortune. So the second section of the wealth half is about judgment: thinking clearly, keeping your identity out of your conclusions, making decisions, collecting mental models and, above all, reading.

The idea behind this part

In an age of leverage, being a little more right is worth far more than working a lot harder.

Chapter 13 · Seeing the long-term consequences

Judgment

In short

Naval defines wisdom as knowing the long-term consequences of your actions, and judgment as wisdom applied to real decisions. In a world of leverage, judgment is the most valuable skill you can have. It's built from experience, but experience can be gathered faster by thinking hard about cause and effect.

The big ideas

  • Wisdom sees further ahead. A wise person asks what a choice leads to in a year, ten years, a lifetime, not just tomorrow.
  • Judgment beats hard work. With leverage, it's more important to point in the right direction than to push hard.
  • Experience can be compressed. Reflecting carefully on each decision and its results teaches more than many years of not noticing.
  • Integrity is part of judgment. Choices that damage trust usually look good in the short term and terrible in the long term.
From the book

Naval gives the example of everyday temptations that look different depending on how far ahead you look. Lying to get out of an awkward meeting saves you an hour today but costs you trust for years. Skipping exercise is pleasant this evening and expensive at sixty. Reading a hard book is dull this afternoon and pays for decades. The person with good judgment isn't smarter in the moment; they're simply weighing the long-term consequences more heavily. He adds that people who are clearly ahead in judgment tend to be calm and unhurried, because they've already thought the consequences through.

Try it this week

Before one decision this week, write down what it leads to in a day, a year and ten years. If the answers point in different directions, go with the ten-year one.

In plain words

Judgment is seeing where your choices lead in the long run. The further ahead you look, the more the right choice changes, and people with good judgment look far ahead.

Next: Think clearly →
Chapter 14 · Simple words and a quiet mind

How to Think Clearly

In short

Clear thinkers understand the basics so well they can explain them simply. Naval says people who use complicated words often haven't really understood. To think clearly, learn the foundations deeply, be honest about what you don't know, and give your mind quiet time so it can settle.

The big ideas

  • Master the basics. Deep understanding of a few fundamentals beats memorising many advanced ideas you can't explain.
  • Simple words signal understanding. If you can explain it to a child, you understand it. Jargon often hides confusion.
  • Make room to think. A mind full of inputs can't reason. Time without notifications lets ideas settle and connect.
  • Separate how things are from how you wish they were. Desire clouds perception. Clear thinking starts by admitting reality.
From the book

Naval describes the smartest people he knows as surprisingly plain speakers. They don't reach for big words; they explain things from first principles, and when they don't know something, they say so. He also says he deliberately keeps empty time in his day, with no meetings and little input, just to think. In his description, a busy mind is like a pond that's constantly stirred: you can't see the bottom. Leave it alone and the mud settles, and things become obvious that weren't before. Much of what he calls clear thinking is simply making time for that to happen.

Try it this week

Block thirty minutes this week with no input at all: no phone, no reading, no music. Bring one problem and a blank page and see what settles.

In plain words

To think clearly, learn the basics deeply, explain things simply, and give your mind quiet time so the noise can settle.

Next: Shed your identity →
Chapter 15 · Labels cost you objectivity

Shed Your Identity to See Reality

In short

The moment you attach your identity to a belief, a team or a tribe, you lose the ability to see clearly about it. You start defending positions instead of testing them. Naval tries to hold as few identities as possible, so that he can change his mind when the evidence changes.

The big ideas

  • Identity locks in conclusions. Once you are "a crypto person" or "an optimist", any evidence against that label feels like an attack on you.
  • Package deals are a warning sign. If your views on unrelated topics all line up with one group, you probably adopted them as a set rather than thinking each through.
  • Admit what you don't know. Being able to say "I don't know" or "I was wrong" is a sign of clear thinking, not weakness.
  • Ego blinds. The more you need to be right, the less you can see what's true.
From the book

Naval describes noticing that whenever he held a strong identity, about a company, a political view or a technology, he became predictable and less honest with himself. He'd find reasons to dismiss good arguments from the other side. The essayist Paul Graham made a similar point in a well-known 2009 essay, Keep Your Identity Small: the topics people argue about most badly are the ones tied to identity. Naval's practice is to hold opinions loosely and hold labels even more loosely, so that each question can be judged on its own.

Try it this week

Write down one label you strongly identify with. Then write the best argument you can for a view that label usually rejects.

In plain words

Every label you attach to yourself makes some ideas hard to consider. Hold fewer identities and you'll see more clearly.

Next: Decision-making →
Chapter 16 · Simple rules for hard choices

Learn the Skills of Decision-Making

In short

Naval offers a handful of rules for deciding well. If you can't decide, the answer is no. When two options seem equal, take the one that's more painful in the short term, because the comfortable path is usually the one that loses in the long run. And be ruthlessly honest with yourself about what you see.

The big ideas

  • If you can't decide, it's no. Modern life offers endless options. Anything that isn't a clear yes will probably cost more than it gives.
  • Choose the short-term pain. When choices look roughly equal, your mind is overvaluing comfort. The harder path usually pays more later.
  • Honesty first. Most bad decisions come from fooling yourself. Seeing the situation truthfully is half the work.
  • Decide quickly, carefully. Spend time on the decisions that are hard to reverse and move fast on the rest.
From the book

Naval explains why the painful path tends to win. Your brain is built to avoid discomfort, so when two choices feel balanced, the one with short-term pain is probably better than it feels and the comfortable one worse. Leaving a safe job to build something, having the hard conversation now rather than later, or learning a difficult skill at an awkward age all feel worse this month and better for years. He combines this with his other rule: in a world of plenty, saying no to the unclear things keeps your life simple enough to say a full yes to the right ones.

Try it this week

Find one decision you've been putting off because both options feel similar. Ask which is more uncomfortable this month, and take it.

In plain words

If you can't decide, say no. If two options feel equal, choose the one that's harder now, because it usually wins later. And be honest with yourself.

Chapter 17 · Tools for thinking

Collect Mental Models

In short

Mental models are compact ideas from many fields that help you understand situations quickly. Naval's favourites include evolution, inversion, compound interest, the principal-agent problem, basic maths and probability, and black swans. The more models you hold, the less likely you are to see every problem through one lens.

The big ideas

  • Evolution explains a lot. Things that exist today survived a long filtering process, which often explains why they work the way they do.
  • Invert. Instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure, then avoid that.
  • Principal and agent. People acting on someone else's behalf, like hired managers, often pursue their own interests. Check who owns the outcome.
  • Compound interest, maths and probability. Small steady gains add up enormously, and a basic feel for numbers and odds protects you from many mistakes.
From the book

Naval admires Charlie Munger's idea of a latticework of models drawn from many disciplines. He says he doesn't try to remember lots of facts; he tries to understand a few deep principles that keep explaining things. He's especially fond of the principal-agent problem, which he uses to explain why owners care more than employees, why politicians and bureaucrats often disappoint, and why being paid for your output beats being paid for your time. And he leans on Nassim Taleb's black swans, rare and extreme events that matter more than everything predictable, to argue for staying humble about forecasts.

Try it this week

Take one problem you're stuck on and run it through two models: invert it (what would make it certainly worse?) and check principal and agent (who's acting for whom, and what do they really want?).

In plain words

Collect a few powerful thinking tools from different fields, like evolution, inversion and compound interest, and use them to see problems from more than one angle.

Chapter 18 · The skill behind the other skills

Learn to Love to Read

In short

Naval credits reading for almost everything he has. His advice is simple: read what you love until you love to read. Don't worry about finishing books or reading the right ones at first. Once the habit is there, lean toward foundations, the original sources in science, maths and philosophy, rather than the latest takes.

The big ideas

  • Read what you love. Start with whatever you can't put down. The aim is to make reading a habit, not a duty.
  • An hour or two a day. Naval says he reads for a couple of hours most days. Over years that's thousands of books' worth of ideas.
  • Foundations over fads. The basics of maths, science, economics and philosophy last; most new books are commentary on them.
  • Skip and reread. It's fine to drop books, skim, and read a few great ones many times.
Naval's example

Naval describes his childhood in New York as largely spent in the library, reading anything he could find, from science fiction to science to philosophy. He says the gap between people isn't intelligence so much as whether they read. As an adult he reads widely, often from several books at once, and stops when a book stops being useful. He points out that a single great book can contain a lifetime of the author's thinking, which you can absorb in a few hours. The book ends with his recommended reading list, which is heavy on science, philosophy and a handful of books he rereads.

Try it this week

Pick the book you'd most enjoy, not the one you think you should read, and read it for twenty minutes a day this week. Drop it without guilt if it stops pulling you.

In plain words

Reading is the skill that unlocks all the others. Read what you love until you love reading, then lean toward the foundations that last.

Part II · happiness

Learning Happiness

The second half turns from what you can earn to how you feel. Naval's surprising claim is that happiness is a skill, like fitness or cooking, that you can learn and practise. These nine chapters cover what that skill is made of: choosing it, being present, finding peace, holding fewer desires, seeing that success won't deliver it, dropping envy, building habits and accepting what you can't change.

The idea behind this part

Happiness is mostly the absence of a feeling that something is missing, and you can train yourself toward it.

Chapter 19 · A skill like fitness

Happiness Is Learned

In short

Naval says he was not naturally a happy person, and that he became happier by working at it, the way you'd work at fitness. Happiness, in his view, is a skill: a set of habits and ways of seeing that you can practise. It's not something that arrives when life finally goes right.

The big ideas

  • It's a skill, not a reward. If happiness came from circumstances, rich people would all be happy. They aren't. It comes more from how you meet circumstances.
  • Practice changes the baseline. Small daily practices, done for months, shift how you usually feel.
  • Happiness is mostly peace. Naval describes it as a state where nothing is missing, rather than a high of excitement.
  • Your mind is trainable. You can learn to notice negative thoughts and let them pass instead of following them.
From the book

Naval describes his younger self as anxious, angry and competitive, someone who thought happiness would come after the next success. It didn't. After he'd done well, he noticed he was no happier, so he started treating happiness as a problem to solve. He read philosophy and Buddhism, tried meditation, changed his habits and watched what actually moved his mood. Over the years he found his baseline rising. He says he's now much happier than he was, and that the change came from decisions and practice, not from new circumstances.

Worth knowing

Research supports the idea that happiness can be raised by practice, but not by unlimited amounts. A widely quoted claim that 40 percent of happiness is under your control (Lyubomirsky and colleagues, 2005) has been criticised as too precise; later work suggests the share is uncertain. Practices like gratitude and meditation show modest, real effects.

Try it this week

Choose one small happiness practice, such as ten minutes of meditation, a walk without your phone or writing three things you're grateful for, and do it every day for two weeks. Rate your mood each evening.

In plain words

Happiness is something you get better at with practice, not a prize at the end. Small daily habits, kept up for months, raise how you usually feel.

Chapter 20 · Deciding how you take it

Happiness Is a Choice

In short

At some point, Naval says, you have to decide to be happy. Every event has a negative and a positive way of seeing it, and you choose which to dwell on. Choosing happiness isn't pretending things are fine. It's refusing to let every irritation decide your mood.

The big ideas

  • Make the decision. Happiness begins with the choice that it matters more to you than being right, winning or getting even.
  • Most events are neutral. Rain, traffic, a curt email: your interpretation turns them into good or bad.
  • Notice the story. When you feel bad, look for the story you're telling. You can often tell a different one that's just as true.
  • It takes practice. Choosing is hard at first, because the habit of complaining is strong. It gets easier.
From the book

Naval says he made a conscious decision at one point that he would be happy, and that it changed how he responded to small things. When something went wrong, he'd catch himself complaining and ask whether there was another way to look at it. A cancelled plan became a free evening. A rude person became someone having a bad day. He insists this isn't about denying real problems. It's about not adding unnecessary suffering on top of them. He also says that he values peace so much that he now lets go of arguments he could win.

Try it this week

Each time you start to complain this week, finish the sentence with "...and another way to see it is...". Notice how often there is one.

In plain words

You can't choose what happens, but you can choose how you take it. Decide that happiness matters to you, and practise choosing the kinder story.

Next: Presence →
Chapter 21 · Being where you are

Happiness Requires Presence

In short

Most unhappiness, Naval says, lives in the past or the future: regrets about what happened, worries about what might. When you're fully in the present, there's usually not much wrong. So a happy life is largely a present one, and anything that pulls your attention into the moment helps.

The big ideas

  • The mind wanders constantly. We spend a big part of our waking hours thinking about something other than what we're doing.
  • Wandering costs mood. People tend to be less happy when their minds are elsewhere, even when they wander to pleasant things.
  • Presence is trainable. Meditation, exercise and absorbing work all pull attention back into the moment.
  • Judgment pulls you out. Constantly comparing the moment to how it should be is a way of not being in it.
From the research

In 2010 Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert of Harvard published a study in Science that used an iPhone app to ask about 2,250 adults, at random moments, what they were doing, what they were thinking about and how happy they felt. People's minds were wandering 46.9 percent of the time, and they were less happy when their minds wandered than when they were focused on what they were doing, whatever the activity. The researchers' summary was that a wandering mind is an unhappy mind. Naval's chapter makes the same point from experience: presence is most of what happiness is.

Try it this week

Pick one routine activity you usually do on autopilot, such as showering, walking or eating, and do it this week with full attention, every time.

In plain words

Most of what makes you unhappy is in your head about the past or future. Spend more of your time actually where you are.

Chapter 22 · Nothing left open

Happiness Requires Peace

In short

Naval describes peace as happiness at rest, and happiness as peace in motion. Peace isn't something you add; it's what's left when you remove the things that disturb it: grudges, lies, constant comparison, an overloaded mind. The fewer open loops you carry, the calmer you are.

The big ideas

  • Peace comes from subtraction. You don't buy peace. You get it by removing conflicts, obligations and mental noise.
  • Honesty is peaceful. Lies have to be remembered and defended. Telling the truth leaves nothing to keep track of.
  • Anger burns the holder. Holding anger is like gripping a hot coal to throw at someone; you're the one who gets burned.
  • Quiet the inputs. Constant news, feeds and arguments keep the mind stirred; less input means more peace.
From the book

Naval says he cut a lot of conflict out of his life deliberately. He avoids arguments he doesn't need to have, doesn't keep up feuds, and tries never to lie, partly because it's simpler: he never has to remember what he said to whom. He compares a mind full of unresolved things to a pond with many stones dropping in; each one sends out ripples, and together they keep the surface from ever going still. Remove the stones, he says, and the stillness comes back by itself.

Try it this week

Close one open loop this week: make the apology, settle the argument, send the email you're avoiding, or unfollow one account that keeps you agitated.

In plain words

Peace is what's left when you clear away grudges, lies, comparisons and noise. Remove the things that disturb you and the calm returns on its own.

Chapter 23 · A contract to feel unhappy

Every Desire Is a Chosen Unhappiness

In short

One of Naval's most quoted lines: a desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want. Desire isn't evil, he says, but you should be choosy. Pick one big desire at a time to pursue with everything, and let the others go.

The big ideas

  • Desire creates a gap. The moment you want something, you feel its absence. That gap is a form of suffering you've signed up for.
  • Choose desires carefully. Some desires are worth the cost. Most are habits or borrowed wants that aren't.
  • One at a time. Naval suggests holding one overwhelming desire and letting the rest go, so you can focus and stay calm.
  • Happiness is nothing missing. When you're not wanting anything, you feel complete. That's what happiness mostly is.
From the book

Naval describes catching himself wanting small things all day: a better car, a nicer outcome in a meeting, a particular reply to a message. Each one, he realised, came with a little dose of unhappiness until it was satisfied, and as soon as one was satisfied another replaced it. So he started treating desires as things he chooses rather than things that happen to him. When he notices one, he asks whether it's worth the unhappiness it will cost. He keeps one big goal he cares about and tries not to pick up the rest.

Try it this week

List everything you want right now. Circle the one that matters most. For each of the others, decide consciously to let it go for this month.

In plain words

Every time you want something, you sign up to feel its absence until you get it. Choose your desires carefully, and hold one big one at a time.

Next: Success isn't happiness →
Chapter 24 · The treadmill

Success Does Not Earn Happiness

In short

Naval's experience is that success solves the problem you were trying to solve and then leaves you where you were. Money takes away money worries, but not the restless mind. We adapt quickly to wins, so the glow fades, and a new desire takes the place of the old one.

The big ideas

  • We adapt to wins. A raise, a car or a new title feels wonderful for a while, then becomes normal.
  • Success removes problems, not restlessness. Money removes money problems. It doesn't give you peace if you didn't have it before.
  • The goalposts move. Each achievement sets up the next want, so happiness is always one more step away.
  • Pursue wealth for freedom, not joy. Naval wants wealth so he doesn't have to worry about money, not because it will make him happy.
From the research

In 1978 the psychologists Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman compared lottery winners in Illinois with people who had recently been paralysed in accidents and with a control group. The winners were not much happier than the controls a while later, and they took less pleasure in everyday things. The study helped launch the idea of the hedonic treadmill: we adapt to good and bad changes and drift back toward a baseline. Naval's chapter puts it plainly: after he succeeded, he was surprised to find himself the same person, with the same mind, just without money problems.

Worth knowing

The 1978 study was small (22 winners) and later research complicates it: large Swedish lottery studies (Lindqvist and colleagues, 2020) found lasting gains in life satisfaction from big wins, though not in day-to-day mood. Adaptation is real but not total.

Try it this week

Think of the last thing you were sure would make you happy once you got it. Write down how long the glow lasted. Use that number next time you chase something.

In plain words

Getting what you want feels great for a while, then becomes normal. Pursue success for the freedom it brings, not because it will make you happy.

Next: Envy →
Chapter 25 · The whole-life swap

Envy Is the Enemy of Happiness

In short

Naval says he used to envy people often, and found a test that cured him: you can't take just the parts of someone's life you like. If you wouldn't swap your whole life for theirs, their health, relationships, habits and mind included, there's no point being envious of any one part.

The big ideas

  • Envy picks and chooses. We envy someone's wealth or fame while ignoring their stress, health, family or inner life.
  • The swap is all or nothing. You'd have to take everything: their body, their worries, their marriage, their reactions.
  • Almost nobody passes. When Naval asked himself whom he'd trade with completely, he found nobody.
  • Envy wastes your attention. Time spent wishing for someone else's life is time not spent improving yours.
Naval's example

Naval recalls feeling envious of particular people at different times: those with more money, more fame, better looks. Then he'd think about what he actually knew of their lives, the long hours, the anxiety, the difficult relationships or poor health, and ask whether he'd trade everything, not just the enviable part. The answer was always no. Once he saw that, he says, envy mostly stopped. He wasn't going to swap, so the comparison was pointless, and he could get back to his own life.

Try it this week

Next time you feel envy, name the person and list five things about their life you'd also have to take. Ask honestly whether you'd swap everything.

In plain words

You can't take just the parts of someone's life you want. If you wouldn't swap your whole life for theirs, let the envy go.

Chapter 26 · Small habits, steady mood

Happiness Is Built by Habits

In short

Naval's practical take: happiness is largely built from habits. He lists the ones that work for him, such as exercising every day, meditating, spending less time on the phone and more in the sun, cutting back on alcohol and caffeine, and spending time with people he loves. Each is small; together they move your baseline.

The big ideas

  • Body first. Daily exercise, good sleep and sunlight do more for mood than most mental tricks.
  • Less stimulation. Cutting down on caffeine, alcohol, sugar and screens removes highs and the crashes that follow.
  • Mind practices. Meditation and gratitude train attention away from complaint.
  • People matter. Time with friends and family, without agenda, is one of the most reliable happiness habits.
From the book

Naval says that when he decided to work on happiness, he treated it like an experiment. He tried changes one at a time and watched what happened to his mood. Some made a clear difference: a morning workout, sitting in meditation, less news, fewer stimulants. Others did little. He admits he doesn't keep all of them perfectly. His point is that the effect of any single habit is small, but they add up, and someone who keeps several of them for a year will usually feel noticeably better than someone who keeps none.

Worth knowing

This is Naval's personal list, not a prescription. Research supports several items (regular exercise, sleep, social connection), while the effect of any one habit on happiness is usually modest.

Try it this week

Pick the one habit from Naval's list you're most likely to keep, and do it every day for a week. Add a second only when the first feels automatic.

In plain words

Happiness is built from small daily habits: exercise, sleep, sun, less stimulation, meditation and time with people you love. Each is small, but they add up.

Chapter 27 · Change it, accept it or leave it

Find Happiness in Acceptance

In short

In any situation, Naval says, you have three options: change it, accept it, or leave it. What doesn't work is the fourth option most people choose: staying, not changing anything, and wishing it were different. Acceptance means making peace with what you can't change, not giving up on what you can.

The big ideas

  • Three real options. Change the situation, accept it as it is, or walk away from it. All three can bring peace.
  • Wishing is the trap. Staying while resenting is the one choice that guarantees unhappiness.
  • Accept what's outside your control. Weather, aging, other people's choices: fighting them only adds suffering.
  • Acceptance isn't passivity. You can accept today completely and still work to change tomorrow.
From the book

Naval describes acceptance as the answer to most of the small and large frustrations of life. When something bothers you, he suggests stopping to ask which of the three options you're taking. If you can change it, do. If you can't and it's bearable, accept it fully and let go of the resentment. If you can't and it's not bearable, leave. He adds that with practice you start accepting things faster, and that a lot of life's unhappiness is simply the delay between something happening and our accepting it.

Try it this week

Name one situation you've been complaining about. Decide this week which of the three options you're taking, and act on it.

In plain words

When something bothers you, change it, accept it or leave it. The only bad option is staying and wishing it were different.

Nobody else can make you happy, healthy or free, Naval argues, so the next section is about doing it yourself. Chapter 28 · No one else can be you

Choosing to Be Yourself

In short

Imitating someone else, however successful, means competing with everyone else who is imitating them too, and it's a betrayal of the one thing you have that nobody else does. Naval's advice is to be yourself, fully, even when it's unpopular. Your uniqueness is both your happiness and your edge.

The big ideas

  • Imitation crowds you in. Copy a famous founder and you're one of thousands doing the same thing, usually worse than the original.
  • Your quirks are assets. The odd combination of your interests and experiences is something nobody else has.
  • Don't live for approval. Trying to be what others want leaves you anxious and second-rate at someone else's life.
  • Authenticity is peaceful. Being yourself removes the strain of keeping up a role.
From the book

Naval notes that people often take a hero, a founder, a writer, a celebrity, and try to become them. But the hero got where they are by being themselves, and nobody can repeat that path in someone else's body and circumstances. He says the most interesting people he knows are those who leaned into their own oddness. Chapter 29 · Health comes first

Choosing to Care for Yourself

In short

Naval puts physical health at the top of his priorities, ahead of work and almost everything else, because everything else depends on it. His version is simple: exercise every day, eat mostly real food, sleep enough. He says the daily workout is the habit he'd keep if he could keep only one.

The big ideas

  • Health is the first priority. A sick body makes every other good thing harder to enjoy or achieve.
  • Exercise daily. Naval suggests making exercise a non-negotiable daily habit, ideally first thing, so the rest of the day can't crowd it out.
  • Eat simply. Fewer sugars and processed foods, more real food. Diets are less important than consistency.
  • Sleep and energy. Good sleep and steady energy matter more than squeezing out extra hours.
Naval's example

Naval says that for years he worked out only now and then, and it never stuck. What changed was deciding to do it every single morning, before anything else, even if only briefly. Because it was daily, it stopped being a decision and became a habit, and his energy, mood and focus improved. Exercise every morning this week, even if it's ten minutes. Don't skip a day; make it short instead.

Your health comes before everything else. Exercise every day, eat real food and sleep enough, because every other goal depends on your body.

Chapter 30 · Intermittent fasting for the mind

Meditation + Mental Strength

In short

Naval describes meditation as intermittent fasting for the mind: a break from constant input that lets old thoughts surface and fade. He suggests a demanding experiment: sit for an hour each morning for sixty days, just watching what comes up without fighting it. The result, he says, is a quieter mind and more choice about which thoughts you follow.

The big ideas

  • Let the mind digest. Unprocessed worries and memories surface when you sit still. Letting them come and go clears them.
  • Don't fight thoughts. Naval favours a choiceless awareness: you don't try to stop thinking, you simply notice and don't follow.
  • The gaps get longer. With practice the space between thoughts grows, and that space is where peace lives.
  • Mental strength is noticing. Being able to see an emotion arise, rather than becoming it, is the core of mental resilience.
Naval's example

Naval says he tried many forms of meditation without much success until he settled on the simplest: sit with eyes closed for an hour each morning and don't try to do anything. Thoughts flood in at first, he says, many of them old unresolved things. If you don't fight them, they run out. He suggests committing to sixty days, so that you get past the uncomfortable early weeks. Afterwards, he finds his mind less noisy during the day and himself less reactive; he can see anger rise and decide not to act on it.

Worth knowing

Research on meditation shows real but modest benefits. A 2014 review in JAMA Internal Medicine (Goyal and colleagues) found moderate evidence that mindfulness programmes ease anxiety, depression and pain. An hour a day is Naval's own practice; most studies use much shorter sessions.

Try it this week

Sit for ten minutes each morning this week. Don't try to stop thinking; just notice each thought and let it go. Write the number of days on a calendar.

In plain words

Meditation is a break for your mind. Sit still, let thoughts come and go without following them, and over weeks the noise quiets down.

Chapter 31 · Changing your habits

Choosing to Build Yourself

In short

The greatest superpower, Naval says, is the ability to change yourself. You do it through habits, and habits change slowly. So pick one habit at a time, give it time to stick, and build on it. Trying to change everything at once usually changes nothing.

The big ideas

  • You are your habits. Most of what you do each day is habit, so changing habits changes who you are.
  • One at a time. Willpower is limited. Spread over many changes, it's too thin to make any of them stick.
  • Make small commitments to yourself. Keep tiny promises and you build trust with yourself, which makes bigger changes possible.
  • Be patient with change. Old habits take a long time to fade. Expect relapses and keep going.
From the book

Naval describes treating himself as a work in progress. When he wants to change something, giving up a habit or starting a new one, he picks just that one, makes the commitment, and gives it weeks or months. He notes that habits are like ruts worn into the mind, and breaking one means repeatedly choosing the new path until it becomes the easier one. People who attempt a full life overhaul on the first of January usually find themselves back where they started by February, because they spread their effort across ten changes instead of one.

Try it this week

Choose the single habit change that would help most. Write it down, keep it for thirty days, and don't start another until it's automatic.

In plain words

Changing yourself is the most powerful thing you can do, and you do it one habit at a time. Small kept promises add up.

Chapter 32 · Learning for life

Choosing to Grow Yourself

In short

The ability to learn is the most important skill, Naval says, because with it you can learn everything else. He stresses foundations: maths, science, persuasion and the basics of how things work. The big ideas

Pick the foundation you're weakest in, maths, probability, writing or persuasion, and spend twenty minutes a day on its basics this week.

The most important skill is learning itself. Get the foundations right, like maths, science and persuasion, and you can learn anything else when you need it.

Chapter 33 · Retirement is not a number

Choosing to Free Yourself

In short

Naval values freedom above almost everything: freedom to do what he wants, and freedom from things he doesn't, like obligations, anger and other people's expectations. His definition of retirement is striking: it's when you stop sacrificing today for an imaginary tomorrow. You can do that at any income.

The big ideas

  • Freedom to and freedom from. Money buys freedom to; letting go of anger, expectations and obligations buys freedom from.
  • Retire from sacrifice, not work. If today is fully worth living as it is, you're retired, whether or not you still work.
  • Guard your calendar. Every recurring commitment trades some freedom for something else. Make sure it's worth it.
  • Expectations are chains. Living up to what others expect of you is a cost, even when it feels like a duty.

Naval says he tries to keep his calendar as empty as he can and avoids recurring meetings, because they lock in his future self's time. He's also said that being free of anger is one of the freedoms he cares about most, since an angry person is controlled by whatever provokes them. His point about retirement turns a financial idea into a personal one: some people with millions keep sacrificing today for a later that never arrives, while others with modest means have already arranged their days so that they'd happily live them again tomorrow.

Try it this week

Look at next week's calendar and cancel or shorten one recurring commitment that you keep only out of expectation.

In plain words

Freedom means time and a mind that are yours. You're retired when you stop trading today for an imaginary tomorrow, whatever your bank balance.

Chapter 34 · Personal, and very small

The Meanings of Life

In short

Naval gives more than one answer to what life means. One is that it's personal: nobody can hand you a meaning, so you must find or make your own. Another comes from zooming out: on the scale of the universe, nothing we do lasts, which he finds freeing rather than bleak. If nothing matters cosmically, you can stop taking yourself so seriously and enjoy the time you have.

The big ideas

  • Meaning is personal. Any answer someone else gives you won't fit. You have to arrive at your own.
  • Zoom out. Over billions of years, our worries and triumphs vanish. That perspective shrinks fear and ego.
  • Freedom, not despair. If nothing is cosmically important, you're free to choose what matters to you and to play.
  • Make it count anyway. A small life can still be rich in love, curiosity and creation.
From the book

Naval describes thinking about the universe's long future, as stars burn out and everything ends in a cold, even stillness that physicists call heat death. Against that, our lives are a flicker. He doesn't take this as a reason for gloom. For him it lifts a weight: the embarrassment, the failure, the status games all stop mattering. What's left is a short time to experience being alive, and the freedom to choose what to do with it. He combines that with the personal answer: since the universe won't give you a meaning, you get to choose one.

Try it this week

When something embarrassing or stressful happens this week, ask: will this matter in ten years? In a hundred? Notice how the feeling changes.

In plain words

Life's meaning is something you choose, not something you're handed. Zoom out far enough and nothing lasts, which makes you free to stop worrying and live.

Chapter 35 · What you do, not what you say

Live by Your Values

In short

Naval lists a handful of values he tries to live by: honesty, long-term thinking, not acting from anger, and treating nobody as above or below him. He says values are the things you do even when they cost you, and the people you'll want around you are those who share them.

The big ideas

  • Honesty, always. He tries never to lie, which simplifies life and attracts people who value the truth.
  • Long-term over short-term. He'd rather lose a deal than lose trust, because trust compounds.
  • No anger. Anger, he says, rarely helps and usually costs. He tries not to act or decide while angry.
  • Nobody above or below. Treating everyone the same, from cleaner to chief executive, keeps both ego and envy in check.
From the book

Naval says values aren't what you write on a wall; they show up in choices that cost you something. Telling a client the truth when it loses the sale. Turning down a quick win to protect a relationship. Not sending the angry email. He adds that shared values are how to pick partners, friends and colleagues: talent and charm fade, but someone who is honest and plays long-term games will still be worth knowing in twenty years. When he finds people who share his values, he tries to stay with them for life.

Try it this week

Write down three values you want to live by. This week, notice one moment where living by one of them cost you something, and choose it anyway.

In plain words

Values are what you do when it costs you. Naval's are honesty, long-term thinking, no anger and treating everyone as an equal.

Chapter 36 · Keep what you can test

Rational Buddhism

In short

Naval calls his spiritual view rational Buddhism. He keeps the parts of Buddhist thought he can test in his own experience, like impermanence, the endless chatter of the mind, and how desire creates suffering, and sets aside the parts that need belief on authority, like rebirth. It's a skeptic's way of borrowing ancient wisdom.

The big ideas

  • Test it yourself. If a teaching can be checked by watching your own mind, keep it. If it requires faith, hold it lightly.
  • Everything changes. Impermanence is easy to see once you look; clinging to things that will change brings suffering.
  • The self is less solid than it feels. When you look closely for the 'I' that's having thoughts, it's surprisingly hard to find.
  • Desire and suffering. Watching your own mind shows how wanting creates a feeling of lack, which ties back to the happiness chapters.
From the book

Naval says he came to Buddhism through reading and meditation rather than religion, and that what drew him was how much of it he could verify for himself. Sitting still, he could watch thoughts arise unbidden. He could see how wanting created tension and how letting go eased it. He could notice that the self he defended so fiercely was mostly a story. The supernatural parts he couldn't test, so he leaves them aside without arguing against them. He reads widely in other traditions too, and says the useful ideas tend to appear in many of them.

Try it this week

Pick one claim from any tradition you've heard, such as "thoughts come uninvited", and test it in ten minutes of sitting quietly. Write what you saw.

In plain words

Naval keeps the parts of Buddhism he can check in his own experience, like impermanence and how desire causes suffering, and sets aside what needs faith.

Chapter 37 · Now is the only place to live

The Present Is All We Have

In short

The book ends where it began its happiness half: with the present moment. The past exists only as memory and the future only as imagination; the present is the only thing that's real. Naval suggests that when you're fully here, there's very little to fear and very little missing, and that's as close to happiness as anyone gets.

The big ideas

  • The past is a story. Memory is a reconstruction. Living in it means living in a story you keep editing.
  • The future is imagination. Worry and longing are about events that aren't happening. They cost real time now.
  • Now is enough. In the present moment, most of life's problems don't exist; they're waiting in the past or future.
  • Return, again and again. Presence isn't a state you reach once; it's a habit of returning whenever you notice you've left.
From the book

In the book's closing pages Naval reflects that the people who seem most at peace are those who live most fully in the present. Children, he notes, aren't yet carrying decades of memory and plans, which is part of why they find the world so absorbing. Adults carry both, and spend much of their lives reviewing what happened or rehearsing what might. He doesn't suggest throwing away memory or planning; you need both. But he argues that you should visit the past and future deliberately, then come home to now, because the present is all any of us ever actually has.

Try it this week

Set three random reminders on your phone this week. When one goes off, notice whether you were in the past, the future or now. Then come back to now.

In plain words

The past is memory and the future is imagination. The present is the only place you can live, so keep coming back to it.
