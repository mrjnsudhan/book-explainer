Phil M. Jones's short book collects 23 sets of "magic words": phrases that make people more likely to listen, decide and say yes, whether you're selling, leading a team or asking a friend for help. This guide takes them one at a time, with how each one sounds in a real conversation and a lab where you write your own version.

Stage one · chapters 1 to 5

Open the Door

The first five sets of magic words are about getting an idea considered at all. People are busy, sceptical and used to being sold to. These phrases lower their guard, make them curious, and let them picture what you are offering before they decide.

The idea behind this stage

People decide whether to listen in the first few words. Start in a way that makes saying "tell me more" the easy answer.

Chapter 1 · Introduce an idea without pressure

I'm Not Sure If It's for You, But

In short

Opening with "I'm not sure if it's for you, but…" takes the pressure off. It tells people they are free to say no, which makes them far more willing to hear you out.

The big ideas

  • It removes the threat. When someone senses a sales pitch coming, their guard goes up. Saying up front that it may not be for them signals that you aren't going to push, so they relax.
  • It triggers curiosity. Being told something might not be for you makes you want to know what it is. The brain hears a gentle challenge and wants to decide for itself.
  • The worst case is "no". The listener can only reply that it isn't for them, which is exactly what you said might happen. You lose nothing, and you keep the relationship.
  • Use it anywhere. It works for a product, a job opening, a charity event, a new idea for your manager, or a suggestion to a friend about a weekend plan.

How it sounds

You

I'm not sure if it's for you, but we're starting a small Tuesday running group. Would you like to know more?

Them

Maybe. What kind of pace?

In plain words

Instead of "You should really try this", which makes people defend their right to say no, you hand them that right at the start. People who feel free to choose are much more willing to listen.

Next: Open-Minded →
Chapter 2 · Ask people to consider something new

Open-Minded

In short

Asking "How open-minded are you about…?" makes nearly everyone say "quite open-minded", because almost nobody wants to see themselves as closed-minded. Then you have their attention.

The big ideas

  • People want to be seen as open. Being open-minded is something almost everyone values in themselves. Ask the question and the answer is nearly always yes.
  • It works on the most sceptical. The person who looks least likely to agree is often the one who most wants to prove they are open. The question gives them a chance.
  • Pair it with a gentle idea. "Would you be open-minded about trying a different supplier for one order?" asks for a small, low-risk step, not a big leap.
  • It sets the tone. Once someone has said they are open-minded, they will tend to stay consistent with that description for the rest of the conversation.

How it sounds

You

How open-minded would you be about a four-day week trial for the team?

Them

Fairly open-minded. What would it involve?

In plain words

If you ask "Would you consider this?", you invite a quick no. If you ask how open-minded someone is, you invite them to describe themselves in a good light, and most people choose to.

Next: What Do You Know? →
Chapter 3 · Gently challenge a fixed opinion

What Do You Know?

In short

When someone has dismissed something they haven't really looked at, asking "What do you know about…?" invites them to admit, to themselves, that they don't know much yet.

The big ideas

  • Opinions often rest on very little. People turn down ideas based on an old experience, a headline, or a friend's complaint. The question shows the gap without you having to point it out.
  • They answer honestly. Most people will say "not much, really". That is the moment they become open to hearing more.
  • It avoids an argument. Telling someone they are wrong makes them dig in. Asking what they know lets them change their mind on their own terms.
  • Follow up with an offer. "Would it make sense to see the latest figures before you decide?" turns the gap into a next step.

How it sounds

Them

Electric cars? No, the batteries don't last.

You

What do you know about the newer batteries?

Them

Honestly, not much.

In plain words

It's the difference between "You're wrong about that" and "What have you heard about it?" The first starts a fight. The second starts a conversation.

Next: How Would You Feel If? →
Chapter 4 · Move people with a future feeling

How Would You Feel If?

In short

"How would you feel if…?" asks people to step into the future and feel the result of a decision. Feelings, more than facts, are what make people act.

The big ideas

  • Decisions are emotional. Facts help people justify a choice, but emotion is what moves them. This question connects your idea to a feeling the listener can sense right now.
  • Use it in two directions. Paint the good outcome ("How would you feel if the project shipped a month early?") or the cost of doing nothing ("How would you feel if the same thing happened next year?").
  • They do the persuading. You aren't claiming anything. They imagine the feeling and describe it in their own words, which is far more convincing to them than anything you could say.
  • Make it specific. A vague future ("feel better") does little. A concrete one ("walk into the meeting knowing every number") lands.

How it sounds

You

How would you feel if you finished this course and could finally fix your own website?

Them

Honestly? Pretty proud.

In plain words

People don't buy a gym membership, they buy how they'll feel in their clothes in six months. Asking them to feel it now is more powerful than describing the membership.

Next: Just Imagine →
Chapter 5 · Let them see it before it happens

Just Imagine

In short

"Just imagine…" invites people to picture a scene. Once they have seen it in their mind, it starts to feel real and possible, and they want it.

The big ideas

  • Imagining is half-way to believing. When we picture something clearly, the brain treats it a little like a memory. The future becomes familiar, so it feels achievable.
  • It bypasses resistance. Nobody argues with an invitation to imagine. There's nothing to disagree with, just a picture to look at.
  • Use the senses. Describe what they'll see, hear and feel. "Just imagine stepping off the plane into warm air, with a week of nothing planned" beats "Just imagine a holiday".
  • Put them in the picture. Make the listener the main character, doing something they care about, at a specific time and place.

How it sounds

You

Just imagine next Christmas with the kitchen finished and the whole family round the new table.

Them

That would be something.

In plain words

A travel brochure doesn't list flight numbers; it shows you on the beach. "Just imagine" does the same thing with words.

Stage two · chapters 6 to 8

Make It Happen

Interest that never becomes action is wasted. These chapters deal with the two most common stalls, "I don't have time" and "I haven't got round to it", and with small word swaps that turn a yes-or-no question into one that moves things forward.

The idea behind this stage

Questions that assume the good outcome are far more likely to get it than questions that offer an easy no.

Chapter 6 · Get past "I'm too busy"

When Would Be a Good Time?

In short

"I don't have time" is often a polite no. Asking "When would be a good time to…?" assumes a good time exists, and gets people to name one.

The big ideas

  • The question assumes yes. You're no longer asking whether they'll do it, only when. Most people find it awkward to answer "never".
  • It respects their time. You aren't pushing for now. You're letting them pick the moment, which feels considerate.
  • It creates a commitment. Once someone has named a time, they've made a small promise. Small promises are kept far more often than vague intentions.
  • Pin it down. Follow up with the specific: "Thursday at ten, then? I'll send an invite." A time in the diary beats "sometime next week".

How it sounds

Them

I'd love to, but I'm flat out this week.

You

No problem. When would be a good time to look at it properly?

Them

Maybe Thursday morning?

In plain words

Ask "Do you have time to talk?" and "No" is an easy answer. Ask "When would be a good time to talk?" and the easy answer is a day of the week.

Next: I'm Guessing You Haven't Got Around To →
Chapter 7 · Follow up without making people feel bad

I'm Guessing You Haven't Got Around To

In short

When someone hasn't done what they promised, "I'm guessing you haven't got around to… yet?" gives them a face-saving way to admit it, or the pleasure of saying they have.

The big ideas

  • It removes the shame. You've already assumed they're behind, kindly. There's nothing to hide or defend, so they tell you the truth.
  • It wins either way. If they haven't done it, they agree easily and you agree the next step. If they have, they get to correct you with pride and tell you about it.
  • No chasing tone. "Did you do the thing yet?" sounds like a teacher. This sounds like a friend who understands people are busy.
  • Use it everywhere. Unpaid invoices, unread proposals, homework, an un-booked doctor's appointment: any follow-up that usually feels awkward.

How it sounds

You

I'm guessing you haven't got around to reading the proposal yet?

Them

Ha, no, sorry. It's been mad.

You

Totally understand. When would be a good time to go through it together?

In plain words

It's the opposite of a nag. You take the awkwardness out by saying the likely truth first, so the other person can simply agree and move on.

Next: Simple Swaps →
Chapter 8 · Small changes to everyday questions

Simple Swaps

In short

Some everyday questions quietly invite the wrong answer. Swapping a few words, such as "What questions do you have?" for "Do you have any questions?", changes what people say.

The big ideas

  • "Do you have any questions?" invites "No". People say no to avoid looking slow. "What questions do you have for me?" assumes there are some, and gives permission to ask.
  • "Can I get your number?" invites hesitation. "What's the best number to reach you on?" assumes you'll be calling and only asks which number.
  • "Did you like it?" invites a yes or a no. "What did you like about it?" makes people search for positives, and they remember what they say.
  • Swap "if" for "when". "If you go ahead" keeps the decision open. "When you go ahead" helps people picture it already happening.

How it sounds

You

So that's the plan. What questions do you have for me?

Them

Actually, yes, how does billing work?

In plain words

These questions are like doors that open one way. The usual wording opens toward "no"; the swap opens toward a real answer.

Stage three · chapters 9 to 14

Guide the Decision

Most people struggle with decisions, and many would rather be guided than left alone with a pile of options. These magic words simplify the choice, make your recommendation easy to agree with, and calm the worry that stops people saying yes.

The idea behind this stage

Confusion leads to "no". A clear, simple frame helps people decide, and most of them are grateful for it.

Chapter 9 · Make a choice simple

You Have Three Options

In short

When people are stuck, laying out "As I see it, you have three options" makes the decision manageable, and lets you place your recommendation where it looks best.

The big ideas

  • Too much choice freezes people. With many options, people often choose nothing. Three is enough to feel free and few enough to compare.
  • Use a clear structure. Option one: do nothing and keep the problem. Option two: an alternative that's hard or costly. Option three: the solution you recommend, which looks sensible next to the first two.
  • It shows you understand. Summing up their situation fairly, including the option of doing nothing, shows you've listened and aren't hiding anything.
  • Then ask which is easiest. End with "Which of these is easiest for you?" Most people pick the one you've made obviously best.

How it sounds

You

As I see it, you have three options. Keep the old laptop and live with it crashing. Spend a weekend rebuilding it yourself. Or swap it for the new one today. Which is easiest for you?

Them

Honestly, the new one.

In plain words

A doctor who says "We can wait and see, try physiotherapy, or operate" helps you decide. One who lists twenty treatments leaves you more confused than when you walked in.

Use it well

Lay out real options honestly. The frame works because it helps people decide; stacking it with a fake "bad" option or hiding a real alternative is manipulation, and people notice.

Next: There Are Two Types of People →
Chapter 10 · Let them choose who to be

There Are Two Types of People

In short

"There are two types of people: those who… and those who…" offers a choice of identity. Most people pick the one that sounds better, and then act like it.

The big ideas

  • We act to fit our self-image. People want their actions to match the kind of person they believe they are. Give them two descriptions and they'll choose one to live up to.
  • Make one side clearly better. "There are two types of people: those who wait for the right moment, and those who make it." Few people want to be the first type.
  • It feels like their choice. You haven't told them what to do. They've chosen which side of the line they stand on.
  • Use it with care. It works best when both descriptions are fair. If one side is a cartoon, people feel pushed and resist.

How it sounds

You

There are two types of people when it comes to pensions: those who hope it works out, and those who check now while there's time to fix it.

Them

I'd better be the second.

In plain words

It's like asking "Are you a morning person?" Once someone has a label they like, they tend to behave like it.

Next: I Bet You're a Bit Like Me →
Chapter 11 · Build quick common ground

I Bet You're a Bit Like Me

In short

"I bet you're a bit like me…" followed by something almost everyone agrees with gets an easy early yes, and makes you feel like allies, not strangers.

The big ideas

  • People like people like them. Shared views create trust quickly. This phrase finds common ground in one sentence.
  • Pick something nearly universal. "…you like to know exactly what you're paying for." "…you'd rather sort this once than keep coming back to it." Something easy to agree with.
  • Then lead from there. Once you've agreed on what matters, your suggestion becomes the obvious way to get it.
  • It's a softer statement. Telling someone what they want feels presumptuous. Saying you bet they're like you is friendly and easy to correct.

How it sounds

You

I bet you're a bit like me, and hate being surprised by extra charges later on.

Them

Absolutely.

You

That's why I'd go for the fixed-price option.

In plain words

Think of the way a good waiter says "If you're anything like me, you'll want to save room for dessert." You nod, you feel understood, and you look at the dessert menu.

Next: If… Then →
Chapter 12 · Link a small step to a result they want

If… Then

In short

"If you… then you will…" connects an action to an outcome in a way that sounds logical and is hard to argue with.

The big ideas

  • It sounds like cause and effect. The structure is how we describe rules and facts, so the listener tends to accept the link without questioning it.
  • Start with something they want. "If you'd like the kitchen done before summer, then we should order the units this month." The goal is theirs; the action follows naturally.
  • Keep the step small. The "if" part should be easy to say yes to. Once they accept it, the "then" part feels like common sense.
  • Be truthful. The link has to be real. A made-up consequence breaks trust the moment it's tested.

How it sounds

You

If you give the free trial a go this week, then you'll know by Friday whether it saves you time.

Them

Fair enough.

In plain words

Parents use it all the time: "If you put your shoes on now, then we can stop at the park." It works because it's simple, clear and connects the step to something the child wants.

Next: Don't Worry →
Chapter 13 · Calm people down and take charge

Don't Worry

In short

When someone is anxious, "Don't worry" followed by what you'll take care of relaxes them and shows you're in control.

The big ideas

  • Worry blocks decisions. An anxious person stalls. Removing the worry is often all it takes to get a yes.
  • It signals authority. People who say "Don't worry" calmly sound like they've handled this before, because usually they have.
  • Follow it with the fix. "Don't worry, I'll handle the paperwork" works. "Don't worry" on its own can sound dismissive.
  • Use it after a problem. When something has gone wrong, it lowers the temperature fast and moves the conversation on to the solution.

How it sounds

Them

I've never done a tax return, I'll probably mess it up.

You

Don't worry, we'll go through it line by line together this afternoon.

In plain words

Think of a pilot's calm voice over the speakers during turbulence. Confidence is contagious, and so is worry. Choose which one you spread.

Next: Most People →
Chapter 14 · Recommend without pushing

Most People

In short

"Most people in your situation…" lets you recommend a choice by pointing to what others do. People feel safe following the crowd.

The big ideas

  • We look to others when unsure. When people don't know what to do, they copy what similar people did. "Most people" gives them that information.
  • It isn't you pushing. You're not saying "you should". You're reporting what others chose, so the advice feels neutral.
  • Make it true. Only use it when it's actually what most of your customers or colleagues do. If it isn't true, don't say it.
  • Add the reason. "Most people go for the annual plan, because it works out two months cheaper" gives social proof and a reason together.

How it sounds

Them

Which size should I go for?

You

Most people with a family of four go for the larger one, so they're not running out mid-week.

In plain words

Restaurants put "most popular" next to a dish for a reason. When you don't know what to order, knowing what others liked makes the choice feel safe.

Next: The Good News →
Stage four · chapters 15 to 18

Close With Confidence

The end of a conversation is where most people go quiet, hint, or apologise. These chapters give you calm ways to share bad news, describe what happens next, understand an objection, and keep the door open when someone is about to say no.

The idea behind this stage

Most people wait for the other person to close. Take the lead, kindly, and make the next step obvious.

Chapter 15 · Turn bad news into a way forward

The Good News

In short

After any setback, "The good news is…" moves attention from the problem to what can be done, and makes you the person with the answer.

The big ideas

  • Attention follows your words. Whatever you put after "the good news is" becomes the focus. Point it at the solution.
  • It works in any mood. There's nearly always some good news: it's fixable, it was caught early, there's a cheaper option, or now we know.
  • It sets you apart. Most people dwell on what went wrong. The person who says "the good news is" sounds like a leader.
  • Don't hide the bad. Be honest about the problem first, then turn to the good news. Skipping the bad part sounds like spin.

How it sounds

Them

We missed the deadline for the grant.

You

That's frustrating. The good news is there's another round in March, and we'll have a much stronger application by then.

In plain words

A mechanic who says "Your brakes are worn, but the good news is the discs are fine, so it's a cheap fix" leaves you relieved, even though you're still paying.

Next: What Happens Next →
Chapter 16 · Close by describing the next steps

What Happens Next

In short

Instead of asking "Do you want to go ahead?", describe "What happens next is…". People who can see the next steps clearly tend to follow them.

The big ideas

  • A question invites a pause. "Shall we go ahead?" gives people a moment to hesitate. Describing the steps assumes you're already moving.
  • Clarity removes fear. Much hesitation is not knowing what will happen. Laying out the steps takes away the unknown.
  • Keep it short and concrete. Two or three steps with times: "I'll send the form today, you sign it by Friday, and we start Monday."
  • End with the first step. Finish with something they can do now, like "So all I need from you is a start date."

How it sounds

You

What happens next is I'll email the plan tonight, we'll have a 15-minute call on Wednesday, and the team starts on Monday. All I need is the best email for the plan.

Them

Sure, use my work one.

In plain words

A good receptionist doesn't ask whether you want to see the doctor. They say "Take a seat, the nurse will call you in about ten minutes, and then you'll see Dr Patel." You just follow along.

Next: What Makes You Say That? →
Chapter 17 · Understand an objection before answering it

What Makes You Say That?

In short

When someone pushes back, asking "What makes you say that?" gets them to explain the real concern, so you answer the right problem.

The big ideas

  • The first objection isn't always the real one. "It's too expensive" might mean "I can't afford it this month", "I don't see the value", or "I need to ask my partner". Each needs a different answer.
  • It buys you time. Instead of reacting defensively, you calmly ask a question while you listen and think.
  • People often answer themselves. Explaining an objection out loud sometimes shows the person it isn't as big as they thought.
  • Tone is everything. Ask with real curiosity. Said sharply, it sounds like a challenge.

How it sounds

Them

I don't think it's the right time.

You

What makes you say that?

Them

We're hiring two people next month and I'm worried about the workload.

In plain words

A good doctor doesn't prescribe as soon as you say "my back hurts". They ask what makes you say that, where, and when. Objections deserve the same diagnosis.

Next: Before You Make Your Mind Up →
Chapter 18 · Keep the door open when they're leaning toward no

Before You Make Your Mind Up

In short

When someone is about to say no, "Before you make your mind up…" asks them to pause and consider one more thing, before the no is final.

The big ideas

  • A spoken no is hard to undo. Once someone has said no out loud, they tend to stick with it to stay consistent. Catch the moment before it's said.
  • It respects their decision. You're not arguing. You're acknowledging they're deciding, and asking for one more fact to be part of it.
  • Offer something new. Follow it with a fact, an option or a question they haven't heard yet. Repeating your pitch won't help.
  • Accept the answer. If it's still no after that, leave gracefully. You'll have earned the chance to come back another time.

How it sounds

Them

I think we'll leave it this year.

You

Before you make your mind up, can I show you the smaller package? It's half the price and covers the two things you mentioned.

In plain words

It's like stopping a friend from sending an angry message: "Before you hit send, read it once more." You aren't telling them what to decide, just asking them to decide with everything in view.

Stage five · chapters 19 to 23

Ask for More

The last group is about asking: for a commitment in return, for a larger amount, for one more small thing, for a favour, and for the truth behind a polite brush-off. Done well, each ask feels natural to the person hearing it.

The idea behind this stage

You rarely get what you don't ask for. Small changes to how you ask make a yes far more likely.

Chapter 19 · Trade a yes for a yes

If I Can, Will You?

In short

When someone asks for something you might be able to give, reply "If I can…, will you…?" so that any concession you make comes with a commitment from them.

The big ideas

  • Never give something for nothing. If you agree to a discount or a deadline straight away, you get nothing back. Making it conditional turns it into a deal.
  • It tests the real objection. If they say "yes, I'd sign today", you know price was the only issue. If they hesitate, there's something else to uncover.
  • Ask before you check. "If I can get it delivered by Friday, will you place the order today?" Get the commitment, then make it happen.
  • Keep it fair. Ask for something proportionate. A big ask in return for a small favour feels like a trap.

How it sounds

Them

Could you do it for £900 instead of £1,000?

You

If I can get it to £900, will you sign today?

Them

Yes, I would.

In plain words

It's how sensible negotiation works in everyday life: "If I cook, will you wash up?" Both sides give something, and everyone knows where they stand.

Next: Enough →
Chapter 20 · Suggest an amount and let them round up

Enough

In short

Asking "Would three be enough?" suggests an amount while letting the other person decide. If your suggestion is a little high, many people go with it, or adjust from there.

The big ideas

  • "Enough" feels like a ceiling. People rarely want to be caught short. Asking whether something is enough makes them think about running out.
  • Your number becomes the anchor. The figure you suggest is where their thinking starts. Suggest a sensible, slightly generous amount, and most people adjust from there.
  • It feels considerate. You're checking they'll have what they need, not trying to sell more. That's why it rarely feels pushy.
  • It works off-sales too. "Would an hour be enough for the meeting?" "Would £20 each be enough for the gift?" It sets the scale of any shared decision.

How it sounds

Them

I'll take some of the printer cartridges.

You

Would six be enough to get you through to the end of the quarter?

Them

Hmm, make it eight to be safe.

In plain words

A café that asks "Would you like a large?" gets fewer large coffees than one that asks "Would a regular be enough, or would you like a large?" The question makes you check whether you'd be left wanting more.

Use it well

This works because of anchoring, the tendency to adjust from the first number you hear. Use it to suggest what people genuinely need, not to load them up with things they don't.

Next: Just One More Thing →
Chapter 21 · Make a small final ask

Just One More Thing

In short

After the main business is done and everyone has relaxed, "Just one more thing…" makes a smaller request that feels easy to agree to.

The big ideas

  • Guards are down at the end. Once the main decision is made, people relax. A small extra ask at that point feels minor.
  • Ask for less than before. If they said no to the big thing, ask for a smaller one: a trial, a referral, a review. After a no, people often like to give a yes.
  • It's a famous move. The TV detective Columbo made it his trademark: he'd head for the door, turn round and ask the question that mattered.
  • Keep it genuinely small. A review, an introduction, a date for the next chat. Anything big deserves its own conversation.

How it sounds

You

Thanks again, I'll get that sent over. Oh, just one more thing: would you be happy to leave a short review once it arrives?

Them

Of course.

In plain words

It's like asking for the salt after the meal has been served. Everyone's settled and happy; a small request at that moment hardly feels like a request at all.

Next: A Favour →
Chapter 22 · Get a yes before you ask

A Favour

In short

Starting with "Could I ask you a small favour?" gets most people to say yes before they know what it is, which makes them much more likely to help when you tell them.

The big ideas

  • People like to help. Being asked for a favour makes people feel trusted and generous. Few turn down a small favour in principle.
  • The yes comes first. Once someone has agreed to help, they want to stay consistent with that, so they're more likely to agree to the actual request.
  • Make it a real favour. Use it for things like a referral, a review or an introduction, not for a sale disguised as a favour.
  • Say thank you. Thank them properly afterwards. A favour well thanked makes the next one easier.

How it sounds

You

Could I ask you a small favour?

Them

Sure, what is it?

You

Do you know anyone else on your street who's been thinking about solar panels?

In plain words

"Can you do me a favour?" "Sure." "Could you look after the cat on Saturday?" By the time the real request arrives, they've already decided to help.

Next: Just Out of Curiosity →
Chapter 23 · Ask the direct question, softly

Just Out of Curiosity

In short

"Just out of curiosity…" turns a blunt question into a light, friendly one, so people give you the real answer instead of a polite brush-off.

The big ideas

  • It lowers the stakes. Framed as curiosity, the question feels casual. The person doesn't feel cornered, so they answer honestly.
  • Perfect for "I need to think about it". "Just out of curiosity, what specifically do you need to think about?" often reveals the real hesitation.
  • Use it to learn. "Just out of curiosity, what made you choose us in the end?" gives you useful feedback without sounding needy.
  • Listen to the answer. The phrase only works if you really are curious. Then use what you hear.

How it sounds

Them

I need to have a think about it.

You

Of course. Just out of curiosity, what is it you'd like to think over?

Them

Mainly whether my partner will agree with the cost.

In plain words

"Why haven't you replied?" sounds like an accusation. "Just out of curiosity, did my email reach you?" sounds like a friendly check, and gets a real answer.

Jones's point is that the right words only help if they come out naturally under pressure. Pick the two or three phrases that fit your situations, write your own versions, and practise them until they sound like you. Everything you saved from the labs is below, kept in this browser.
