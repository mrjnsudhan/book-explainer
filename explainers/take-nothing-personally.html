A section-by-section guide to Shubham Kumar Singh's book

Take Nothing Personally,
Explained

Most of what stings you, a short reply, a cold look, a "no", was shaped by the other person's day long before it reached you. Take Nothing Personally is Shubham Kumar Singh's short, gentle guide to noticing that, so you stop spending your energy on reactions and spend it on your own life instead. The book comes in two parts: Take Nothing Personally steps you out of the emotional noise (overreacting, needing approval, rejection, overthinking, boundaries), and Focus on Yourself turns the energy you win back toward a calm, intentional life. This guide covers twelve themes across those two parts, with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

What people say tells you about their world; what you do next is the only part that belongs to yours.

Part I · take nothing personally

Take Nothing Personally

The first half of the book is about stepping out of the emotional noise. Most of the hurt we carry around doesn't come from what happened, but from what we decided it meant about us. These seven sections look at where that habit comes from and how to loosen it: other people's moods, the gap before you react, the spotlight you imagine on yourself, the hunger for approval, criticism and rejection, the replay loop in your head, and the boundaries that keep your peace intact.

The idea behind this part

Other people's words and moods are information about them. You can notice them, learn from them and still decline to carry them.

Section 1 · Other people speak from their own day

It Was Never About You

In short

When someone is short with you, it's natural to ask what you did. Far more often the answer is: nothing. People's words come out of their own mood, stress, history and assumptions, and you happen to be standing where those words land.

The big ideas

  • Everyone carries weather. A tired parent, a manager behind on a deadline, a friend who just got bad news: each of them speaks through that state. Their tone is a report on their inside, not a verdict on yours.
  • We blame character, not circumstances. When others behave badly we assume it's who they are; when we do, we know it was the day we had. Turning that same generosity outward shrinks most stings.
  • Personal is a choice of story. The event is "she didn't reply". The personal part is the story you add: "she's annoyed with me". You can't always stop the first thought, but you can ask for a second one.
  • Not personal doesn't mean not important. Taking nothing personally isn't pretending you don't care. You can still address a problem; you just don't have to treat it as proof of your worth.
From the research

In a classic 1967 study, Edward Jones and Victor Harris asked students to read essays about Fidel Castro, some for him and some against. The readers were told plainly that each writer had been assigned which side to argue. Even so, they judged that the writers of pro-Castro essays really did like Castro. Psychologist Lee Ross later named this habit the fundamental attribution error: we read people's behaviour as a window into who they are and underweight the situation that produced it. The same error runs in reverse when a curt message lands on you. You read it as being about you, when the situation behind it, the deadline or the bad night, is doing most of the work.

Worth knowing

Later research found the attribution error is weaker in some cultures and situations than first claimed, but the basic tilt (explaining others by character and ourselves by circumstance) shows up reliably.

Try it this week

The next time a message stings, write down three things that might be going on for the sender that have nothing to do with you. Only then decide whether to reply.

In plain words

People talk from inside their own day. When someone is sharp with you, most of it is about what they're carrying. Ask what else might be going on before you take it as a judgement on you.

Section 2 · Overreacting and the gap that fixes it

The Space Before You React

In short

A sting arrives as a wave of feeling that rises quickly and, left alone, settles. Overreacting usually means acting at the top of the wave. The single most useful skill in the book is a pause: let the wave pass, name what you feel, then choose what to do.

The big ideas

  • Feelings are waves. A rush of anger or hurt peaks and then fades if you don't keep feeding it. The trouble starts when you reply, post or decide right at the peak.
  • Replaying refuels the wave. Every time you rehearse the insult in your head, you set the wave off again. A short sting becomes an afternoon.
  • Name it to tame it. Putting a word on the feeling, "I'm embarrassed", "I feel dismissed", moves you from being the feeling to noticing it.
  • Respond, don't react. A reaction is automatic; a response is chosen. The difference is a few breaths, a walk, or a draft you don't send yet.
From the research

In 2007 Matthew Lieberman and colleagues at UCLA showed people photos of angry and frightened faces while scanning their brains. When people simply looked, the amygdala, a region involved in threat responses, lit up. When they chose a word to describe the emotion on the face, such as "angry", that response was lower, and a region linked to self-control in the prefrontal cortex became more active. Choosing a matching name, like a person's gender, didn't have the same effect. Labelling a feeling is a small act, but it seems to put a little distance between you and the wave.

Worth knowing

You'll often see a "90-second rule" for emotions, from neuroanatomist Jill Bolte Taylor's book My Stroke of Insight. It's a useful picture of a wave that passes, not a measured law; how long a feeling lasts varies a lot and depends heavily on whether you keep thinking about its cause.

Try it this week

Pick a rule for this week: no replies to anything that stung until twenty minutes have passed. Write the reply if you want, but don't send it until then.

In plain words

The feeling that comes with a sting is a wave. If you wait for it to pass, and don't keep replaying the cause, it settles. Then you can answer as the person you want to be.

Section 3 · Overthinking what other people think

Nobody Is Watching as Closely as You Think

In short

We feel as if our mistakes, outfits and awkward moments are on a stage. In reality, other people are busy starring in their own lives and notice far less of ours than we fear. Much of what we take personally is a reaction to an audience that isn't paying attention.

The big ideas

  • You are the centre of your own view. Because you experience every moment of your own life, your slip feels huge. To everyone else, it's a blip in their busy day.
  • Other people are anxious too. The people you imagine judging you are mostly wondering how they're coming across.
  • Small mistakes fade fast. The stumble in your talk or the typo in your email is often forgotten by others within minutes, while you replay it for days.
  • Lower the stakes, act more freely. Once you know the spotlight is dimmer than it feels, you can speak up, try things and be a beginner without as much fear.
From the research

In 2000 Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky asked Cornell students to put on a T-shirt with a large picture of the singer Barry Manilow, a choice most of them found embarrassing, and walk into a room where other students were filling in questionnaires. Afterwards the shirt-wearers guessed how many people in the room had noticed the shirt. They guessed about half. When the researchers asked the people in the room, fewer than a quarter (around 23 percent) could name who was on it. The researchers called this the spotlight effect: we overestimate how much others notice about us, because we can't stop noticing ourselves.

Try it this week

After your next awkward moment, ask one person who was there whether they noticed. Write down what you expected them to say and what they actually said.

In plain words

People notice much less about you than you think, because they're busy thinking about themselves. The spotlight you feel is mostly your own.

Section 4 · Letting go of the need for approval

You Can't Please Everyone

In short

Wanting to be liked is human. Needing everyone's approval is a trap, because people want different and often opposite things. The more you bend to please everyone, the less of you is left, and you still won't reach everyone.

The big ideas

  • Tastes conflict. One person wants you louder, another quieter; one loves your jokes, another finds them too much. No single version of you wins every vote.
  • Pleasing costs you yourself. Each adjustment is small, but they add up until you're performing a person you don't recognise, and resenting the people you're performing for.
  • Your people find the real you. When you're recognisably yourself, the people who like that version can find you. Those friendships don't need constant upkeep.
  • Approval is a nice bonus, not fuel. Enjoy it when it comes. Just don't make it the thing that keeps you running.
A scene

Picture a new team member who spends her first month trying to fit everyone. In the morning meeting she's cautious because the lead likes quiet; at lunch she's loud because the sales team likes energy; in reviews she softens every opinion. By the end of the month she's exhausted, and nobody can say what she actually thinks. In the second month she starts saying what she thinks, kindly. Two people cool on her. Four others start bringing her into decisions, because now they know what they're getting. She's liked by fewer people in total than she imagined at the start, but by the right ones, and it takes far less effort.

Worth knowing

This lab is an illustrative model: 24 people with tastes spread along one line. Real people judge on many lines at once, which makes pleasing everyone even harder.

Try it this week

Notice one moment this week when you're about to soften an opinion only to be liked. Say it plainly and kindly instead, and see what actually happens.

In plain words

People want different things from you, so you'll never please all of them. Trying wears you out and hides who you are. Be yourself and let the right people find you.

Section 5 · Handling rejection and feedback

Criticism Is Information, Not a Verdict

In short

Rejection and criticism hurt because they feel like a judgement on who you are. Most of the time they're narrower than that: a comment on one piece of work, mixed with the critic's own mood and style. Pull those apart, keep the useful part, and let the rest go.

The big ideas

  • Three things arrive at once. A piece of criticism usually carries some information, some tone or mood from the person giving it, and an implied verdict on you. Only the first is worth keeping.
  • The work is not the worker. "This draft is unclear" is about a draft. Hearing it as "you are not good enough" is a translation you added.
  • No is usually about fit. A rejected application, a declined invitation or an unanswered pitch reflects their needs, timing and taste far more than your value.
  • Ask a question instead of defending. "What would make it clearer?" turns a sting into something useful and keeps you out of a fight you don't need.
From the research

In 2003 Naomi Eisenberger, Matthew Lieberman and Kipling Williams had people play Cyberball, a simple computer game of catch with two other players, inside a brain scanner. Partway through, the other two stopped throwing the ball to them. Being left out of a trivial game made people feel rejected, and it was accompanied by activity in brain regions that are also involved in the distress of physical pain. Rejection really does hurt, which is exactly why it helps to look at what was actually said, and not only at how it felt.

Worth knowing

How closely social pain and physical pain share brain circuits is still debated; later work suggests the overlap is partly about salience and alarm in general. The finding that exclusion stings quickly and strongly, even from strangers in a game, has held up well.

Try it this week

Take the last piece of criticism that stuck with you. Write out only the useful information in it, in neutral words, and throw the rest away.

In plain words

Criticism mixes useful information with the other person's tone and an imagined judgement of you. Keep the information, let the rest go, and ask a question if you need more.

Section 6 · Overthinking and the rumination loop

Stop Replaying the Scene

In short

Thinking a problem through can help. Going round and round the same hurtful moment doesn't. Rumination feels like working on it, but it mostly keeps the feeling alive and pulls you away from the day you're actually in.

The big ideas

  • Replaying isn't solving. Problem-solving moves toward a decision. Rumination circles the same scene, the same words, the same "why", and ends where it started.
  • Name the loop. Noticing "I'm replaying again" is the first step out. You don't need to win the argument in your head to stop having it.
  • Absorb your attention elsewhere. A walk, a task that needs your hands, a conversation about something else: anything absorbing gives the loop nothing to feed on.
  • Write it once. Putting the whole thing on paper, one time, gives your mind permission to stop rehearsing it.
From the research

Psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema spent much of her career at Michigan, Stanford and Yale studying rumination. In a series of experiments with colleagues, people who were already feeling low were asked to spend about eight minutes either thinking about their feelings and what they meant, or picturing neutral scenes, such as clouds forming in the sky. Those who ruminated stayed low or felt worse, and later found it harder to solve everyday problems; those who were distracted for a few minutes felt better. Her broader research linked a habit of rumination to longer and deeper low moods.

Worth knowing

The shape of the curve in this lab is an illustrative model, not data from the studies.

Try it this week

When you catch yourself replaying a scene, set a timer for ten minutes and do something that needs your hands or your full attention. Notice how much of the feeling is left afterwards.

In plain words

Going over and over a hurtful moment doesn't solve anything; it just keeps it alive. Notice the loop, write it down once, and give your attention something else to do.

Section 7 · Saying no without guilt

Boundaries Protect Your Peace

In short

If you can't say no, your time and energy belong to whoever asks first. A boundary is a decision about what you will and won't do, said kindly and kept. It isn't an attack on anyone, and other people's disappointment isn't proof that it was wrong.

The big ideas

  • A boundary is about you. "I don't answer work messages after eight" is a boundary. "You must stop messaging me" is a demand. You can only enforce the first.
  • Every yes spends something. Your energy is limited. Saying yes to everything means saying no, quietly, to rest, to your own projects and to the people who matter most.
  • Disappointment is allowed. Someone feeling let down by your no is their feeling to have. It doesn't mean you were unkind.
  • Short and kind beats long and guilty. "I can't take that on this week" works better than a long justification, which invites a negotiation.
A scene

A teacher we'll call Ravi was the person everyone asked: cover a class, mark an extra set of papers, run the weekend club, lend an ear at nine at night. He said yes to all of it and started to dread his phone. When he finally sat down and decided what he would do, he kept two of the extras he enjoyed and dropped the rest, with a short, friendly message for each. One colleague was put out for a week. Most simply asked someone else. He found he had the evenings back, and that he was kinder to the people he still said yes to, because he wasn't quietly resenting them.

Try it this week

Write one boundary as a sentence that starts with "I", such as "I don't take calls during dinner". Tell one person about it this week and keep it.

In plain words

A boundary is a decision about what you'll do, not a rule for others. Say it kindly and briefly, keep it, and let people be disappointed if they are.

Part II · focus on yourself

Focus on Yourself

Taking things less personally frees up a surprising amount of time and energy. The second half asks what you'll do with it. These five sections turn inward: hearing your own voice under the borrowed ones, putting your effort where you have control, keeping promises to yourself, choosing on purpose what your days are for, and building the kind of steadiness that lets life be easier without needing it to be perfect.

The idea behind this part

The energy you stop spending on reactions is yours to invest. Put it into the few things you can actually shape: your choices, your promises and your days.

Section 8 · Trusting yourself over borrowed opinions

Hear Your Own Voice

In short

Once you stop reacting to every outside opinion, it gets quieter, and you can hear your own judgement. Many of the voices in our heads were borrowed: from parents, teachers, critics or social media. Learning which ones are actually yours is the first step in focusing on yourself.

The big ideas

  • Not every thought is yours. "You're not the creative type" may be a teacher's line from years ago. Ask where a thought came from before you obey it.
  • Your voice sounds like values. Your own judgement tends to point toward something you care about. Borrowed voices tend to warn you about what others will think.
  • Quiet helps. Time without input (a walk without headphones, a page of writing) is when your own voice gets loud enough to hear.
  • Trust grows by testing. Follow your judgement on small things and notice how it turns out. Self-trust is built from evidence, not declared.
A scene

Picture someone choosing between two jobs. One is prestigious and her family would be proud. The other is smaller but the work is exactly what she lights up talking about. When she lists her reasons, every reason for the first job starts with what someone else would think: her parents, her old classmates, a recruiter. Every reason for the second starts with "I". She doesn't have to choose the smaller job, but now she knows which voice is which, and whichever she picks, it will be her decision rather than a vote she held on behalf of other people.

Try it this week

Spend ten minutes this week writing, without stopping, about one decision you're facing. Then underline every sentence that is really about what someone else will think.

In plain words

Some of the voices in your head belong to other people. Learn to hear the one that speaks for what you value, and practise trusting it on small things first.

Section 9 · The circle of what's yours

Spend Energy Where You Have Control

In short

Other people's opinions, moods and choices sit outside your control. Your own effort, words and habits sit inside it. A lot of wasted energy comes from pushing on the outside circle while neglecting the inside one.

The big ideas

  • Three rings. Some things you control (your actions, your effort), some you can influence (a team's plans, a friend's mood), and much you can't touch (the weather, other people's opinions).
  • Worry outside the circle is a leak. Energy spent wishing people thought differently changes nothing. The same energy spent on your own work changes something.
  • Influence through the inner circle. You can't make someone respect you, but you can do work that earns it and treat them well. Influence comes from the middle out.
  • Old wisdom, new language. The Stoic Epictetus taught that some things are up to us and some are not, nearly two thousand years ago; Stephen Covey's circles of concern and influence say the same thing for a modern reader.
A scene

Imagine a small business owner after a harsh online review. For two days her energy goes to the reviewer: rereading the post, drafting replies, checking whether anyone else agreed. On the third day she draws three circles on a page. In the outer one she writes the reviewer's opinion and what strangers might think. In the middle, her public reply. In the centre, the two things the review fairly pointed out about her service. She writes one calm reply, fixes the two things, and stops reading the post. The review is still there, but most of her attention is back where it can actually do some good.

Try it this week

List what's worrying you this week and put each item in one of three columns: control, influence, neither. Pick one item from the first column and act on it today.

In plain words

You control your own actions, you can influence some things, and much is out of your hands. Put your energy into the first two and let the rest be.

Section 10 · Self-respect and self-trust

Keep the Promises You Make to Yourself

In short

We work hard to keep promises to other people and break the ones we make to ourselves without a second thought. Each broken one teaches you that your own word doesn't count. Self-respect comes back the same way it left: one kept promise at a time, starting small.

The big ideas

  • You're keeping score. Every time you say "tomorrow I'll start" and don't, part of you notices. Over time you stop believing yourself.
  • Make promises you can keep. A promise to walk for ten minutes that you keep does more for self-trust than a promise to run five miles that you break.
  • Self-respect makes criticism smaller. When you trust yourself, other people's opinions have less to grab on to. You know what you're doing because you have the evidence.
  • Respect includes rest. Keeping promises to yourself also means keeping the ones about sleep, breaks and limits, not only the ones about productivity.
A scene

Think of someone who has promised himself, every Monday for a year, that this is the week he'll get up early and write. Most weeks it lasts a day. Eventually he notices that the failures have started to colour everything: why would anyone take him seriously when he doesn't take himself seriously? He changes one thing. Instead of two hours at five in the morning, he promises fifteen minutes after breakfast, and he does it every day for a month. The writing is modest. The bigger change is that he starts to trust his own word again, and with that, other people's doubts carry less weight.

Worth knowing

An illustrative model: each kept promise adds a little trust, and each broken one takes away a bit more than a kept one adds.

Try it this week

Make one promise to yourself that is almost too small to fail, and keep it every day this week. Tick it off somewhere you'll see.

In plain words

Breaking small promises to yourself slowly teaches you not to trust yourself. Make promises you can keep, keep them, and your self-respect grows back.

Section 11 · Turning reclaimed energy into a life

Live on Purpose

In short

Every hour you no longer spend replaying, defending or checking what others think is an hour you get back. The second half of the book asks you to decide on purpose what that time is for, so it doesn't simply drain into the next distraction.

The big ideas

  • Reactions are expensive. Ten minutes rereading a message, twenty replaying an argument, fifteen checking reactions online: across a year that's hundreds of hours.
  • Decide what matters. Clarity is knowing the few things you want your days to be about. Without that, freed time fills back up with noise.
  • Plan the when and where. "After dinner, at the kitchen table, I'll study for thirty minutes" is far more likely to happen than "I'll study more".
  • Ease comes from direction. When you know what you're building, small slights matter less. You're busy with something that's yours.
From the research

Psychologist Peter Gollwitzer spent years studying the gap between intending to do something and doing it. His answer was the implementation intention, a plan in the form "if situation X happens, then I will do Y". In study after study, people who wrote such plans were more likely to follow through than people who only set the goal, whether the goal was exercise, studying or screening appointments. A 2006 review by Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran of 94 studies found a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals. Deciding the when and where in advance turns good intentions into actual hours.

Try it this week

Write one if-then plan for the time you'd normally spend replaying: "If I notice I'm replaying a conversation, then I'll open my notebook and work on X for ten minutes."

In plain words

Reacting to other people eats far more time than it feels like. Decide what you want that time for and plan exactly when you'll spend it.

Section 12 · Living with ease

Steady, Not Perfect

In short

The book doesn't promise you'll never feel hurt again. It promises steadiness: things will still sting, but they'll tip you less and you'll come back faster. Ease is what life feels like when you aren't fighting every wave.

The big ideas

  • Steadiness is a keel, not a wall. You can't stop the waves. You can build something below the waterline (habits, self-trust, perspective) that rights you after each one.
  • Practice is what deepens it. Every pause, every boundary kept, every time you choose not to replay, adds a little depth. It compounds.
  • Be kind to yourself when you slip. You'll take things personally again. Treating that with understanding rather than more criticism gets you back to calm faster.
  • Ease isn't indifference. You still care about people and outcomes. You just stop handing your peace to whoever speaks loudest.
A scene

Picture two people who get the same blunt message from a client on a Friday afternoon. The first spends the weekend on it: rereading it, drafting replies, telling the story to friends, sleeping badly. The second feels the same jolt, says to herself "that stung", jots down the one fair point in it for Monday and goes for a walk. Nothing about the message was different. The difference was a year of small practice, which had given her a keel: the wave still tipped her, but she came back upright within the hour.

Worth knowing

An illustrative model of the book's idea of steadiness, not a measurement of anything.

Try it this week

The next time you slip and take something personally, say to yourself what you'd say to a friend in the same spot. Then do one small thing that brings you back to your day.

In plain words

You won't stop feeling the waves, and that's fine. With practice you tip less and come back faster, and that steadiness is what makes life feel easier.
