A section-by-section guide to Joseph Nguyen's book

The Overthinker's Guide to Making Decisions,
Explained

You don't overthink because something is wrong with you. You overthink because the choice matters, and somewhere under it sits a fear: of failing, of regret, of letting people down. That is the starting point of The Overthinker's Guide to Making Decisions by Joseph Nguyen, the author of Don't Believe Everything You Think. The book argues that better decisions come less from more analysis and more from trusting yourself again, and it gives you two tools for that: SAGE, four questions that point to the choice that fits the life you want, and TRUST, five steps that walk you out of a spiral and into one small action. This guide covers the book's ideas in twelve sections and four parts, with an illustration and a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

Stop hunting for the right choice and start trusting the one you already sense, then take the smallest step towards it.

Part I · a new way to decide

A New Way to Decide

The book opens by turning the usual advice upside down. Most decision guides tell you to think harder: list the options, weigh the pros and cons, ask around. Nguyen starts from the people for whom that has stopped working, because thinking harder is exactly what they already do. These four sections look at why choice feels so heavy, what is really driving the loop, where your attention goes while you're in it, and why your best decisions probably never came out of a spreadsheet.

The idea behind this part

Overthinking isn't a thinking problem to be solved with more thinking. It's fear dressed up as analysis, and the way out starts with noticing that.

Section 1 · Why deciding feels so heavy

The Silent Weight of Choice

In short

Being able to choose is one of the great freedoms of being human, and also one of its quiet burdens. When there are many options and a pressure to find the one right answer, the mind turns small choices into high stakes, and asking more people for their view often makes it worse, not better.

The big ideas

  • Choice is a gift with a weight. Every option you could take is also a life you won't live. The more open doors there are, the more there is to lose by walking through one, so freedom can feel like pressure.
  • The hunt for the 'right' answer. Most overthinkers believe there's a correct option hidden somewhere and that their job is to find it before choosing. That belief turns every decision into an exam you can fail.
  • Small choices get blown up. A worried mind runs the tape forward: this job leads to that city, which leads to the wrong partner, the wrong life. A choice about dinner can borrow the weight of a choice about a career.
  • Advice as reassurance. Asking around often isn't about information. It's a search for someone to say it'll be fine, or someone to share the blame. Each new opinion adds noise, and your own voice gets harder to hear.
From the research

In a well-known 2000 study, the psychologists Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a tasting table in an upmarket California grocery store. On some days it offered 24 kinds of jam, on others just 6. The big display drew more people in: about 60% of passers-by stopped, against 40% for the small one. But only about 3% of the people who stopped at the big table bought a jar, against roughly 30% at the small one. More choice attracted attention and then stalled the decision. Barry Schwartz later made the idea famous as 'the paradox of choice', and it's a good picture of what Nguyen describes: the more options feel open, and the more each seems to matter, the heavier it becomes to pick one.

Worth knowing

The jam study is famous but its effect is fragile. A 2010 meta-analysis of 50 choice-overload experiments by Benjamin Scheibehenne and colleagues found an average effect close to zero: sometimes more options paralyse people, sometimes they help. Choice overload seems to bite when options are hard to compare, you don't know what you want, and the stakes feel high, which is exactly an overthinker's situation.

Try it this week

Next time you face a small choice (what to eat, which film), cut it to two options before you start thinking, and give yourself sixty seconds.

In plain words

Having lots of choice sounds great, but it makes deciding heavier, especially when you think there's one right answer. Asking more people usually adds noise, not clarity.

Section 2 · Fear wearing the mask of analysis

Why Do We Overthink?

In short

You don't overthink because your mind is broken. You overthink because the decision matters: it touches who you are, your safety or your connection to people. Underneath the loop sits a fear, and arguing with each anxious thought only keeps it going. Naming the fear is what loosens it.

The big ideas

  • It isn't a flaw. Overthinking is a sign you care. Decisions you don't care about don't loop. That reframing alone takes some of the shame out of it, and shame is fuel for more looping.
  • Look for the fear underneath. The thoughts sound like analysis ('but what if the market changes?'), yet the engine is a fear: of failing, of regret, of disappointing someone, of being found out as not enough.
  • Each choice becomes a test of identity. The hidden question is often 'what will this say about me if I get it wrong?' Once a decision feels like a verdict on you, no amount of information feels sufficient.
  • Control is quicksand. Trying to control every outcome, and what everyone will think, is like struggling in quicksand: the harder you thrash, the deeper you sink. Presence and acceptance work better than more effort.
A scene

Maya has been offered a promotion that means managing her former teammates. For two weeks she has been making lists. What if the team resents her? What if the new targets are unrealistic? What if she's worse at managing than at doing? Every time she answers one question, two more appear. One evening a friend asks her a different question: 'What are you actually afraid of?' She's quiet for a while, then says: 'That they'll find out I'm not as good as they think.' The lists stop making sense after that. The decision wasn't really about targets or team dynamics; it was about a fear of being exposed. Seeing it doesn't make the fear vanish, but it stops it posing as a spreadsheet.

Worth knowing

There's good research behind naming a feeling. In a 2007 brain-imaging study, Matthew Lieberman and colleagues at UCLA found that putting a feeling into words ('affect labelling') reduced activity in the amygdala, part of the brain's alarm system. Related work by Susan Nolen-Hoeksema showed that repetitive, passive brooding about problems ('rumination') predicts and prolongs low mood and anxiety. If your loops come with anxiety that doesn't ease, a doctor or counsellor can help.

Try it this week

When a decision starts looping, finish this sentence on paper: 'What I'm really afraid of is that…'. Don't solve it. Just write it down and notice what changes.

In plain words

If you can't stop thinking about a choice, it's probably because you're scared of something. Find and name that fear instead of answering every worried thought one by one.

Section 3 · Whatever you focus on grows

Feed the Right Wolf

In short

An old story says two wolves live inside you, one of fear and doubt and one of hope and confidence, and the one that wins is the one you feed. Nguyen adds a twist: fighting fear is still feeding it, because resistance is attention too. Decisions made while staring at the danger tend to steer you into it.

The big ideas

  • Attention is food. Whatever you keep your mind on gets bigger and more vivid. Rehearsing the worst case doesn't protect you from it; it makes it the most real thing in the room.
  • Resisting is feeding. Telling yourself 'don't think about failing' still puts failing at the centre. The way out isn't to fight the fear wolf but to turn and feed the other one.
  • Look where you want to go. Skiers and mountain bikers learn to look at the gap between the trees, not at the trees, because you drift towards what you stare at. Decisions work the same way.
  • Your mind finds what it looks for. Confirmation bias means a fearful mind collects evidence for its fear and overlooks the rest. Widening your view to include what could go right is not naive; it corrects the bias.
From the book

Nguyen retells the parable of the two wolves: an elder tells a child that a fight goes on inside every person between a wolf of fear, anger and doubt and a wolf of joy, hope and confidence. 'Which one wins?' asks the child. 'The one you feed.' Nguyen's addition is that you can't win by starving the fear wolf through force of will, because every time you push it away you're looking at it. He pairs it with a picture from skiing: a beginner who fixes their eyes on the tree they're scared of hitting tends to ski straight into it, while a skier who looks at the open line between the trees glides through. The fear is real in both cases. What changes is where the eyes go.

Worth knowing

The two wolves story is usually called a Cherokee legend, but there's no good evidence it is one. Versions of it appear in American Christian writing in the 1970s (Billy Graham told it with two dogs), and the 'Cherokee' label was attached later. It still works as a parable; it's just not a Cherokee teaching.

Try it this week

For one week, each time you catch yourself rehearsing what could go wrong with a decision, spend the same number of breaths picturing what it would look like if it went well.

In plain words

What you focus on grows. If you spend all your time on what could go wrong, even to fight it, that's what fills your head. Look where you want to go instead.

Section 4 · How your best decisions were really made

Intuition, Not Spreadsheets

In short

Think of the best big decision you've made. It probably came from a felt sense of yes, not a list of pros and cons. Nguyen argues that intuition and inner alignment, not logic or approval, lead to the choices that change a life. Information still matters, but only until it brings clarity; after that, more research just breeds doubt.

The big ideas

  • Your best choices weren't calculated. Moving abroad, leaving a relationship, starting something: the choices that changed people's lives usually came from hope and a sense of rightness, then the reasoning came after.
  • The conditioned self and the real you. The conditioned self wants safety and to fit in, so it votes for whatever keeps things the same. The real you treats growth as the point. Overthinking is often the conditioned self stalling.
  • Contract or expand? One test cuts through a lot: does this choice make me smaller, or expand who I'm becoming? You can usually feel the answer in your body before you can argue it.
  • Gather facts until they clarify. Look things up, check the numbers, ask the one person who knows. But notice the moment more research stops adding clarity and starts adding doubt. That's the moment to stop and decide.
A scene

Ravi spent four months choosing between two master's programmes. He built a spreadsheet with twenty-two weighted criteria, from rankings to rent to the weather. Every time one programme pulled ahead, he adjusted a weight, and the other caught up. One evening his sister asked him to imagine an email arriving saying he'd been accepted to one and the other had closed. 'Which one do you hope it says?' He answered before she had finished the sentence. The spreadsheet hadn't been helping him decide; it had been helping him avoid an answer he already had, because choosing the smaller, less famous programme felt like it needed a justification. Once he saw that, he accepted the offer in an afternoon.

Worth knowing

Psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein, who disagreed about a lot, agreed in a 2009 paper that intuition is trustworthy where the world is regular and gives fast feedback (chess, firefighting, nursing) and unreliable for predictions where it doesn't, such as stock picking. Nguyen's 'intuition' is mostly about values: which life fits you. That's a question your gut is well placed to answer; whether a specific investment will pay off is not.

Try it this week

Pick one decision you've been researching. Write down what single fact, if you learned it, would change your mind. If you can't name one, you've finished researching.

In plain words

Your best decisions probably came from a gut sense, not a list. Gather facts until they make things clearer, then stop, because past that point more research only makes you doubt.

Part II · the fears that keep you stuck

The Fears That Keep You Stuck

Two fears sit under most stuck decisions. One is the fear of choosing wrong: of regret, of failure, of a door closing forever. The other is the fear of upsetting someone: a parent, a partner, a boss, the people who'll have opinions. Nguyen takes each in turn, with an old parable for the first and a hard look at approval for the second.

The idea behind this part

Neither outcome nor approval can be known in advance, so neither can be the test of a good decision.

Section 5 · The fear of making the wrong decision

We'll See

In short

The first great fear is choosing wrong. Nguyen answers it with an old parable: a farmer whose neighbours keep calling his luck good or bad while he only says 'we'll see'. Events are too connected to judge in the moment, meaning comes from how you respond, and no single decision locks you into a path for good.

The big ideas

  • Verdicts come too early. We grade decisions the week after we make them. But consequences keep unfolding for years, and the thing that looked like a mistake often turns into the reason for something good.
  • Events are neutral; meaning is made. A lost job, a failed move, a rejected application: what it means depends a lot on what you do next. That puts more of the outcome in your hands than the decision itself ever did.
  • Destruction can enrich. Nguyen compares some decisions to volcanic eruptions: frightening and destructive in the short term, yet the ash leaves some of the most fertile soil on earth.
  • No decision is a life sentence. Almost every path can be changed later. Seeing a choice as one step in a long story, not a final verdict, takes most of the terror out of it. (The book adds one boundary: this isn't a licence to do harm.)
From the book

In the parable, a farmer's only horse runs away. 'What bad luck,' say the neighbours. 'Maybe. We'll see,' he says. A few days later the horse comes back, leading several wild horses. 'What good luck!' 'We'll see.' His son tries to tame one, is thrown, and breaks his leg. 'How awful.' 'We'll see.' Then soldiers arrive to take every able young man to war, and pass over the son with the broken leg. The story could go on forever, which is the point: at every step, the neighbours' verdict was premature. Nguyen uses it to loosen the grip of 'what if it's the wrong choice?', because 'wrong' is a judgment you can only make from a vantage point you never actually reach.

Worth knowing

The story is very old. It comes from the Huainanzi, a Chinese collection compiled around 139 BC, as the tale of the old man at the frontier who lost his horse (sai weng shi ma), and it's still a Chinese proverb for misfortune that may turn out to be a blessing. It's often retold as a Taoist or Zen story.

Try it this week

Think of a decision from five years ago you once called a mistake. Write down one thing in your life now that wouldn't exist without it.

In plain words

You can't know if a choice was good or bad until much later, and even then the story keeps going. So stop trying to get it perfect; what you do next matters more.

Section 6 · The fear of upsetting other people

Whose Life Is It?

In short

The second great fear is letting people down. Nguyen argues that someone's disapproval doesn't prove your choice is wrong; it may mean you're finally honouring yourself. Much advice reflects the adviser's fears, guilt is often old conditioning, and the people who love you without conditions want you alive more than compliant.

The big ideas

  • Disapproval isn't evidence. Someone being upset tells you about their hopes and fears. It doesn't, on its own, tell you whether your choice is right for your life.
  • Advice is autobiography. Most advice is the adviser's own history and fears, offered with love. Hear it, thank them, and remember it was shaped by a life that isn't yours.
  • Good isn't the same as keeping the peace. Many of us were taught that being good means not upsetting anyone. Guilt that fires when you choose for yourself is often that old rule, not a sign of wrongdoing.
  • Conditional and unconditional love. Conditional love asks you to shrink to stay loved. Unconditional love wants you to choose what makes you come alive. Ask honestly which kind each voice in your head comes from.
A scene

Daniel trained as an accountant because his parents, who had worked two jobs each to get him through university, were so proud of the idea. Ten years in, he wants to retrain as a primary school teacher. He has rehearsed the conversation with his father a hundred times, and in every version his father looks crushed. When he finally tells him, his father is quiet, then angry: 'After everything we gave up?' Three weeks later, his father calls back and says something Daniel didn't expect: that he had wanted to be a mechanic, and had never let himself. Nguyen makes this point directly: other people's anger at our choices can be grief for the self they had to give up. It isn't always, but it's worth remembering that their reaction is about their story, too.

Worth knowing

The book isn't saying other people's views don't matter. Choices affect families, partners and children, and listening well is part of deciding well. The point is narrower: people's comfort with your decision shouldn't be the test of whether it's right, and you can respect their feelings without being ruled by them.

Try it this week

Before you ask anyone else about a decision this week, write down what you'd choose if no one would ever find out. Keep it, then compare it with the advice you get.

In plain words

People being upset by your choice doesn't make it wrong. Their advice comes from their own lives and fears. Listen, but make sure the final voice is yours.

Part III · the actualized decision

The Actualized Decision

Here the book gets practical. First it changes the question: instead of 'what is the right choice?', ask 'what life do I want to build with this choice?' The SAGE compass gives four ways to answer. Then TRUST turns that into a routine you can run in a few minutes whenever a decision starts to loop: take five deep breaths, reveal the root decision, uncover the fear and its cost, shift from fear to intuition, and take the smallest possible action. These four sections walk through SAGE and then the five steps.

The idea behind this part

A good decision is one made from trust, alignment and love rather than fear, and it becomes real the moment you take one small step.

Section 7 · The SAGE compass

The Actualized Decision

In short

An actualized decision is one made from self-trust, alignment, presence and love, not from fear or the need for approval. Borrowing Maslow's idea of self-actualization, Nguyen replaces 'what's the right choice?' with 'what life do I want to build with this choice?', and offers four questions to answer it: serenity, alignment, growth and emotion.

The big ideas

  • Change the question. 'Right or wrong' assumes there's an answer key. 'What do I want to build?' doesn't, and it's a question only you can answer, which is exactly why it frees you from hunting for approval.
  • S for serenity, A for alignment. Which option brings the deepest, longest-lasting peace, not just relief tonight? And which fits the person you want to become rather than the one you've been?
  • G for growth, E for emotion. Which choice expands you most? And is it driven by love, curiosity and abundance, or by fear, scarcity and avoiding blame?
  • Listen to the body. Nguyen suggests noticing what happens physically when you imagine each option. Fear tends to tighten the chest and stomach; an aligned choice tends to feel like softening, even when it's also scary.
A scene

Lena has two offers. One is a well-paid role at a large bank, the obvious next step on her CV. The other is a smaller climate start-up, less pay, more risk. Her lists come out almost even. So she runs SAGE. Serenity: the bank wins on short-term calm, but when she imagines five years there, the peace curdles into restlessness. Alignment: the start-up fits the person she keeps describing to friends. Growth: no contest, the start-up. Emotion: when she's honest, the bank is the choice she'd make to avoid her parents' worry, the start-up the one she'd make out of love for the work. She takes the start-up, and she also negotiates a six-month review, because self-trust doesn't mean ignoring risk.

Worth knowing

Maslow's ideas are usually shown as a pyramid, but he never drew one; the pyramid was added by later writers, and his own view was more fluid. Self-actualization, for him, was about becoming more fully who you are, which is the sense the book uses.

Try it this week

Take a decision you're stuck on and write S, A, G and E down the side of a page. Answer each in one line for each option. Notice which answer came fastest.

In plain words

Stop asking which choice is 'right'. Ask which brings real peace, fits who you want to be, helps you grow, and comes from love instead of fear.

Section 8 · Steps one and two

TRUST: Breathe, Then Reveal the Root

In short

TRUST starts with the body. Take five deep breaths, with a long slow exhale, to move from alarm to clarity. Then reveal the root decision: write what you're actually deciding in one plain sentence, with no outcomes or what-ifs attached. Most tangled decisions turn out to be one simple question underneath.

The big ideas

  • T: take five deep breaths. A racing mind can't decide well because the body thinks it's in danger. Five slow breaths, breathing out for longer than you breathe in, tell your nervous system it can stand down.
  • Calm first, think second. This isn't a relaxation tip bolted on. It's the first step for a reason: every later step needs a mind that isn't in fight-or-flight.
  • R: reveal the root decision. Write the decision as one sentence. 'Do I stay in this job or leave?' not 'Should I leave if the market turns and my manager changes and…'.
  • Strip out the outcomes. The root sentence has no predictions in it. The predictions are where the fear lives; you'll meet them in the next step, on purpose, rather than letting them blur the question.
From the research

Slow breathing has some good evidence behind it. In a 2023 study at Stanford, Melis Yilmaz Balban, Andrew Huberman, David Spiegel and colleagues asked about 110 people to spend five minutes a day for a month on one of three breathing exercises or on mindfulness meditation. The group doing 'cyclic sighing', two inhales then a long, slow exhale, reported the biggest improvement in mood and the largest drop in breathing rate at rest. The study was small, and the differences between the techniques were modest, but it fits a long line of research: lengthening the exhale slows the heart and nudges the body out of alarm. That's the physical ground TRUST stands on.

Worth knowing

Nguyen's suggestion is roughly four seconds in and eight out. Any slow pattern with a longer out-breath works; if long holds or deep breaths make you dizzy, shorten them.

Try it this week

Set a reminder for the next time you feel a decision tightening your chest. When it comes, do five breaths with a long out-breath before you think anything else.

In plain words

Before you think, breathe: five slow breaths with long exhales. Then write what you're really deciding in one short sentence, with no what-ifs.

Section 9 · Steps three and four

TRUST: Uncover the Fear, Then Shift

In short

Step three is to uncover the fear and its cost: name what you're afraid of, what it says about you, and what listening to it has already cost you. Step four is to shift from fear to intuition: run the options through SAGE and ask what you'd choose if every doubt vanished. Write that down as your actualized decision.

The big ideas

  • U: name the fear. Ask 'what am I afraid will happen?' and then 'what would that say about me?' The second answer is usually the real one, and it's usually older than this decision.
  • Then count its cost. Fear keeps quiet about its own price. What has obeying it already cost you: months, opportunities, energy, the version of you that would have tried? Staying put is a choice too, and it has a bill.
  • S: shift to intuition. With the fear named, ask the SAGE questions, then the simplest one: if all doubt disappeared right now, what would I choose? Most people know within seconds.
  • Write the actualized decision. Put that answer on paper as a sentence that starts 'I choose…'. Writing it turns a feeling into a commitment you can act on in the last step.
A scene

Sam has wanted to move out of the city for three years. The root decision is simple: 'Do we move to the coast or not?' When he uncovers the fear, it's 'we'll hate it and have wasted everything', and under that, 'I'll prove I can't make good calls'. Then he counts the cost of listening to that fear: three summers of saying 'next year', a growing rent bill, a partner whose patience is thinning, weekends spent browsing listings instead of living. Seen next to each other, the fear of a bad move looks smaller than the fact of a frozen life. When he asks what he'd choose if the doubt vanished, the answer is instant. He writes 'I choose to try a year on the coast'. It isn't certainty, just a direction.

Worth knowing

Counting the cost of inaction isn't only Nguyen's idea. Research by Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec in the 1990s found that over the long run people regret things they didn't do more than things they did; in the short term it's the reverse. The fear in the moment is about acting; the regret years later is usually about not acting.

Try it this week

Write down one decision you've been putting off and, next to it, one concrete thing waiting has cost you so far. Look at that line before you look at the fear.

In plain words

Say what you're scared of, and be honest about what being scared has already cost you. Then ask what you'd do if the doubt vanished, and write that down.

Section 10 · Step five

TRUST: Take the Smallest Possible Action

In short

The last step is the one that ends paralysis: take the smallest possible action towards your actualized decision, today. Not the whole plan, just one step small enough that fear doesn't bother to object. Movement creates clarity that thinking can't, and each step makes the next one easier.

The big ideas

  • Action dissolves paralysis. You can't think your way out of a loop that thinking created. A small physical step changes the situation, and a changed situation gives you new information.
  • Make it smaller than feels useful. Update one line of your CV. Write one sentence of the email. Look up one train time. If the step still feels heavy, it's not small enough yet.
  • Today, not someday. The point of the smallest action is that it can happen now. A step you schedule for next month is a plan, and plans are where overthinkers like to hide.
  • Steps build trust. Each completed step is a promise to yourself kept. That's the raw material of self-trust, which the last part of the book is about.
A scene

Priya decided months ago that she wants to start a small ceramics business, and nothing has happened since. Her to-do list starts with 'write a business plan', which is enormous, so she never begins. She shrinks it. 'Write a business plan' becomes 'decide what I'll sell first', which becomes 'list three pieces I'd be proud to sell', which becomes 'take a photo of the bowl on my shelf'. That last one takes thirty seconds. She does it on the spot, and because the photo is good she posts it, and a friend asks if she can buy one. None of that was in the plan. It came from a step so small her fear didn't notice it.

Worth knowing

This idea has support beyond the book. BJ Fogg's 'Tiny Habits' research at Stanford and Teresa Amabile's 'progress principle' (from studying nearly 12,000 diary entries by knowledge workers) both found that small, visible steps build motivation better than big intentions. Amabile found that making progress in meaningful work was the single biggest boost to people's inner work life.

Try it this week

Look at the decision you wrote down in the last lab. Shrink its first step until it takes under two minutes, and do it before you close this page.

In plain words

Don't wait to feel sure. Take one tiny step today towards what you've chosen. Moving makes things clearer than thinking does.

Part IV · living it

Living It

The last part of the book is a workbook. Nguyen says information alone doesn't change a life; practice does. So he closes with 31 short decision-making principles, more than seventy journaling prompts that help you see yourself as a decision-maker, templates for running TRUST, and twenty 'mini decision-making experiments': tiny, low-stakes choices made on purpose to rebuild the habit of trusting yourself.

The idea behind this part

Self-trust is a muscle built from evidence: many small choices, made from your gut, that you then live with.

Section 11 · Principles, prompts and practice

Know Yourself as a Decision-Maker

In short

Nguyen's closing argument is that information alone doesn't change you; integration does. So the last part of the book is practical: 31 short decision-making principles grouped by theme, journaling prompts to discover your patterns and reinvent how you decide, and templates for running TRUST again and again until it's a habit.

The big ideas

  • 31 principles to reread. Short statements grouped under fear and protection, intuition and inner knowing, growth, alignment and choice, and self-trust and emotional freedom. They're meant to be returned to when you're stuck, not read once.
  • Discover your patterns. Self-discovery prompts ask where you hesitate, what role fear plays, whose voices shaped you, and what overthinking has cost you. Seeing the pattern is half of breaking it.
  • Reinvent how you decide. Self-reinvention prompts ask you to reconnect with your inner knowing, let go of old beliefs, rebuild trust, and describe your 'actualized self', the person who decides from trust.
  • Tell intuition from fear. The most useful skill in the whole book: learning to hear the difference between the quiet, steady voice of intuition and the loud, urgent voice of fear.
From the book

The book suggests a rhythm for this part rather than a one-off read. Review the principles and run a TRUST exercise whenever overthinking shows up. Answer one discovery prompt a day, in writing, so you see your own patterns in your own words. And try one of the mini decision-making experiments each week (the next section). Over a few weeks, Nguyen says, you'll notice the gap between a decision appearing and the loop starting getting longer, and the gap between a decision and a first step getting shorter. That's what learning to trust yourself looks like from the inside.

Worth knowing

A rough guide to telling them apart, drawn from the book and common to many traditions: fear tends to be loud, urgent, repetitive and full of 'what if'; it narrows your options and tightens your body. Intuition tends to be quiet, calm, brief and often surprisingly specific; it stays the same when you come back to it a day later.

Try it this week

Answer one prompt in writing today: 'When in my life did I trust myself, and what happened?' Keep it short, and keep it somewhere you'll see it again.

In plain words

The end of the book is a workbook. Reread the principles, answer the prompts, and practise TRUST until hearing the difference between fear and intuition becomes second nature.

Section 12 · Mini decision-making experiments

Tiny Acts of Self-Trust

In short

Self-trust grows from evidence, not affirmations. The book ends with twenty low-stakes experiments that give you that evidence: flip a coin and notice which side you hope for, order the first dish that appeals to you, decide with 80% of the information, choose without reading reviews, take a walk with no destination. Small choices, made from your gut and lived with, build the muscle for big ones.

The big ideas

  • Evidence beats affirmation. Telling yourself 'I trust myself' doesn't work if your record says otherwise. Each small decision you make and survive changes the record.
  • The coin flip. Assign two options to heads and tails and flip. Before you look, notice which side you're hoping for. The flip doesn't decide; it reveals what you already wanted.
  • Practise on low stakes. Order the first dish that appeals. Choose a film without checking ratings. Decide something at 80% information. These are the reps that make big decisions feel lighter.
  • Design your own. The book invites you to invent experiments that fit your life, and to try one a week. Honouring a small preference, stating a need, saying a guilt-free no: each one counts.
A scene

Ana tried the coin flip on a decision she'd been chewing for a week: whether to go to a friend's wedding abroad or save the money. Heads, go; tails, stay. The coin landed tails. Her first feeling was a little drop in her stomach, disappointment, and then she laughed, because that was the answer. She booked the flight. Over the next month she tried other small experiments: ordering without reading the whole menu, picking a book by its first page. None of them mattered much, which was the point. By the time a big decision came, whether to accept a transfer, she noticed she was faster to hear herself, and calmer once she had.

Worth knowing

The coin trick has some research behind it. In a 2019 study, Mariela Jaffé and colleagues at the University of Basel found that flipping a coin helped people notice their own preference, even though they were free to ignore the result. And economist Steven Levitt found in a large coin-flip experiment that people who were told by the coin to make a big change (like quitting a job) reported being happier six months later. Both are single studies, so treat them as encouraging, not proof.

Try it this week

Pick one tiny act from the lab and do it this week. When it's done, add a pebble, and notice that nothing bad happened because you trusted yourself.

In plain words

You learn to trust yourself the same way you learn to trust anyone: by watching yourself keep your word on small things. Start with tiny, low-stakes choices made from your gut.

Your TRUST sheet

The whole book as one habit: when a decision starts to loop, breathe, say what you're really deciding, name the fear and what it has cost you, ask what you'd choose if the doubt vanished, and take the smallest step today.
