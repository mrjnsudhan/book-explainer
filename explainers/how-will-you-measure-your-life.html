A chapter-by-chapter guide to the book by Clayton M. Christensen, James Allworth and Karen Dillon

How Will You Measure Your Life?,
Explained

Clayton Christensen was the Harvard Business School professor behind the theory of disruptive innovation. On the last day of his course he asked students to point the same business theories at their own lives, and in 2010, while being treated for cancer, he turned that class into a famous talk and an essay. How Will You Measure Your Life? is the book that came out of it: what motivates us, why our strategy is where our time goes, how to invest in the people we love, and how good people end up in jail. This guide takes it chapter by chapter, with a lab in each where you can try the idea.

The book in one line

The theories that explain why good companies fail can help you build a life you'll be proud of, measured by the people you helped.

Introduction · Just because you have feet

Theories, Not Answers

The book grew out of the last class Clayton Christensen taught each year at Harvard Business School. After a term of business theory, he asked his students to turn the same theories on their own lives, and to answer three questions before they left.

The idea behind this part

Good theories don't tell you what to think. They tell you how to think, so you can work out the answer to a problem nobody has handed you yet.

Introduction · Three questions, and how to think about them

Just Because You Have Feet

In short

On the last day of his course, Christensen asked his students three questions. How can I be sure I'll be happy in my career? How can I be sure my relationships with my family become a lasting source of happiness? How can I be sure I'll stay out of jail? The book doesn't give answers. It gives theories, tested in business, that help you work out your own.

The big ideas

  • Theories predict. A good theory explains what causes what, and under which circumstances. That lets you see what is likely to happen before it happens.
  • How to think, not what to think. Advice tells you what to do in one situation. A theory lets you reason about situations no one has seen before.
  • The three questions. Career happiness, lasting relationships and integrity. The book is organised around them.
  • Your classmates are the warning. Christensen watched bright, well-meaning people from his own Harvard class end up unhappy at work, divorced or estranged from their children, and a few in prison. None of them had planned it.
From the book

Andy Grove, then the head of Intel, read Christensen's early research on disruption and invited him to explain it to his team. Grove soon interrupted: he didn't have time for the academic part, just tell him what it meant for Intel. Christensen refused, politely. He walked them through how small steel minimills had started with the cheapest product, reinforcing bar, then climbed market by market until the big integrated mills had nothing left. Grove saw it for himself and said what it meant: Intel had to go to the bottom of its own market. That became the low-cost Celeron chip. Christensen hadn't told Grove what to think. He had given him a way to think, and Grove reached the answer himself.

Try it this week

Write down the three questions and answer each one in a sentence, as honestly as you can. Keep the page; you'll come back to it at the end.

In plain words

The book borrows business theories to help you think about your own life. It won't hand you answers, but it will help you ask better questions about your work, your family and your integrity.

Section I · Finding happiness in your career

Finding Happiness in Your Career

The first three chapters are about work: what actually motivates people, how to balance a plan against the opportunities that turn up, and why your real strategy is wherever your time and money go.

The idea behind this part

Find work that gives you meaning, not just pay. Hold your plan loosely until you find it, then put your resources behind it.

Chapter 1 · Hygiene factors and motivators

What Makes Us Tick

In short

Following the psychologist Frederick Herzberg, Christensen argues that satisfaction and dissatisfaction aren't two ends of one scale. Pay, status and working conditions can make you unhappy if they're bad, but getting them right only stops the unhappiness. What actually makes work fulfilling is something else: challenge, responsibility, recognition and the chance to grow.

The big ideas

  • Two separate scales. The opposite of job satisfaction isn't dissatisfaction. It's no satisfaction. You can be not unhappy and still unfulfilled.
  • Hygiene factors. Pay, status, job security, working conditions, company policy and how you're supervised. Get them wrong and people are miserable. Get them right and people stop complaining, nothing more.
  • Motivators. Challenging work, recognition for real achievement, responsibility, and personal growth. These are what make people love their work.
  • Money is a trap when it's the point. Choosing a job mainly for pay, planning to do the meaningful thing later, is one of the most common ways careers go wrong.
From the book

Christensen describes why he came to think management, done well, is one of the most noble jobs there is. Picture someone coming home after a day at work. If their manager gave them a real challenge, trusted them with responsibility and noticed what they achieved, they walk in with more self-respect, and that changes how they treat their family that evening. If the day left them feeling ignored and small, that comes home too. Pay doesn't change which evening it is. The motivators do.

Try it this week

List what you'd change about your work. Mark each item H for hygiene or M for motivator. If your list is all H, ask what would make the work itself more meaningful.

In plain words

Fair pay and good conditions stop you being unhappy at work, but they won't make you happy. For that you need work that challenges you, trusts you and lets you grow.

Chapter 2 · Deliberate and emergent strategy

The Balance of Plans and Opportunity

In short

Every strategy is a mix of two things: the deliberate plan you make, and the emergent opportunities and problems you didn't foresee. Early in a career, when you don't yet know what work will fulfil you, stay open to the emergent. Once you find it, switch to a deliberate strategy and commit.

The big ideas

  • Deliberate strategy. The plan you make on purpose, based on what you believe you want and what you think will work.
  • Emergent strategy. What turns up that you didn't plan: a surprise opening, a problem, a door you didn't know existed. It often turns out better than the plan.
  • Know which to use when. If you haven't found work that motivates you, experiment and follow what works. Once you have, focus and push.
  • Ask what has to prove true. Before you commit to a path, list the assumptions that must be right for it to work, and test the shakiest ones first.
From the book

When Honda came to the United States in 1959, the plan was to sell big motorcycles against Harley-Davidson. It went badly: the bikes weren't built for long American highway rides, and they leaked oil and broke down. Meanwhile, Honda's own staff in Los Angeles were riding small 50cc Super Cubs around town on errands, and people kept asking where they could buy one. Honda's leaders were reluctant, since the small bike didn't fit their plan, but in the end they followed the demand. The Super Cub opened up a whole new market of people who had never owned a motorcycle, and it made Honda's name in America.

Try it this week

Write down the path you're on now and three things that have to prove true for it to make you happy. Which one could you test this month?

In plain words

Make a plan, but keep your eyes open for the opportunities you didn't plan for. Until you find work you love, be willing to change direction. Once you find it, commit.

Chapter 3 · Strategy is how you allocate resources

Your Strategy Is Not What You Say It Is

In short

A company's real strategy isn't in its mission statement. It's in thousands of daily decisions about where money, time and people go. The same is true of you. Your life strategy is how you spend your time, energy and talent, and high achievers tend to pour them into whatever gives the quickest visible reward.

The big ideas

  • Strategy is what you fund. Whatever you say your priorities are, your strategy is wherever your resources actually go, day after day.
  • The pull of the immediate. Closing a deal, shipping a product or getting a promotion pays off this quarter. Raising a child or building a marriage pays off years later, so it loses the daily fight for your time.
  • Resources are more than money. Time, energy, attention and talent are your scarcest resources, and they're the ones families need most.
  • Decide on purpose. If you don't allocate deliberately, the loudest short-term demand will decide for you.
From the book

Christensen points out that at Intel, the shift from memory chips to microprocessors didn't start with a strategy meeting. Middle managers, ranking orders by profit margin, kept giving factory capacity to microprocessors because they paid better. By the time the leaders officially changed strategy, the company had already moved. Christensen then turns it on his classmates. None of them planned to neglect their families, but day after day, they allocated their spare hour to whatever showed results by Friday. Over years, those small allocations added up to a strategy they would never have chosen.

Try it this week

Track one week of your evenings and weekends in half-hour blocks. Then compare it with the list of what you say matters most.

In plain words

You don't have a strategy because you wrote one down. You have one because of where your time and energy go every day. Check that it matches what you care about.

Section II · Finding happiness in your relationships

Finding Happiness in Your Relationships

The longest section turns business ideas on family and friendship: invest before you need to, understand the job the people you love need doing, don't outsource raising your children, let them learn from hard experiences, and build a family culture on purpose.

The idea behind this part

Relationships with family and close friends are the deepest source of happiness, and they pay back only if you invest in them long before you need them.

Chapter 4 · Good money and bad money

The Ticking Clock

In short

In business, Christensen says, new ventures need money that is patient for growth but impatient for profit. Relationships are similar but harsher: you have to invest in them long before you need them, because by the time a problem shows, it's often too late to start.

The big ideas

  • Good money and bad money. Investors who want quick, huge growth push a new venture into a big bet too early. Money that is patient for growth but wants early profit makes it find a strategy that works.
  • Relationships compound slowly. The return on time spent with family and friends arrives years later, often all at once, in a crisis or in your children's teens.
  • The clock is running. Children grow up on their schedule, not yours. The years when they most want your time are the years your career wants it most.
  • You can't catch up later. If you wait until the relationship needs attention, the investment you didn't make can't be made in a hurry.
From the book

The chapter is about the trap of 'later'. Christensen describes high-achieving people telling themselves they'll invest in their families once their careers are secure: after the next promotion, after the deal closes, after the kids are older and more interesting. By the time they look up, the window has passed. Teenagers have other places to turn, and a spouse has learned to live a separate life. The investments that would have paid off now had to be made years before, when nothing seemed urgent.

Try it this week

Name one relationship you've been planning to invest in later. Put one hour for it in your calendar this week, before anything else gets the slot.

In plain words

Invest in the people you love now, not once you have time. Relationships pay back slowly, and you can't make up the investment when you suddenly need it.

Chapter 5 · Jobs to be done, at home

What Job Did You Hire That Milkshake For?

In short

Customers don't buy products, they hire them to do a job. Christensen's famous example is a milkshake bought by morning commuters to make a long drive less boring. In a marriage or friendship, the question is the same: what job does the other person need done? It's often not the one you'd guess.

The big ideas

  • Hire, don't buy. People bring a product into their lives to get a job done. Understand the job and you understand what to offer.
  • Same product, different jobs. The morning commuter and the parent buying a treat in the afternoon want different things from the same milkshake.
  • Ask what job your partner needs done. Not what you'd want in their place, but what they actually need, which takes attention and empathy to see.
  • Sacrifice deepens commitment. The more you give for someone, the more you value the relationship. Doing the job well often means putting your own priorities second.
From the book

A fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes and asked customers how to improve them. Nothing changed. Christensen's colleagues tried something else: they watched who bought milkshakes and when. A surprising share were bought early in the morning by people alone, heading for a long commute. They were hiring the milkshake to make a boring drive interesting and keep them full until lunch, and it beat bagels and bananas because it lasted and fitted in one hand. Christensen then tells of a man who came home to find his wife exhausted after a long day with the children, so he cleaned up the kitchen. She was still upset. He'd done a job, but not hers: what she needed that evening was for him to listen to her day.

Try it this week

Ask someone you love: "What would make this week easier for you?" Then do that, rather than what you'd planned to do for them.

In plain words

Don't assume you know what the people you love need. Work out the job they're trying to get done, and do that job well, even when it's not the one you'd have picked.

Chapter 6 · What happens when you outsource

Sailing Your Children on Theseus's Ship

In short

Dell handed its supplier ASUS one piece of its business after another, and each step looked good on the numbers. At the end, ASUS could make the whole computer and launched its own brand. Christensen warns parents about a similar trap: outsource too much of your children's growing up, and the values and skills they pick up come from someone else.

The big ideas

  • Theseus's ship. If you replace every plank of a ship one at a time, is it still the same ship? Outsourcing works the same way: piece by piece, the capability moves elsewhere.
  • Capabilities are three things. Resources (what you have), processes (how you do things) and priorities (what you choose). You can buy resources. Processes and priorities have to be built.
  • Activities aren't capabilities. Lessons, camps and tutors can give children skills, but the way they handle hard problems comes from the people who are around when the problems happen.
  • Be there for the learning. Your children learn your values when you face difficulties together, not when you've arranged for someone else to be there.
From the book

Dell built its business by assembling computers fast and cheaply. Its Taiwanese supplier, ASUSTeK, offered to make the simple circuit boards for less, and Dell agreed: its numbers improved. Then ASUS offered to make the motherboards, then assemble the whole computer, then manage the supply chain, then design the machine. Each step made Dell's return on assets look better. Then ASUS announced it would sell computers under its own name. Every individual decision made sense, and together they had handed away the heart of the business.

Try it this week

Look at your children's week, or a younger person you mentor. Where are they facing something hard? Make sure you're there for one of those moments.

In plain words

Each thing you hand off can look sensible on its own. Add them up and you may have handed away what mattered most. With children, be there for the hard parts.

Chapter 7 · Courses, not credentials

The Schools of Experience

In short

Christensen learned, painfully, that an impressive résumé doesn't mean someone can do a particular job. What matters is whether they've taken the right 'courses' in the school of experience: whether they've faced the specific problems this job will throw at them. Children need the same: hard, real problems to work through while you're there.

The big ideas

  • Right stuff isn't enough. Hiring for impressive attributes, like a big-company title, predicts poorly for jobs that need different experience.
  • Schools of experience. The management researcher Morgan McCall argues that people build ability by working through specific challenges. Ask which courses a candidate has taken.
  • Hard problems build children. If children only ever do easy things, or you solve their problems for them, they miss the courses they'll need later.
  • Let them struggle, with you near. Look for real challenges your children can take on, and be there to help them learn from it, rather than to take it over.
From the book

When Christensen co-founded his materials company, he and his partners hired senior executives with glittering résumés from big, established firms. Several of them struggled. They were capable people, but they had always worked with the resources and systems of a large company behind them. Nobody had ever asked them to build a new plant from nothing or sell a product no one had heard of. They hadn't taken those courses. Christensen came to see that the better question was not "What has this person achieved?" but "Which problems have they already wrestled with?"

Try it this week

Think of the next hard thing your child, or someone you mentor, will face. Instead of removing it, ask how you can help them get through it themselves.

In plain words

To judge whether someone is ready for a job, ask what problems they've already handled, not how impressive their résumé is. Give your children the chance to take those hard courses too.

Next: Family culture →
Chapter 8 · Culture is how you've solved problems before

The Invisible Hand Inside Your Family

In short

Culture, in the management researcher Edgar Schein's sense, is the way a group has learned to solve problems, repeated so often that people stop thinking about it. Every family has one. It forms whether you shape it or not, so decide what you want it to be, and solve problems that way, together, again and again.

The big ideas

  • Culture is learned. When a group solves a problem one way and it works, it does the same next time. Soon nobody thinks to do it differently.
  • It forms on its own. If you don't shape your family's culture deliberately, the habits of the moment will shape it for you.
  • Pick the right tool. How much people agree on what they want, and on how to get it, decides whether you need power, leadership, management or culture to get them to cooperate.
  • Repeat it on purpose. To build a culture of, say, working hard or being kind, give children the chance to solve problems that way, and to see it work.
From the book

Christensen uses a business idea to explain family life. When the people in a group disagree on both what they want and how to get it, only power works: rules, rewards and punishments. When they agree on what they want but not how, a leader with a vision helps. When they agree on how but not why, management tools such as training and procedures help. When they agree on both, culture does the work and no one needs to be told. He uses the matrix to show why a family culture is worth building on purpose. When parents and children share both the goals and the ways of reaching them, the family cooperates without anyone having to be told, even when the parents aren't in the room.

Try it this week

Pick one value you want your family, or your team, to be known for. Find a small problem this week you can solve together in a way that shows it.

In plain words

Families develop a culture whether or not anyone plans it. Decide what you want yours to be, and keep solving problems that way together until it becomes automatic.

Next: Just this once →
Section III · Staying out of jail

Staying Out of Jail

The shortest section is the bluntest. Some of Christensen's own classmates ended up in prison. None of them set out to. They got there one small exception at a time.

The idea behind this part

Decide what you stand for, and hold to it all the time. Holding a line 100 percent of the time is easier than holding it 98 percent of the time.

Chapter 9 · The marginal-cost trap

Just This Once...

In short

Businesses often make bad decisions by looking only at the marginal cost of the next move, not the full cost over time. People do the same with their principles. Crossing a line 'just this once' looks cheap each time, and that's how good people end up in places they'd never have chosen.

The big ideas

  • Marginal versus full cost. Established companies often say no to building something new because using what they already have looks cheaper. Over time, it costs them far more.
  • Small exceptions add up. The first step across a line looks small. The next step is smaller still, because the line has already moved.
  • 100 percent is easier than 98. If you've decided something is never an option, you don't have to re-decide it every time temptation turns up.
  • Decide in advance. Work out what you stand for before you're under pressure, because under pressure every exception looks reasonable.
From the book

Nick Leeson was a young trader in Singapore for the old British bank Barings. He hid a small loss in a little-checked error account, meaning to win it back. When he didn't, he hid the next one, and the next. By 1995 the hidden losses came to more than eight hundred million pounds, and Barings collapsed. Christensen tells a quieter story of his own. As a student at Oxford, he played centre on the university basketball team. They reached the final of the British championship, and the game was scheduled for a Sunday, which Christensen had committed never to play on for religious reasons. His coach and teammates urged him to make an exception, just this once. He didn't play. The team won without him, and he came to see that if he'd crossed the line that day, he would have kept finding reasons to cross it.

Try it this week

Write down one line you will never cross, at work or at home. Tell one person about it, so the decision is made before the pressure arrives.

In plain words

Small exceptions to your principles look harmless each time, but they add up and get easier. Decide what you stand for in advance, and hold to it every time.

Epilogue · The purpose of your life

The Purpose of Your Life

The book ends with the question in its title. Christensen argues that a purpose has to be built deliberately, out of three parts, and that the measure that will matter most at the end is not money or titles.

The idea behind this part

Decide who you want to become, commit to it, and pick the measure your life will be judged by: the individual people you helped.

Epilogue · Likeness, commitment and metrics

The Purpose of Your Life

In short

A purpose doesn't arrive by accident, Christensen argues, any more than a company's does. It has three parts: a likeness of the person you want to become, a deep commitment to it, and a few metrics to measure your life by. His own conclusion was that the measure that will matter is the individual people you helped.

The big ideas

  • The likeness. A picture of who you want to be when you're finished, not just what you want to have done.
  • Commitment. Purpose only changes what you do if you're committed to it at a deep level, deeply enough to hold when it's inconvenient.
  • The metrics. A few measures you'll use to judge your life, chosen on purpose. If you don't pick them, money and titles will be picked for you.
  • Count individuals. Christensen concluded that his life would be measured not by totals or aggregates, but by the individual people whose lives he made better.
From the book

As a Rhodes scholar at Oxford, studying econometrics at a demanding pace, Christensen decided to spend an hour every night reading, thinking and praying about why he had been put on Earth. It felt like time he couldn't afford. He later said that if he'd spent that hour learning more econometrics instead, he would have used that knowledge only a few times a year, while he used what he learned about his purpose every day. Years later, after a cancer diagnosis, he wrote that the measure by which God would assess his life wasn't dollars or titles, but the individual people whose lives he had touched.

Try it this week

Set aside an hour this week, alone, to write a first draft of your likeness and your metrics. Treat it as the most useful hour of the week, because Christensen thought it was.

In plain words

Decide on purpose who you want to become, commit to it, and pick how you'll measure your life. Christensen's answer was simple: by the individual people you helped.
