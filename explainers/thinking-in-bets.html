A chapter-by-chapter guide to Annie Duke's book

Thinking in Bets,
Explained

Annie Duke trained as a cognitive psychologist and then spent two decades as a professional poker player. Thinking in Bets takes the habits of the best players into everyday life: almost every decision is a bet made with incomplete information and some luck, so judge decisions by their reasoning rather than their results, put numbers on how sure you are, and build people and habits around you that help you learn. This guide takes the introduction and all six chapters in twelve sections, with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

A bad result doesn't mean a bad decision. Judge the bet, not the outcome, and ask yourself how sure you really are.

Part one · the bet

The Bet

Duke starts with a claim that sounds obvious and turns out not to be. Almost every decision we make is a bet, because we never have all the facts and luck always gets a vote. Chess, where everything is visible and the better player usually wins, is a poor model for life. Poker, where good players lose plenty of hands, is a much better one.

The idea behind this part

The quality of a decision and the quality of its outcome are two different things. Mixing them up teaches us the wrong lessons.

Introduction · Every decision is a bet

Why This Isn't a Poker Book

In short

Most decisions are made without all the facts and with luck in the mix. That makes them bets, and poker players, who live with both every hand, have useful habits for making them.

The big ideas

  • From psychology to poker. Duke studied cognitive psychology as a graduate student at the University of Pennsylvania. She left shortly before finishing her doctorate and spent about twenty years as a professional poker player, winning a World Series of Poker bracelet in 2004 and the NBC National Heads-Up Poker Championship in 2010.
  • Two things you can't control. Between any decision and its result sit two things: luck, and hidden information. You never know everything that matters, and even a perfect choice can be undone by chance.
  • A bet is a choice about the future. Taking a job, moving city, hiring someone or cutting a price all put something at stake on one version of the future over others. You are betting, whether or not you call it that.
  • Bet on purpose. The aim isn't to remove uncertainty, which is impossible. It is to get comfortable with it: to say "I'm not sure", put rough odds on what might happen, and learn from what does.
From the book

When Duke left graduate school she started playing poker in a small card room in Montana, coached by her brother Howard Lederer, who was already a top professional. Every hand turned out to be a tiny experiment in decision-making, with real money at stake and a result within minutes. What struck her over the years was that the habits separating winners from losers had little to do with the cards. They were about how players thought about uncertainty, luck and their own mistakes, and those habits work just as well away from the table.

In plain words

Whenever you choose, you're betting on a future you can't fully see. Some of what happens will come down to what you didn't know, some to plain luck, and only some to the choice itself. Knowing which is which is the whole game.

Chapter 1 · Resulting

Life Is Poker, Not Chess

In short

We judge decisions by how they turned out. Poker players call this resulting, and it teaches the wrong lessons, because good decisions can end badly and bad ones can end well.

The big ideas

  • The worst call in history? With 26 seconds left in Super Bowl XLIX in February 2015, the Seattle Seahawks trailed the New England Patriots by four points and had the ball on the one-yard line. Coach Pete Carroll called a pass instead of a handoff to his star running back, Marshawn Lynch. It was intercepted, Seattle lost, and the next day's headlines called it the worst play call ever.
  • Judge the call, not the result. Duke points out that interceptions from the one-yard line were rare, and that passing on second down stopped the clock and left time for two more plays. If the pass had been caught, the same call would have been praised as clever. The decision was the same either way. Only the luck was different.
  • Resulting. That is the poker word for judging a decision by its outcome. In the short run, the link between how good a decision is and how it turns out is loose, so resulting fools us often.
  • Hindsight bias. Once we know the outcome, it feels as though we should have seen it coming. That makes us blame ourselves for bad luck and take credit for good luck, and both mistakes stick.
From the book

Duke describes a chief executive who, asked for the worst decision he had made that year, named firing his company's president. The president hadn't been performing, and the executive had thought it through: he took advice, planned a replacement search and made the change. Afterwards, things went badly, and he had concluded the firing was a mistake. When Duke walked through the decision with him, he couldn't find anything wrong with how he had made it. He had judged it purely by what happened next.

In plain words

Before you decide a choice was bad, ask what you knew, what you could have found out and what the odds were. If the answers hold up, a bad result was probably bad luck. Grade the decision on those answers, not on the scoreboard.

Chapter 1 · Skill, luck and "I'm not sure"

Decisions Versus Outcomes

In short

Separate the decision from the outcome and you get four boxes, and only the decision is yours to grade. Getting good at telling them apart starts with being willing to say "I'm not sure".

The big ideas

  • Chess versus poker. The mathematician John von Neumann, a founder of game theory, didn't think of chess as a real game: in principle every position has a right answer you could calculate. Real life, he said, is full of bluffing and guessing what other people are thinking. That makes it much more like poker.
  • Your best and worst decisions. Ask people for their best and worst decisions of the past year and they almost always name their best and worst outcomes. Good decisions that turned out badly, and bad ones that got lucky, don't come to mind.
  • Drunk, and home safe. Driving home after too many drinks and arriving safely doesn't make it a good decision. One lucky result says almost nothing about the odds you took.
  • "I'm not sure" is a strength. Saying it isn't admitting ignorance. It's an accurate description of a world full of luck and hidden information, and it opens the door to the next question: how sure are you?
From the book

Duke recalls the line about von Neumann from the scientist Jacob Bronowski, who worked with him. Bronowski once described himself as a chess fan, and von Neumann corrected him: chess is a form of computation, he said, and real games, like real life, involve bluffing, small deceptions and asking yourself what the other person thinks you are going to do. Von Neumann's game theory drew on poker rather than chess, and Duke takes that as her starting point.

In plain words

Every outcome lands in one of four boxes: good decision with a good result, good decision with a bad result, bad decision with a good result, bad decision with a bad result. The outcome tells you which row you landed in. It doesn't tell you which column, and the column is the part you can improve.

Part two · beliefs and outcomes

Beliefs and Outcomes

Every bet rests on beliefs, and we form beliefs carelessly. Then, once results come in, we sort them in whatever way flatters us. These two chapters are about catching both habits: rating how sure we really are, and learning from outcomes without fooling ourselves.

The idea behind this part

Treat beliefs as estimates you can update, and treat outcomes as clues you must sort fairly into luck and skill.

Chapter 2 · How we form beliefs

Wanna Bet?

In short

Every decision is a bet on our beliefs, yet we form beliefs carelessly. We tend to take in what we hear as true and check later, if ever, and then read new facts so they fit what we already think.

The big ideas

  • Believe first, check later. Research by the psychologist Daniel Gilbert suggests our minds accept a claim as true by default. Questioning it is a separate, later step, one we often skip when we're busy or distracted.
  • The bald truth. Duke spent years believing, and repeating, that baldness is inherited from your mother's father. It's a common myth. Hair loss is shaped by genes from both sides of the family, but the claim had never been worth checking.
  • Beliefs defend themselves. Once a belief is in, we look for evidence that supports it and pick holes in evidence that doesn't. This is motivated reasoning, and it explains why fake news spreads so well: we want to believe the stories that suit us.
  • Smart is no shield. Being clever doesn't protect you. In a study by Dan Kahan and colleagues, people who were good with numbers read data accurately until the answer clashed with their politics. Then they were just as likely as anyone to read it the way their side wanted, and the gap between sides was, if anything, wider among the most numerate.
From the book

In 1951 Dartmouth and Princeton played a bad-tempered football game full of penalties and injuries. Afterwards the psychologists Albert Hastorf and Hadley Cantril showed students from both colleges a film of the game and asked them to note every infraction. Princeton students counted far more fouls by Dartmouth players, more than twice as many as Dartmouth students saw. The two groups watched the same film and, in a real sense, saw different games. Their beliefs about their own team shaped what they took in.

In plain words

Most of your beliefs arrived without a check at the door. That's fine for trivia and dangerous for the beliefs you bet on. The ones that feel most obviously true, especially about your own side, are the ones most worth questioning.

Chapter 2 · Beliefs in percentages

Redefining Confidence

In short

Instead of treating beliefs as simply right or wrong, rate how sure you are. Saying "I'm 70 percent sure" makes it easier to change your mind and harder to fool yourself.

The big ideas

  • "Wanna bet?" If someone challenged you to put money on a belief, you'd suddenly think harder: where did I hear that, how much do I really know? Duke suggests asking yourself the question even when nobody else does.
  • Beliefs come in degrees. Very little of what we believe is certain. Rating a belief from 0 to 100 forces you to say how much you actually know, instead of rounding everything to yes or no.
  • Updating gets easier. If you were 100 percent sure and you're wrong, you've failed. If you were 60 percent sure, new information just moves the number. Changing your mind becomes an adjustment, not a defeat.
  • It invites help. Saying "I'm fairly sure, maybe 70 percent" tells people you're open to what they know. They're more likely to share the fact that would change your mind.
From the book

In the poker world Duke came up in, a confident claim over dinner was often met with two words: "Wanna bet?" The effect was immediate. Someone who had just stated a fact would start to hedge: well, they'd read it somewhere, they were fairly sure, it might have been a while ago. Nothing about the world had changed. Being asked to put something on the line simply made them vet a belief they had been happy to state as certain a moment before.

In plain words

Next time you state a belief, attach a number to it, even silently. Over time, check whether the things you're 90 percent sure of come true about 90 percent of the time. That habit alone makes you a better bettor.

Chapter 3 · Fielding the unfolding future

Bet to Learn

In short

Outcomes are feedback, but only if we sort them honestly into luck and skill. We tend to take credit for good results and blame luck for bad ones, and that keeps us from learning.

The big ideas

  • Every result is a puzzle. When something happens, we have to decide how much came from our decisions and how much from luck. Duke calls this fielding outcomes, and we rarely field them fairly.
  • The self-serving bias. We credit our wins to skill and our losses to bad luck. Duke quotes lines said to come from car insurance claim forms, where drivers describe crashes as something that happened to them, such as a telephone pole that "was approaching".
  • The learning loop breaks. If every loss is bad luck, you never fix your mistakes. If every win is skill, you repeat the lucky ones. Either way, experience stops making you better.
  • Rewire the reward. Borrowing from Charles Duhigg's work on habits, Duke suggests keeping the cue (an outcome) and the reward (feeling good about yourself), but changing the routine: feel good for spotting luck, crediting others and finding your own mistakes, rather than for being right.
From the book

Duke describes having dinner with Phil Ivey, one of the best poker players in the world, right after he had won a tournament. Most people would have spent the evening enjoying the win. Ivey spent it going through the hands he thought he had played badly, asking for opinions and picking apart his own decisions. He wasn't upset. He simply treated a win as just as much a chance to learn as a loss, and Duke saw that habit as a large part of why he was so good.

In plain words

After any result, good or bad, ask two questions: what part of this was luck, and what would I do differently? Asking them after wins is the hard part, and the most valuable.

Next: Other people's outcomes →
Chapter 3 · The mirror flips

Other People's Outcomes

In short

We do the reverse for others: their wins look like luck and their losses look like their fault. And because we feel results by comparison, we'd often rather beat someone than learn.

The big ideas

  • The mirror flips. When a rival succeeds, we put it down to luck; when they fail, we put it down to poor play. That robs us of the lessons in other people's experience, which is most of the experience available to us.
  • Winning means beating someone. We judge outcomes against other people's. In one survey, about half the people asked preferred earning $50,000 a year while others earned $25,000 over earning $100,000 while others earned $200,000.
  • Schadenfreude. Taking pleasure in other people's bad luck feels good, which is exactly why it's a trap. It's a sign we are keeping score against them instead of learning from them.
  • Change what you compete at. Duke doesn't ask us to drop the competitive drive. She suggests aiming it at accuracy: compete to be the best at spotting luck, giving credit and admitting mistakes.
From the book

Duke tells of a player she calls Nick the Greek, who was convinced that seven-deuce, the weakest starting hand in Texas Hold'em, was a great hand to play because opponents never expected it. When it won, that proved his genius. When it lost, which was most of the time, that was bad luck. Since every result confirmed his theory, he never had a reason to change it, and he lost a lot of money playing it. The pattern is extreme, but the habit of fielding outcomes to protect a belief is something we all do.

In plain words

When something good happens to someone you don't like, ask what they did right. When something bad happens to them, ask how much was luck. Treating other people's outcomes the way you treat your own, and the reverse, doubles what you can learn.

Part three · other people

Other People

It is very hard to see your own biases. It is much easier for someone else to see them, if the group around you is set up for it. Duke describes how to build a small truthseeking pod, the rules from science that keep it honest, and how to bring the same spirit to people who never signed up for it.

The idea behind this part

A good group rewards accuracy over agreement, shares all the data and makes dissent part of the job.

Chapter 4 · Find a truthseeking pod

The Buddy System

In short

It's hard to be objective on your own. A small group that rewards accuracy, holds each other to account and welcomes different views can catch the biases you can't see in yourself.

The big ideas

  • A charter. A group helps only if its members agree up front what they're there for: accuracy over feeling good, accountability for their reasoning, and openness to views that differ from their own.
  • Exploratory thought. The psychologists Jennifer Lerner and Philip Tetlock found people think most even-handedly when they know they'll have to explain their reasoning to an audience whose views they don't know, who cares about accuracy and is well informed.
  • Agreement isn't the point. A group of people who all think alike is an echo chamber: it makes everyone more confident without making anyone more right. The value of a pod comes from members who see things differently.
  • Many minds beat one. In a 2015 study led by Anna Dreber, researchers betting in a prediction market on whether psychology studies would replicate got it right 71 percent of the time, against 58 percent when the same experts were simply surveyed.
From the book

Early in her poker career, Duke tried to tell a bad-beat story, a tale of losing a hand to rotten luck, to the champion player Erik Seidel. He stopped her. He didn't want to hear about luck, he said; if she had a question about how she had played the hand, he was happy to talk about that. It stung at the time. It also showed her what a good truthseeking partner does: they won't help you feel better at the expense of seeing clearly, and they keep the conversation on the decisions you can actually improve.

In plain words

Find two or three people who care more about getting it right than about agreeing with you. Agree on the rules out loud. Then bring them your decisions, especially the ones that worked out, and let them poke holes.

Chapter 5 · CUDOS: rules for a truthseeking group

Dissent to Win

In short

In 1942 the sociologist Robert K. Merton described the norms that make science work, known as CUDOS. Duke borrows them as rules for any group that wants to make better bets.

The big ideas

  • Communism: share the data. Information belongs to the group. Share all the details, especially the ones that make you look bad, because we tend to leave out whatever doesn't fit our story. Duke points to the film Rashomon, in which four witnesses give four different accounts of the same event.
  • Universalism: don't shoot the message. Judge an idea on its merits, not on who said it. Duke admits that early on she wrote off players she didn't respect and missed the things they were doing well. Good ideas come from people you don't like, too.
  • Disinterestedness: hide the ending. We all have a stake in how a story comes out. When you ask for advice on a decision, describe it without saying how it turned out, so listeners judge the decision rather than working back from the result.
  • Organised scepticism: dissent on purpose. Make disagreement part of the job. The US State Department has a Dissent Channel that lets staff send objections to policy straight to senior leaders, and many organisations use red teams whose task is to argue the other side.
From the book

Duke describes what happens whenever she tells a poker hand to friends or students: almost the first thing they want to know is how it ended. And once they know, their analysis bends around the result. If she won, her choices look sound; if she lost, they look reckless. So in her own truthseeking group, players learned to describe hands up to the decision point and stop there, letting the others judge the play before hearing the outcome. It is the same idea as a blind trial in science, applied over a drink.

In plain words

When you review a decision, yours or anyone's, cover up the ending first. Ask what was known, what the options were and what the odds looked like. Only then look at the result, and notice how much it wants to change your mind.

Chapter 5 · Talking to people outside the pod

Dissent Without a Fight

In short

Most people haven't signed up for truthseeking. To share a different view without setting off their defences, express uncertainty, lead with agreement, say "and" instead of "but", ask first and talk about the future.

The big ideas

  • Express uncertainty. Starting with how sure you are, or aren't, makes it easier for the other person to disagree, and easier for you to listen when they do.
  • Lead with assent. Begin with the parts you agree with, and mean it. People are far more open to the rest once they've heard their own view acknowledged.
  • "And", not "but". "But" tells the listener that everything before it didn't count. "And" adds to it. Duke borrows the idea from improv comedy's rule of "yes, and".
  • Ask, then look forward. Before giving hard feedback, ask whether the person wants it; sometimes they just want to vent. And instead of picking over what went wrong, ask what they might do next. People are much more open to the future than to being judged on the past.
From the book

Duke gives the example of someone venting about a bad situation, a boss who treats them unfairly or a relationship that went wrong. Pointing out where they went wrong, however true, only makes them defensive. Asking what they would like to happen from here, and what they might try next time, lets them look at the same facts without feeling judged. The truthseeking still happens; it just arrives facing forward.

In plain words

If you want someone to hear a hard truth, make it easy to accept: say how sure you are, agree with what's right, add rather than cancel, ask before you advise and point the conversation at what happens next.

Part four · your future self

Your Future Self

The person making a decision is often the version of us least suited to it: tired, angry or carried away by the moment. The last chapter is a toolkit for bringing past and future selves into the room, and for mapping the futures that could follow before you choose.

The idea behind this part

Before you bet, travel in time: ask how your future self will see it, and imagine both success and failure in advance.

Chapter 6 · Let your future self in

Adventures in Mental Time Travel

In short

In the moment, emotions and the present loom large. Pulling in the views of your past and future selves, with tools like 10-10-10 and advance commitments, keeps one bad hour from steering a big decision.

The big ideas

  • Night guy and morning guy. Jerry Seinfeld jokes that night guy stays up late without a care, and morning guy pays for it. Our present self keeps making choices our future self has to live with.
  • 10-10-10. The business writer Suzy Welch's test: how will I feel about this in ten minutes, ten months and ten years? Asking it shrinks the moment back to its real size.
  • Zoom out on the ticker. Don't watch your happiness like a stock price, minute by minute. A bad day feels like a crash when you're zoomed in. Zoom out and it is often a small dip in a longer trend.
  • Ulysses contracts. Ulysses had himself tied to the mast so he could hear the Sirens without steering onto the rocks. You can bind your future self too: a loss limit at the table, a rule to wait a day before sending an angry email, a "decision swear jar" for phrases that signal you're not thinking straight.
From the book

In poker, Duke writes, a bad beat, losing a hand you were a big favourite to win, can send a player on tilt. The word comes from pinball machines, which flash TILT and freeze when you shake them too hard. A player on tilt chases losses, plays hands they would normally fold and makes choices their calmer self would never sign off on. Recognising the signs, the hot face, the urge to win it all back right now, is the cue to step away from the table before the next bet.

In plain words

When a decision feels urgent and emotional, ask how you'll see it in ten months and ten years. And for the moments you know will tempt you, decide in advance and make the rule hard to break.

Chapter 6 · Scenario planning, backcasting and premortems

Scouting the Future

In short

Map the futures that could follow a decision, put rough odds and values on each, and work backwards from both success and failure. When the result arrives, don't let hindsight chop the rest of the tree away.

The big ideas

  • Scenario planning. List the ways a decision could turn out, estimate how likely each is and what each is worth. Even rough guesses are better than none, because they make you think about the futures you'd rather ignore.
  • Backcasting. Imagine you've succeeded and ask how you got there. Working backwards from the goal reveals the steps you need, and the luck you'd be relying on.
  • The premortem. The psychologist Gary Klein's technique: imagine it's a year from now and the plan has failed. Why? It's far easier to spot weaknesses when failure is a given. Gabriele Oettingen's research points the same way: positive fantasies alone can sap the energy to act, while pairing a goal with its obstacles helps.
  • Don't take a chainsaw to the tree. Before a decision, the future is a tree with many branches. Once an outcome happens, hindsight cuts away all the others, and the one that happened looks as if it was always going to. Remember the whole tree when you look back.
From the book

Duke worked with After-School All-Stars, a charity that relies on grants. Its team ranked grant applications by how much money each could bring in, and put the most effort into the biggest. When she asked them to estimate their chance of winning each one, the list reshuffled: a huge grant with a slim chance could be worth less than a modest one they were likely to win. Putting rough odds on the futures, even guesses, changed where the team spent its time.

In plain words

Before a big decision, sketch the main futures, guess their odds and values, and run a premortem on your favourite. Afterwards, judge yourself against the tree you drew, not just the branch that happened.

Afterword

Your bet log

The whole book in one habit: before a decision, write down what you're betting on and how sure you are. Afterwards, check back and ask how much was the decision and how much was luck.
