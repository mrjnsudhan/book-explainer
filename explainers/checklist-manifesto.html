A chapter-by-chapter guide to Atul Gawande's book

The Checklist Manifesto,
Explained

Atul Gawande is a surgeon who noticed that most mistakes in his operating room weren't caused by a lack of knowledge, but by failing to use what everyone already knew. The Checklist Manifesto follows a humble tool, the checklist, from 1930s bombers to intensive care, skyscrapers and the World Health Organization, and shows how a two-minute pause can save lives. This guide takes the introduction and all nine chapters one at a time, with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

When the work outgrows memory, a short list of the steps that matter isn't a crutch. It's how experts get it right every time.

Part one · the problem

The Problem

Gawande opens with a puzzle. We know more than any generation before us, yet skilled, hard-working people still make avoidable mistakes every day. The reason is not laziness or a lack of knowledge. It is that the work has become too complex for any one memory to carry.

The idea behind this part

Most failures today are not failures of knowing. They are failures of applying what we already know, reliably, every single time.

Introduction · Two reasons we fail

Introduction

In short

We fail for two reasons: because we don't know enough, or because we don't use what we know. Today the second kind is the bigger problem, and it calls for a different fix.

The big ideas

  • Two kinds of failure. In the 1970s, the philosophers Samuel Gorovitz and Alasdair MacIntyre split human error into ignorance (the knowledge doesn't exist yet) and ineptitude (the knowledge exists, but we fail to apply it).
  • The balance has flipped. For most of history, ignorance was the main enemy. A doctor in 1950 had little to offer a heart attack patient. Now there are many proven treatments, and the trouble is giving the right ones, correctly, to every patient.
  • Ineptitude feels worse. We forgive a doctor who couldn't have known. We are far harder on one who knew the right thing and still didn't do it, and so are doctors on themselves.
  • Know-how has outgrown us. Medicine, building, finance and flying now hold so much knowledge, in so many steps, that even the best-trained expert slips. The question is what tool can help.
From the book

As a young surgical resident, Gawande helped treat a man stabbed at a costume party. The wound looked small, the man was stable, and the team prepared for a routine repair. Then his blood pressure collapsed. The blade had gone much deeper than anyone guessed, into the body's main artery. They saved him, but in the review afterwards nobody could say who should have asked the obvious question: what was the weapon, and how long was it? Everyone knew the step. Nobody took it.

In plain words

When something goes wrong, ask whether anyone could have known better, or whether someone knew and it slipped. Most modern mistakes are the second kind, and you don't fix those with more training. You fix them with a better system.

Chapter 1 · Too many steps for one memory

The Problem of Extreme Complexity

In short

Modern work, from intensive care to engineering, involves so many steps that even near-perfect people make several errors a day. Specialising helps, but it can't keep up on its own.

The big ideas

  • The girl in the pond. A three-year-old in the Austrian Alps fell through the ice of a frozen pond and spent about thirty minutes underwater. Her heart had stopped. A rescue team, a helicopter and a hospital ready with a heart-lung machine brought her back, and she recovered fully. It took dozens of people doing hundreds of things right, in order.
  • 178 actions a day. A study of intensive care units in Israel counted an average of 178 separate actions per patient per day, from giving a drug to suctioning a lung. Staff got them right about 99 percent of the time, which still left around two errors a day for every patient.
  • The size of the field. The World Health Organization's list of diseases and injuries runs to more than 13,000 entries, and doctors have thousands of drugs and procedures to choose from. No one can hold all of it.
  • Specialists are not enough. Medicine's answer was to split into ever-narrower specialties. That helps with knowledge, but it multiplies the number of people who must coordinate, and the gaps between them become a new source of error.
From the book

Gawande describes intensive care as a place where a single patient can need help with breathing, blood pressure, kidneys and infection all at once, each managed by different people with different machines. One common slip is infection from a central line, the tube threaded into a large vein. Each line is put in by someone who knows exactly how to do it safely, yet line infections were common enough to kill many thousands of patients a year. The steps were known. They just weren't always followed.

In plain words

Complexity, not stupidity, is what trips experts up. When a job has hundreds of steps, a small error rate turns into errors every day. The fix has to work at the level of the system, not just the individual.

Part two · the tool

The Checklist

A pilot's card and a builder's wall chart turn out to answer the same problem. A short list of the steps people skip catches slips of memory, and a schedule of who must talk to whom catches the problems no list can predict.

The idea behind this part

There are two kinds of checklist: one for the simple steps everyone forgets, and one that forces experts to talk before the unexpected bites.

Chapter 2 · From the B-17 to the ICU

The Checklist

In short

When a plane became too complex for one pilot to remember, pilots wrote a checklist. Decades later, a doctor used the same trick to wipe out a deadly hospital infection.

The big ideas

  • Too much airplane. In 1935, Boeing's new Model 299 bomber crashed on a demonstration flight, killing two of the crew. The expert pilot had forgotten to release a lock on the controls. A newspaper called it too much airplane for one man to fly.
  • An index card. Instead of more training, test pilots wrote simple checklists for takeoff, flight, landing and taxiing, short enough for a card. The plane went on to fly 1.8 million miles without an accident, and the army ordered thousands as the B-17.
  • Five steps in the ICU. In 2001, Peter Pronovost at Johns Hopkins listed the five steps to avoid line infections: wash hands, clean the skin with chlorhexidine, cover the patient with sterile drapes, wear a mask, hat, gown and gloves, and put a sterile dressing on the site.
  • Nurses could stop doctors. Nurses watched for a month and found doctors skipped at least one step with more than a third of patients. So the hospital let nurses stop a doctor who skipped a step. The ten-day infection rate fell from 11 percent to zero.
From the book

The results kept coming. Over a little more than a year at Johns Hopkins, Pronovost's checklist was credited with preventing 43 infections and 8 deaths and saving about two million dollars. Then Michigan tried it across its intensive care units in what was called the Keystone Initiative. Within three months, line infections fell by two thirds, and over its first eighteen months the project was estimated to have saved more than 1,500 lives. The steps were not new. What was new was writing them down and making them stick.

In plain words

A checklist doesn't teach anyone something new. It makes sure the dull, essential steps happen every time, and it gives the whole team, not just the person in charge, the right to say "stop, we skipped one".

Chapter 3 · How builders handle the unexpected

The End of the Master Builder

In short

No single master builder can design a modern skyscraper. Construction copes with complexity by pairing a checklist of tasks with a checklist of conversations, and by pushing decisions out to the people closest to the problem.

The big ideas

  • Sixteen trades. Buildings were once designed and built by a master builder. Today they need structural engineers, architects, electricians, plumbers and many more specialists, none of whom sees the whole job.
  • A schedule of tasks. On a Boston tower project, Gawande saw a huge wall chart listing every task in order, day by day. It is a checklist for the steps everyone knows must happen.
  • A schedule of conversations. Beside it was a second chart, the submittal schedule, that listed which experts had to check in with each other, and by when. It covers the problems no one can predict by making sure the right people talk.
  • Power to the edges. When Hurricane Katrina hit, Walmart's chief told store managers to make decisions above their level and do the right thing. Stores sent out water and food days before the government. In a crisis, central plans fail and local judgement plus communication wins.
From the book

Gawande sat with the structural engineer Joe Salvia, whose firm worked on the building. Salvia explained that the industry had learned to distrust any one person's judgement on a complex job. Every decision that crossed specialties had to be signed off by the others, so problems surfaced early, in meetings, instead of later, in collapsing beams. The result is a striking safety record: the book notes that serious building failures happen at a rate of less than 0.00002 percent a year.

In plain words

For simple, known steps, use a task list. For complex, unpredictable work, use a list that forces the right people to talk at the right moments, and then trust them to decide.

Part three · the craft

Building One

Gawande takes the idea into the operating room for the World Health Organization. The first drafts flop, and he learns from Boeing's checklist makers that a good checklist is short, precise, tested and built for a pause in the work.

The idea behind this part

A bad checklist is long, vague and treats people as idiots. A good one is a quick reminder of the critical steps that even experts miss.

Chapter 4 · Simple tools, big effects

The Idea

In short

Gawande is asked to help the World Health Organization make surgery safer worldwide. Looking at kitchens and a public health study with soap, he sees how simple tools can change what people do.

The big ideas

  • The scale of surgery. By the WHO's estimate, around 234 million operations were done each year, more than the number of births. At least 7 million people a year were left disabled by complications, and about a million died.
  • Soap in Karachi. Stephen Luby, a public health researcher, gave free soap to families in poor neighbourhoods of Karachi and showed them when to wash. Compared with similar families, their children had about half as much diarrhoea and pneumonia.
  • Why soap worked. The families already knew washing mattered. The good soap made the habit easy and pleasant, and the simple instructions made it routine. A small tool changed behaviour where lectures hadn't.
  • A chef's checklist. At a busy Boston restaurant, Gawande saw recipes treated as checklists, a list of each night's changes for the cooks and a head chef checking every plate before it left. Great cooks still use lists.
From the book

The WHO gathered surgeons, anaesthetists, nurses and safety experts from around the world in Geneva to find a single fix for surgery, which has thousands of different operations. The group argued over training, incentives and new technology. What kept coming back was something cheaper: a short check that any team, in any hospital, could run in a couple of minutes, covering the steps most often missed.

In plain words

People often know the right thing already. What changes behaviour is a simple tool that makes the right thing easy, clear and routine. That's what a checklist is meant to be.

Chapter 5 · Teamwork is a checklist item

The First Try

In short

The first surgical checklist was clumsy and confused the team. But one item proved surprisingly powerful: having everyone in the room say their name and role before the operation starts.

The big ideas

  • Antibiotics on time. Giving an antibiotic in the hour before the first cut cuts infection risk sharply, yet it was missed in a large share of operations. That became a classic checklist item.
  • Say your name. Operating teams often hadn't met. Studies at Johns Hopkins and elsewhere found that when everyone introduced themselves before starting, people were more likely to speak up later when they saw a problem.
  • The activation phenomenon. Giving someone a chance to say something at the start, even just their name, seems to switch on their sense of responsibility and their willingness to talk.
  • A messy first test. When Gawande's own team tried the draft, nobody knew who should lead it, the questions were unclear and it took too long. A checklist can be right in principle and still fail in practice.
From the book

Gawande describes the awkwardness of the first trial runs in his own operating room. Some items made sense on paper but confused people at the table. Some checks happened at the wrong moment, after the step they were meant to catch. The surgeons felt silly reading aloud. It was clear that writing a good checklist was a craft in itself, and that the team would need to learn it from people who had been doing it for decades.

In plain words

Some of the most important checklist items aren't technical at all. Making sure everyone knows each other and has spoken once turns a group of strangers into a team that will speak up when it matters.

Chapter 6 · How Boeing writes a checklist

The Checklist Factory

In short

At Boeing, Gawande learns that a good checklist is short, precise and built for a natural pause in the work. It is not a how-to manual; it is a quick check of the killer steps.

The big ideas

  • Pause points. Decide when the checklist will be used. In aviation it's before engine start, before takeoff and so on: moments when the team can stop for a minute.
  • Read-do or do-confirm. With a read-do list, you read each step and then do it, like a recipe. With a do-confirm list, the team works from memory, then stops to confirm nothing was missed.
  • Five to nine items. Keep it to a single page with roughly five to nine items, the steps most dangerous to skip and most likely to be missed. Run it in about a minute.
  • Test and rewrite. Use simple, exact words, familiar to the people using it. Then try it in the real world, find where it fails, and fix it. Checklists are never finished.
From the book

Gawande spent time with Daniel Boorman, who had written checklists for Boeing's aircraft for years. In a flight simulator, Boorman showed him how a crew handles a warning light: one pilot flies while the other pulls up the right checklist and reads it aloud, step by step. The list was short and precise, made for the moment. Boorman's message was that a bad checklist is vague and long and tries to spell out everything, and that a good one trusts the pros and targets only what they might miss.

In plain words

A good checklist is short, specific, quick and used at a set moment. It reminds skilled people of the few things they're most likely to skip, and it gets tested and rewritten until it works.

Part four · the proof

Proof and Pride

The surgical checklist is tried in eight hospitals around the world, and the results are startling. Then Gawande asks why so many experts still refuse it, with stories from investors, an airliner on the Hudson and one of his own operations.

The idea behind this part

Using a checklist is not a sign of weakness. In a complex world, it is what discipline and teamwork look like.

Chapter 7 · Eight hospitals, one checklist

The Test

In short

The WHO surgical checklist was tried in eight very different hospitals around the world. Major complications fell by more than a third and deaths by almost half.

The big ideas

  • Eight cities. The hospitals were in Toronto, London, Seattle, Auckland, Amman, New Delhi, Manila and Ifakara in rural Tanzania, from rich teaching hospitals to places with few resources.
  • Three pause points. The final checklist had about nineteen items in three stops: before anaesthesia, before the first incision, and before the patient leaves the operating room. It took about two minutes.
  • The results. Across nearly 4,000 patients before and nearly 4,000 after, major complications fell from 11 to 7 percent, and deaths fell from 1.5 to 0.8 percent. Every hospital improved.
  • Doubters who would want it. About a fifth of staff found it hard to use or felt it didn't improve safety. But when asked whether they would want the checklist used if they were the patient, 93 percent said yes.
From the book

Gawande admits he was nervous. The checklist could easily have done nothing, or the hospitals could have quietly stopped using it. When the data came in, the size of the drop surprised even the researchers. Some of the gain came from specific items, like antibiotics being given on time. But a lot of it seemed to come from teamwork: people who had talked before the first cut handled the unexpected better when it came.

In plain words

The checklist worked everywhere it was tried, rich hospital or poor. Even the people who grumbled about it wanted it used on themselves.

Chapter 8 · Discipline, not swagger

The Hero in the Age of Checklists

In short

We love lone heroes, but the real experts are the ones who follow their checklists under pressure. Gawande finds them among investors and in the cockpit of a plane that landed on the Hudson.

The big ideas

  • Investors with lists. Some investors, like Mohnish Pabrai, studied their own and others' costly mistakes and turned them into checklists to run before buying. It made them slower to jump and helped them avoid traps others fell into.
  • Fear and greed. Checklists help most in high-pressure moments when emotions take over and people skip the steps they know they should take.
  • Miracle on the Hudson. In January 2009, US Airways Flight 1549 hit a flock of geese after takeoff from LaGuardia and lost both engines. Captain Sullenberger flew while First Officer Skiles worked the engine failure checklist. They glided onto the Hudson and all 155 people survived.
  • Not one hero. Sullenberger kept stressing that the crew, the procedures and the training did it. The press wanted a lone hero; the reality was a disciplined team.
From the book

Gawande notes that pilots are trained to treat checklists as part of being a pro, not a slight on their skill. On Flight 1549, there were only about three and a half minutes from the bird strike to the water. In that time, the co-pilot ran through as much of the restart checklist as he could while the captain picked a landing spot, and the cabin crew got passengers into brace positions. Each did their part, by the book, at speed.

In plain words

Following a checklist under pressure is what heroes actually do. The best performers are disciplined enough to admit they are fallible and use the tools that catch them.

Chapter 9 · When the checklist catches you

The Save

In short

Gawande ends with one of his own operations, where a sudden, massive bleed could have killed the patient. The checklist had already put the team, and the blood, in place.

The big ideas

  • He uses it too. Gawande started using the checklist in his own operating room, half expecting it to be a waste of time. It soon caught problems he would have missed, like an antibiotic not yet given.
  • The tear. During an operation to remove a tumour, he accidentally tore the vena cava, the body's largest vein. The patient lost a huge amount of blood in moments.
  • Ready before it happened. Because the team had discussed the risk of major blood loss before the first cut, blood had been ordered and was ready. Everyone knew each other's names and roles. They worked fast and the patient survived.
  • Humility. Gawande's closing point is that we are not as good as we think. Accepting a checklist means accepting our limits, and that is the beginning of doing better.
From the book

Gawande writes that he went into the operation with no particular worry. The tumour was in a difficult spot, and in the checklist briefing he had mentioned, almost in passing, that blood loss was possible. The anaesthetist made sure blood was available. When the vein tore, the room didn't freeze. The nurses knew what to fetch, the anaesthetist started transfusing at once, and he was able to get control of the bleeding. Without that two-minute conversation, he believes, the patient would have died.

In plain words

Checklists don't replace skill. They protect it. The day something unexpected goes wrong, the conversation you had at the start may be what saves the day.

Your checklist
