A habit-by-habit guide to the book by Stephen R. Covey

The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People,
Explained

Stephen Covey spent years reading two hundred years of American writing on success, and noticed a shift. The older books were about character: integrity, patience, fairness, hard work. The newer ones were about personality: techniques, image and quick fixes. The 7 Habits is his argument for going back to character, and his plan for doing it in seven habits that take you from depending on others, to standing on your own, to working well with other people. This guide takes the book section by section, with a lab in each where you can try the idea.

The book in one line

Change starts inside out: master yourself first, then you can work well with others, and keep renewing both.

Part One · Paradigms and principles

Paradigms and Principles

Before any habit, Covey asks you to look at the lens you look through. How you see a situation decides what you do about it, so lasting change has to start with how you see, and with who you are, not with a new technique.

The idea behind this part

Work from the inside out. Fix the way you see and the person you are, and the behaviour follows.

Section 1 · Paradigms: the maps in our heads

Inside-Out

In short

Each of us looks at the world through paradigms: mental maps of how things are and how they should be. We rarely notice them, but they shape every judgement we make. Change the map and your behaviour changes with it, often in an instant.

The big ideas

  • The map is not the territory. A paradigm is a map of reality, not reality itself. If you're using a map of the wrong city, trying harder or thinking positively won't get you there.
  • We see the world as we are. We think we see things objectively, but we describe the world through our own conditioning. Two people can look at the same thing and both be right about what they see.
  • A paradigm shift changes everything at once. When you see a situation differently, you don't need a technique to act differently. The new behaviour comes on its own.
  • Inside-out, not outside-in. Covey's approach starts with yourself: your paradigms, your character and your motives, before you try to change other people or circumstances.
From the book

One Sunday morning on a New York subway, Covey sat in a quiet carriage until a man got on with his children. The kids ran around, shouted and grabbed people's papers, while their father sat with his eyes closed and did nothing. Irritated, Covey finally asked him whether he could control his children a bit more. The man looked up, as if noticing for the first time, and said quietly that they had just come from the hospital where the children's mother had died about an hour before. He didn't know what to think, and he supposed they didn't know how to handle it either. In that moment, Covey writes, his irritation vanished and he felt only sympathy. Nothing in the carriage had changed except how he saw it.

Try it this week

Pick someone who annoyed you this week. Write down two other explanations for what they did that would make you see it differently.

In plain words

How you see a situation decides how you feel and act. Before you try to change what you do, check the map you're using.

Section 2 · Primary greatness and the law of the farm

The Character Ethic

In short

Covey read two centuries of writing on success and found that for the first 150 years it focused on character: integrity, humility, courage, patience. After the First World War it shifted to the personality ethic: image, attitude and techniques for getting your way. Techniques work for a while, he argues, but lasting success rests on character.

The big ideas

  • Two kinds of greatness. Secondary greatness is talent, status and social skill. Primary greatness is goodness of character. Without the second, the first eventually shows through.
  • Principles are like lighthouses. Fairness, honesty, service and growth are natural laws that don't move. You can break yourself against them, but you can't break them.
  • You can't cram on a farm. In school you can cram the night before an exam. On a farm you can't forget to plant in spring and cram in autumn. Relationships and character work like the farm.
  • What you are speaks louder. If people sense you are using a technique on them, it backfires. What builds trust is who you are, shown over time.
From the book

Covey and his wife Sandra were worried about one of their sons, who was struggling at school, socially awkward and small for his age. They tried everything the personality ethic suggested: encouragement, positive talk, coaching him on how to do better. Nothing worked, and he seemed to lose confidence. Then they realised that underneath all the encouragement, the message their son was getting was that his parents thought he wasn't good enough and needed protecting. So they worked on themselves instead, on seeing him as capable and letting him grow at his own pace. Over time, and without being pushed, he began to flourish.

Try it this week

Notice one moment this week when you reach for a technique to get someone to do something. Ask what the character answer would be instead.

In plain words

Tricks for getting your way can work in the short run. Real, lasting success comes from being trustworthy, and that takes time, like growing a crop.

Section 3 · Knowledge, skill and desire

The Seven Habits: An Overview

In short

Covey defines a habit as the place where three things meet: knowledge (what to do and why), skill (how to do it) and desire (wanting to do it). The seven habits move you along a maturity continuum, from dependence to independence to interdependence.

The big ideas

  • Three circles make a habit. Know what to do but not how, and nothing happens. Know how but don't want to, and nothing happens. A habit needs all three.
  • Dependence: you take care of me. The dependent person blames others and relies on them for results.
  • Independence: I can do it. The independent person takes responsibility and relies on themselves. It's a big step, but it isn't the top.
  • Interdependence: we can do it better. The interdependent person combines their efforts with others' to achieve more than either could alone. Only an independent person can choose it.
From the book

Covey compares breaking a deep habit to the flight of Apollo 11. Leaving Earth took more energy in the first few minutes of lift-off than the astronauts would use in the days of travel that followed. Habits have that kind of gravity, he says: breaking free of procrastination, impatience or selfishness takes a huge effort at the start. But once you're out of the pull, you have a new kind of freedom.

Try it this week

Pick a habit you keep failing to build. Which circle is missing: knowing what and why, knowing how, or really wanting to?

In plain words

A habit needs three things together: knowing what to do, knowing how, and wanting to. The seven habits take you from leaning on others, to standing on your own, to working well with others.

Section 4 · The P/PC balance

Production and Capability

In short

Effectiveness, Covey says, is a balance between P, production of the results you want, and PC, production capability: looking after whatever produces them. Neglect the capability and the results dry up. He takes the idea from Aesop's fable of the goose that laid golden eggs.

The big ideas

  • Eggs and goose. The eggs are the results: money, output, a clean house. The goose is what makes them: your health, your machine, your relationships, your team.
  • Too much P kills the goose. Push for output and skip maintenance and you get more eggs for a while, then none.
  • Too much PC starves you. Spend all your time looking after the goose and you never collect any eggs. The answer is balance, not one or the other.
  • It applies to people. Treat employees, children or a spouse as producers of results only, and the relationship that produces those results wears out.
From the book

In the fable, a poor farmer finds a golden egg in the nest of his goose, then another each day. He grows rich, and then greedy and impatient. Wanting all the gold at once, he kills the goose to get at the eggs inside, and finds nothing. Covey adds an example of his own: he once bought a power lawn mower and used it hard without ever servicing it. It worked well for two seasons and then broke down, and it had cost him far more than the maintenance would have.

Try it this week

Name one golden goose in your life that you've been running without maintenance: your sleep, a key relationship, a tool. Book an hour for it this week.

In plain words

Results come from something that produces them. Look after that thing, whether it's your health, your team or your relationships, or the results will stop.

Part Two · Private victory

Private Victory

The first three habits are about mastering yourself. They take you from dependence, where other people and events run your life, to independence, where you choose your response, decide what you stand for and organise your week around it.

The idea behind this part

You can't win with other people until you've won with yourself. Private victories come before public ones.

Habit 1 · The space between stimulus and response

Be Proactive

In short

The first habit is to take responsibility for your own life. Between what happens to you and how you respond there is a space, and in that space is your freedom to choose. Reactive people let their feelings and circumstances decide. Proactive people decide based on their values.

The big ideas

  • Responsibility is response-ability. Covey splits the word: the ability to choose your response. Proactive people don't blame circumstances, conditions or upbringing for their behaviour.
  • Four human gifts. Self-awareness, imagination, conscience and independent will. Together they let you step back from your conditioning and choose.
  • Weather inside, not outside. Reactive people feel good when the weather is good and the people around them are nice. Proactive people carry their own weather.
  • Nobody can hurt you without your consent. What hurts us most is not what happens, but our response to it. That gives back a lot of control.
From the book

Covey retells the story of Viktor Frankl, a psychiatrist imprisoned in Nazi death camps, whose parents, brother and wife died there. Stripped of everything, Frankl realised there was one thing his captors could never take: his freedom to decide how all of this would affect him. In that space between what was done to him and his response, he used his imagination and conscience to picture himself one day lecturing about what he had learned. He helped other prisoners find meaning in their suffering, and became, in Covey's words, freer than his guards.

Try it this week

Next time something sets you off, count to five before you answer and ask: what response would I be proud of tomorrow?

In plain words

Things happen that you can't control. How you respond is always yours to choose, and choosing on purpose is the first habit.

Next: Proactive language →
Habit 1 · "I can't" or "I choose"

Listen to Your Language

In short

The words you use show whether you're acting reactively or proactively, and they also feed back into how you see yourself. Reactive language hands power to circumstances: "I have to", "there's nothing I can do", "he makes me so mad". Proactive language takes it back.

The big ideas

  • Reactive language is self-fulfilling. Say "that's just the way I am" often enough and you'll believe you have no choice, and then act as if you don't.
  • Swap "I must" for "I choose". "I have to go to work" becomes "I choose to go because I want to keep my job". The task stays the same; the ownership changes.
  • Love is a verb. Covey says that in the proactive sense love is something you do: sacrifices, listening, appreciating. The feeling follows the action.
  • Make and keep small commitments. The quickest way to build proactivity is to make a small promise to yourself and keep it. Each kept promise adds to your sense of control.
From the book

At one of Covey's seminars, a man came up to him and said his marriage was in trouble. He and his wife didn't have the feelings for each other they used to, and he thought he just didn't love her anymore. What should he do? "Love her," said Covey. The man repeated that the feeling wasn't there. "Then love her," Covey said again. "If the feeling isn't there, that's a good reason to love her." Love the feeling, he explained, is a fruit of love the verb: serve her, sacrifice for her, listen to her, appreciate her. Hollywood taught us that we aren't responsible for our feelings. Proactive people make love a choice.

Try it this week

For one day, catch yourself every time you say "I have to". Restate it, out loud or in your head, as "I choose to, because..."

In plain words

Watch your words. "I can't" and "they made me" give your power away; "I choose" and "I will" take it back.

Habit 1 · Work on what you can change

The Circle of Influence

In short

Picture two circles. The Circle of Concern holds everything you care about: the economy, your health, your boss's mood, the news. Inside it is a smaller Circle of Influence: the things you can actually do something about. Reactive people pour energy into the outer ring and their influence shrinks. Proactive people work inside the smaller circle, and it grows.

The big ideas

  • Energy follows focus. Worry about the weather or a colleague's flaws and you feel helpless, and your influence shrinks. Work on what you can affect and it expands.
  • Have and be. The outer ring is full of "haves": if only I had a better boss. The inner ring is full of "bes": I can be more patient, more skilled.
  • Direct, indirect, none. Some problems you control directly (your behaviour), some indirectly (other people's, by influencing them), and some not at all (the past). For the last kind, the proactive move is to accept it calmly.
  • Mistakes are in the circle too. You can't undo a mistake, but you can acknowledge it, correct it and learn from it right away. That is inside your circle.
From the book

Covey describes an executive who worked under a talented but domineering company president. The other executives spent their meetings complaining about the boss. This man didn't. He worked on what he could control: he anticipated the president's needs, did his own job brilliantly, and brought him analysis and recommendations as well as data. Over time the president came to rely on him. He was consulted on decisions the others were simply told about, and the circle of influence of a man with the same boss as everyone else grew far larger than theirs.

Try it this week

Write down your three biggest worries. For each, find one thing inside your Circle of Influence that you can do this week, and do it.

In plain words

Spend your energy on the things you can do something about. The more you act there, the more you'll be able to change.

Next: Your own funeral →
Habit 2 · Write your personal mission statement

Begin With the End in Mind

In short

The second habit is to start every big effort, including your life, with a clear picture of where you want to end up. All things are created twice: first in the mind, then in reality. If you don't write your own first creation, other people and circumstances will write it for you.

The big ideas

  • All things are created twice. A house is designed on paper before a single brick is laid. Your life works the same way: decide what it's for, then build it.
  • Leadership comes before management. Management is climbing the ladder efficiently. Leadership is making sure it's leaning against the right wall.
  • A personal constitution. A personal mission statement says what you want to be (character), what you want to do (contributions) and the values behind both. Covey suggests writing it over weeks, not in one sitting.
  • Roles and goals. Break your mission into the roles you play, such as parent, partner, manager or friend, and set a goal or two for each.
From the book

Covey asks readers to picture driving to a funeral, walking into the room and seeing friends and family gathered. As you walk to the front and look in the casket, you see your own face. This is your funeral, three years from now. There are four speakers: someone from your family, a friend, a colleague from work, and someone from your church or community. What would you want each of them to say about you? What character would you want them to have seen in you, and what difference would you want to have made? If you think hard about it, Covey says, you have found your deepest values.

Try it this week

Spend twenty minutes on the funeral exercise for real. Keep what you write and come back to it next week to turn it into a first draft of your mission.

In plain words

Decide what you want your life to stand for before you get busy living it. Picture the end, write it down, and use it to steer.

Habit 2 · What sits at the middle of your life

Your Centre

In short

Whatever sits at the centre of your life becomes your source of four things: security (your sense of worth), guidance (your sense of direction), wisdom (your perspective) and power (your ability to act). Put a person, money, work or pleasure there and your life wobbles whenever it does. Covey argues for putting principles at the centre.

The big ideas

  • Everyone has a centre. Spouse, family, money, work, possessions, pleasure, a friend, even an enemy can become the thing everything revolves around. Most of us have a mix.
  • Each centre has a weakness. Money-centred people feel worthless when money is short. Work-centred people are lost when they retire. Enemy-centred people let someone they dislike run their lives.
  • Principles don't move. Fairness, honesty, service and growth don't change with circumstances, so a principle-centred life gives steady security, guidance, wisdom and power.
  • Principles help you weigh everything else. A principle-centred person still cares about family, work and money. They just see each in balance, rather than through one of them.
From the book

Covey sets a test. You've promised to take your spouse to a concert tonight; you have the tickets and they're looking forward to it. At four o'clock, your boss asks you to stay late to help with an important meeting the next morning. A spouse-centred person goes to the concert to please their spouse, or stays at work and feels guilty. A work-centred person stays because it's good for their career. A money-centred person thinks of the overtime or a raise. A principle-centred person steps back, weighs all the needs, considers the alternatives and makes the choice they believe is best. They might go or stay, but either way the decision is theirs, and they can be at peace with it.

Try it this week

Look back over last week's big decisions. What were you really protecting each time: someone's approval, money, work, pleasure, or a principle?

In plain words

Whatever you put at the centre of your life will steer it. Put things that never change there, like honesty and fairness, and you'll stay steady when everything else shifts.

Next: Important, not urgent →
Habit 3 · The time management matrix

Put First Things First

In short

Habit 3 is about acting on Habit 2: managing your time around what matters. Covey sorts every activity by two questions, is it urgent and is it important, into four quadrants. Effective people live in Quadrant II: important but not urgent. That's where planning, relationships, prevention and growth live.

The big ideas

  • Quadrant I: urgent and important. Crises, deadlines and pressing problems. Necessary, but live here and you burn out.
  • Quadrant II: important, not urgent. Planning, prevention, relationships, learning, exercise. Nothing forces you to do them, which is why they get skipped, and why doing them pays off so much.
  • Quadrants III and IV: not important. Interruptions, some calls and meetings, busywork and time-wasters. They feel productive or relaxing, but they don't serve your mission.
  • Plan the week, not the day. Covey's method: each week, review your roles, set one or two Quadrant II goals for each, and schedule them first. Then fit everything else around them.
From the book

Covey opens the chapter by asking readers two questions. What is one thing you could do regularly, that you aren't doing now, that would make a big positive difference in your personal life? And the same question for your work. Most people know the answer straight away: exercise, a weekly date with their partner, planning, a skill they keep meaning to learn. Covey points out that these answers are almost always Quadrant II. They're important, and nothing about them is urgent, so they never happen unless you put them first.

Try it this week

On Sunday, list your roles and write one Quadrant II goal for each. Put those in your calendar before anything else goes in.

In plain words

Don't let what's loud crowd out what's important. Schedule the important-but-not-urgent things first, and you'll have fewer emergencies.

Habit 3 · Gofer delegation or stewardship

Delegate for Results

In short

To put first things first you have to let go of some things, and that means delegating to people. Gofer delegation means telling someone each step: "go for this, go for that". Stewardship delegation means agreeing on the result and letting them choose how. It takes more time up front and saves far more later.

The big ideas

  • Five parts of a stewardship agreement. Desired results, guidelines, resources, accountability and consequences. Be clear on all five and then step back.
  • Focus on results, not methods. Say what you want done and by when, not how to do it. That's what makes it theirs.
  • Let them judge themselves. In a trust-based agreement, the person reports and evaluates their own results against the standard you agreed.
  • Delegation is how you get time for Quadrant II. Doing everything yourself keeps you in Quadrant I. Delegating well frees time for what only you can do.
From the book

When Covey's son Stephen was seven, Covey handed him the yard. He took him next door and showed him the neighbour's lawn: that's "green". Then he showed him a yard with no rubbish: that's "clean". Stephen could use the sprinklers, a hose or buckets; the method was up to him. Twice a week they'd walk round together and Stephen would say how it was going. Covey would help, if he had time, whenever Stephen asked. For days Stephen did nothing. The lawn turned yellow and rubbish piled up after a barbecue. Covey bit his tongue. On the walk-round he asked how the yard was going. Stephen burst into tears: "It's so hard!" Covey asked what he wanted help with. Stephen remembered: "You said you'd help if you had time." They went round together, Stephen in charge, and from then on he kept the yard green and clean.

Try it this week

Pick one task you keep doing yourself. Hand it over this week with all five parts of a stewardship agreement written down.

In plain words

Agree on the result, the rules and the resources, then let the other person decide how. Check in, don't hover.

Part Three · Public victory

Public Victory

Habits four to six are about working with other people. Covey's claim is that trust, not technique, is what makes them work, so this part opens with how trust is built and spent before it gets to win-win, listening and synergy.

The idea behind this part

Interdependence is a choice only independent people can make. Build trust, then look for answers that are good for everyone.

Section 12 · Deposits and withdrawals of trust

Paradigms of Interdependence

In short

Before the public victory habits, Covey introduces a metaphor: the Emotional Bank Account. It's the amount of trust in a relationship. Courtesy, honesty and kept promises are deposits; rudeness, broken promises and disrespect are withdrawals. With a high balance, communication is easy and mistakes are forgiven. With an overdrawn one, every word is treated with suspicion.

The big ideas

  • Six major deposits. Understanding the individual, attending to the little things, keeping commitments, clarifying expectations, showing personal integrity, and apologising sincerely when you make a withdrawal.
  • Deposits are what the other person values. What counts as a deposit is up to them. To make one, you first have to understand what matters to them.
  • Little things are big things. Small kindnesses and small discourtesies add up. In relationships, the little things often matter most.
  • Integrity includes the absent. Loyalty to people who aren't in the room is a deposit with everyone who is: they know you'll speak about them the same way.
From the book

Covey tells of a friend whose son was obsessed with baseball. The friend had no interest in the game at all. But one summer he took his son to see every major league team play, a trip of six weeks or more that cost a lot of money. When he got back, someone asked whether he liked baseball that much. "No," he said, "but I like my son that much." That trip, Covey writes, did more for the relationship than years of talk could have, because it was a deposit in the currency the son valued.

Try it this week

Pick one relationship that feels strained and make three small deposits this week that the other person, not you, would value.

In plain words

Every relationship has a trust balance. Keep promises, be kind, apologise when you get it wrong, and you'll have trust to draw on when it's needed.

Next: Win-win →
Habit 4 · Courage and consideration

Think Win-Win

In short

Habit 4 is a frame of mind that looks for mutual benefit in every interaction. Covey lists six paradigms: win-win, win-lose, lose-win, lose-lose, win, and win-win or no deal. Win-win isn't being nice; it takes both the courage to say what you need and the consideration to care about what the other side needs.

The big ideas

  • Win-lose is the default. Many of us are trained by school grades, sports and forced rankings to think that for me to win, you have to lose.
  • Lose-win is not kindness. Giving in to keep the peace looks generous, but the buried resentment comes out later.
  • Win-win or no deal. If you can't find a solution that works for both, agree not to make a deal. It frees you from having to push, and from having to give in.
  • Character, relationships, agreements, systems. Win-win needs integrity, maturity and an abundance mentality, a trusting relationship, clear agreements, and systems that reward cooperation rather than competition.
From the book

Covey was called in by a company president who complained that his managers wouldn't cooperate. They were selfish, he said, and wouldn't work together. Covey noticed a chart on the president's wall: a horse race, with each manager's face on a horse, and at the finish line a travel poster of Bermuda. Every few weeks the president would remind his managers how much they could achieve by working together, and then point out which of them was ahead in the race for the trip. He wanted cooperation, but he'd built a system in which only one of them could win.

Try it this week

Before your next negotiation, write down what a win looks like for the other side as well as for you. Start the conversation from there.

In plain words

Look for the answer that's good for both of you. If there isn't one, it's fine to walk away. Be firm about what you need and genuinely care about what they need.

Next: Listen to understand →
Habit 5 · Empathic listening

Seek First to Understand, Then to Be Understood

In short

Most people listen with the intent to reply, not to understand. They filter everything through their own experience and jump in with advice. Habit 5 is to listen empathically first, until you truly understand the other person's view, and only then to make your own case.

The big ideas

  • Five levels of listening. Ignoring, pretending, selective listening, attentive listening, and empathic listening. Only the last gets inside the other person's frame of reference.
  • Four autobiographical responses. We evaluate (agree or disagree), probe (ask questions from our own frame), advise (give counsel from our experience) and interpret (explain them to themselves). Each pulls the conversation back to us.
  • Diagnose before you prescribe. No good doctor writes a prescription before examining you. Advice without understanding is malpractice in relationships too.
  • Then be understood. The second half takes courage: present your ideas clearly and in the context of the other person's concerns. Covey uses the Greek ethos, pathos and logos: credibility, feeling, then logic.
From the book

Covey describes going to an optometrist with blurry eyesight. After listening briefly, the optometrist takes off his own glasses and hands them over. "Put these on. I've worn this pair for ten years and they've really helped me. I have an extra pair at home; you can wear these." You put them on and everything is worse. "Well, they work great for me," he says. "Try harder." Nobody would go back to that optometrist, Covey says, yet that's exactly what we do when we listen just long enough to hand someone our own solution.

Try it this week

In your next important conversation, before you give your view, summarise theirs until they say "Yes, that's it." Then speak.

In plain words

Before you argue, advise or explain, listen until you really understand the other person. They'll be far more ready to hear you afterwards.

Next: The third alternative →
Habit 6 · Creative cooperation

Synergize

In short

Synergy is when the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. Habit 6 is what the earlier habits prepare you for: people who trust each other, think win-win and truly understand each other can treat their differences as raw material and find a third alternative that is better than either side's starting position.

The big ideas

  • Value the differences. If two people agree on everything, one of them is unnecessary. Differences are where new answers come from.
  • Three levels of communication. With low trust, communication is defensive and protective. With middling trust, it's respectful, and ends in compromise. With high trust and cooperation, it's synergistic, and the answer is better than anyone's opening idea.
  • Compromise is 1 + 1 = 1½. In a compromise both sides give something up. Synergy aims for 1 + 1 = 3 or more.
  • Work with the forces. When change is blocked, adding more driving force often just meets more resistance. Synergy works on the restraining forces, by understanding and involving the people behind them.
From the book

Covey tells of a man who had planned a family holiday at a lake: the trip was booked, the kids were excited. His wife wanted to use the time to visit her mother, who was seriously ill and lived far away. Each felt strongly. They could have argued it out, with one of them winning, or split the time and both felt short-changed. Instead they kept talking until each understood what the other really cared about, and found an option neither had started with, one that let her be with her mother while the family still had real time together. It worked better for everyone than either of the original plans.

Try it this week

Next disagreement, ask: "Would you be willing to look for a solution that's better than what either of us has in mind?" Then keep going until you find it.

In plain words

When two people disagree, don't settle for one winning or both half-losing. Keep talking until you find a third option that's better for both.

Part Four · Renewal

Renewal

The last habit keeps the other six alive. Covey ends where he began, with the inside-out approach, now as a spiral: learn, commit and do, then do it again at a higher level.

The idea behind this part

Take time to renew yourself in body, mind, heart and spirit. It is the habit that makes all the others possible.

Habit 7 · Balanced self-renewal

Sharpen the Saw

In short

Habit 7 is taking time to renew the greatest asset you have: yourself. Covey names four dimensions to look after regularly: physical, mental, social and emotional, and spiritual. It's the ultimate Quadrant II activity, and the P/PC balance applied to you.

The big ideas

  • Physical. Exercise, good food, rest and managing stress. Covey suggests a few hours a week of exercise covering endurance, flexibility and strength.
  • Mental. Reading good books, writing, planning, learning. Most of us stop deliberately educating ourselves when formal schooling ends.
  • Social and emotional. Service, empathy, synergy, a sense of security from within. This dimension is renewed mainly through your relationships, in daily life.
  • Spiritual. Your core and your commitment to your values: meditation, prayer, nature, great literature or music. Whatever reconnects you with what you care about most.
From the book

Covey describes coming across someone in the woods sawing frantically at a tree. They've been at it for over five hours, they say, and they're exhausted. The saw is blunt and the work is slow. Why not stop for a few minutes and sharpen it? "I don't have time to sharpen the saw," comes the answer. "I'm too busy sawing."

Try it this week

Schedule one hour this week for each of the four dimensions. Treat those hours as appointments you can't move.

In plain words

Look after yourself, body, mind, heart and spirit, a little every day. You'll get more done, not less.

Section 17 · The upward spiral and the transition person

Inside-Out Again

In short

Covey closes where he started. Growth moves in an upward spiral: learn, commit, do, and then learn, commit and do again at a higher level. Along the way, any of us can become a transition person: someone who stops a harmful pattern handed down through a family or an organisation, and passes something better on.

The big ideas

  • Learn, commit, do. Educating your conscience is not enough. Each turn of the spiral needs you to commit to what you've learned and then act on it.
  • Scripts get handed down. Much of how we react was learned from parents and others around us. Many of those scripts are good; some, like anger, neglect or criticism, do harm.
  • You can be a transition person. Instead of passing a harmful script on, you can absorb it and transmit something better. It changes the lives of people who will never know your name.
  • Principles are the anchor. Covey ends with his own belief that correct principles are natural laws, and that living by them is the source of lasting effectiveness.
From the book

Covey writes about a sabbatical his family took in Hawaii. Each afternoon he and Sandra rode out together on a small motorbike, found somewhere quiet and talked for hours. One day they began talking about their childhoods, and Covey found himself exploring a pattern in his own family that he had never looked at before: he understood for the first time where some of his reactions came from. The conversations went deeper than they ever had, and he describes it as one of the richest times of their marriage. It was an example of the book's whole method: becoming aware of your scripts is where rewriting them starts.

Try it this week

Name one script you inherited that you don't want to pass on. Write down what you'll do instead, and tell one person who'll notice.

In plain words

Personal change moves in a spiral of learning, committing and doing. And you can be the one who stops a bad pattern from being passed on.
