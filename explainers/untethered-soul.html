A chapter-by-chapter guide to Michael A. Singer's book

The Untethered Soul,
Explained

There is a voice in your head that never stops talking, and there is someone who hears it. Michael Singer's The Untethered Soul is about the second one. Across nineteen short chapters it asks you to notice that you are the one watching your thoughts and feelings, not the thoughts and feelings themselves, and then to practise one simple thing: when something inside you tightens, relax and let it pass instead of closing around it. This guide takes each chapter in turn, with a lab in every one where you can try the idea.

You are not the voice in your head. You are the one who hears it, and you can stop letting it run your life.

Part one · awakening consciousness

Awakening Consciousness

Singer opens with something you can check for yourself right now: there is a voice in your head that talks almost all the time, and you can hear it. If you can hear it, you can't be it. The first four chapters walk you back from the noise to the one who notices it.

The idea behind this part

You are not your thoughts, your feelings or your circumstances. You are the awareness that experiences them.

Chapter 1 · Who is listening?

The Voice Inside Your Head

In short

There is a running commentary in your head that rarely stops. It narrates, judges, worries and rehearses. Singer's first point is simple: you can hear it, so you are not it. You are the one listening.

The big ideas

  • It never really stops. The voice comments on the weather, the driver in front, what someone meant by that look. It talks even when nothing needs saying.
  • Notice that you hear it. Say "hello" silently in your head, then notice that you heard it. The listener and the voice are two different things.
  • It argues both sides. The voice can say "call her" and then "don't call her" a second later. Something that switches sides that easily isn't a reliable guide, and it isn't you.
  • Most of it is about comfort. Much of the chatter is an attempt to make the world feel controllable: predicting, explaining, rehearsing. It talks because it is uneasy.
From the book

Singer asks you to try something. Sit quietly and say "hello" inside your head a few times, then shout it inside, then whisper it. Now notice: who heard all that? The voice spoke, and you were aware of it speaking. He compares it to a running narration of a trip you are already on. You can see the trees, yet the voice still says "look at the trees". Once you notice the narrator, you notice it is almost never quiet.

In plain words

Several times today, notice the voice talking and say to yourself, "there it goes again". You don't need to argue with it or shut it up. Just notice that it is something you hear.

Chapter 2 · Would you live with this person?

Your Inner Roommate

In short

Imagine the voice in your head as a real person sharing your home, saying out loud everything it says inside. You would never put up with it. Singer uses that picture to show how much it talks, and why: it is trying to calm an inner unease.

The big ideas

  • Give it a body. Picture the voice as a roommate who follows you around talking. It worries about tomorrow, replays yesterday and comments on everyone. You would ask it to leave.
  • It talks because you are uneasy. The more disturbed you feel inside, the more the voice talks. Chatter is the mind's attempt to release that inner pressure.
  • It builds a model of the world. The voice narrates so it can feel in control: if everything is described, labelled and predicted, it feels safer.
  • Don't fight it, step back. Trying to silence the voice is just more voice. The way out is to notice it calmly, and the calmer you are, the less it has to say.
From the book

Singer suggests a test. Suppose the voice really were a person sitting on your couch. You're about to fall asleep and it starts: "Did you lock the door? What did your boss mean today? What if you get sick?" A minute later it says the opposite of what it just said. Then it starts on your partner. You'd think this person needed help, and you'd certainly not ask them for advice. Yet you listen to the same thing inside your own head all day and take it seriously.

In plain words

When the voice is loud, don't ask what it's saying. Ask what you're feeling. The chatter is a sign of inner unease, and easing that unease quiets it far better than arguing.

Chapter 3 · Peeling back the answers

Who Are You?

In short

Ask "who are you?" and most people give a name, a job, a body, a history. Singer keeps asking: are you that, or are you the one who knows that? Each answer turns out to be something you are aware of, which leaves the awareness itself.

The big ideas

  • Not your name. You could change your name tomorrow and still be you. A name is a label you notice.
  • Not your body. Your body has changed completely since you were a child, yet the one looking out through your eyes feels the same.
  • Not your thoughts or feelings. Thoughts and feelings come and go, and you notice them doing it. Something that watches them can't be them.
  • The one who sees. What's left is the plain sense of being there, aware. Singer calls it the Self, and says you have always been it.
From the book

Singer imagines a conversation. Someone asks who you are and you give your name. "No, that's just a label." You say you are the person who was born in a certain town, who did certain things. "That's your history." You point to your body. "If you lost an arm, would you still be you?" Then he asks you to remember looking in a mirror at age ten, at twenty, and now. The face changed every time. The one looking did not.

In plain words

Next time you look in a mirror, notice that you are looking. The face is something you see. You are the one seeing it, and that one hasn't aged a day.

Next: The lucid self →
Chapter 4 · Awake to being aware

The Lucid Self

In short

Your awareness can be pulled into what it looks at, the way you vanish into a film. Singer describes three things you are aware of: thoughts, feelings and the outside world. Being lucid means staying aware that you are aware while they go by.

The big ideas

  • Three kinds of object. Thoughts inside, emotions inside, and the world outside through the senses. Consciousness experiences all three.
  • Getting lost. When a thought or show grabs you completely, you forget yourself. You are still there, just absorbed, the way you forget you are in a cinema.
  • Centred behind. You can rest back, aware of the thoughts and feelings in front of you without being dragged into them. That is the seat of Self.
  • Like a lucid dream. In a lucid dream you know you're dreaming. The lucid self knows it is watching, even while life is going on.
From the book

Singer uses the picture of watching television. You sit down meaning to relax and an hour later you've forgotten the room, your body, even that you are watching. Your awareness poured itself into the screen. The same thing happens with a worry or a grudge: awareness focuses so hard on one object that it forgets itself. The practice is to notice the moment you wake up from that, and to rest there a bit longer each time.

In plain words

A few times a day, ask yourself "am I here?" You will usually find you've been lost in something. The question itself wakes you up, and that moment of waking is what lucidity feels like.

Next: Infinite energy →
Part two · experiencing energy

Experiencing Energy

Once you are watching from the seat of awareness, you start to notice something else: a flow of energy inside you that rises when you feel open and drops away when you shut down. These chapters are about that flow and the heart that controls it.

The idea behind this part

Energy is always available inside you. What cuts it off is the habit of closing your heart when something disturbs you.

Chapter 5 · Where your energy comes from

Infinite Energy

In short

You know the feeling of being so excited you don't need sleep, and the feeling of being so low you can't get out of bed. Singer says the energy for both comes from inside you. It doesn't run out. It gets blocked when you close down.

The big ideas

  • Energy rises from inside. When you fall in love or get great news, a wave of energy comes up from nowhere. It wasn't in your food. It was always there.
  • Closing blocks it. When you close down, because you're hurt, defensive or bored, the flow stops and you feel drained.
  • Opening lets it back. You don't need to get energy from somewhere. You need to stop blocking it, and opening is how.
  • Stay open, always. Singer's advice is blunt: don't close. Whatever happens, choose to stay open, and the energy keeps coming.
From the book

Singer points out how odd energy is if you think of it only as food and sleep. Someone who is falling in love can stay up all night talking and feel wonderful the next day. The same person, told something they didn't want to hear, can feel exhausted in minutes with no change in calories. Nothing physical changed. What changed was whether they were open or closed inside.

In plain words

Notice when you feel suddenly tired. Often it's right after something you didn't like. See if relaxing and opening, rather than shutting down, brings some of the energy back.

Next: The secrets of the spiritual heart →
Chapter 6 · The valve that controls the flow

The Secrets of the Spiritual Heart

In short

Singer describes the heart as an energy centre that opens and closes, a valve for the flow from chapter five. Old hurts that were never let go of are stored there and get in the way. When something touches one, the heart closes.

The big ideas

  • You feel it open and close. Love opens the heart and you feel warmth and energy in the chest. Hurt closes it and you feel tight and cut off.
  • Stored impressions. Experiences you resisted don't leave. They get stored, and Singer borrows the yogic word samskara for them: blockages in the flow.
  • Triggers stir them up. When something resembles an old hurt, the stored block stirs, and the heart closes to protect it.
  • It can be cleared. Each block you let pass instead of protecting is one less thing in the way. The heart can open more and more.
From the book

Singer describes how someone you love can open your heart just by walking in, and how the same person can shut it with one comment that reminds you of an old wound. You didn't decide to feel either way. The heart reacted to what was stored inside it. He compares the stored hurts to blockages in a pipe: energy that should flow freely meets them and backs up, and you feel that as tightness, anxiety or a wall going up.

In plain words

When you feel a sudden tightness in your chest around a person or topic, that's an old block being touched. It's information, not a command. You can let it be felt and pass.

Next: Transcending the tendency to close →
Chapter 7 · Relax when you feel it start

Transcending the Tendency to Close

In short

Closing is a habit. When something hits a sore spot, the heart starts to shut almost by reflex. Singer's practice is to notice the very start of that closing and relax into it instead, every time, until staying open becomes the habit.

The big ideas

  • Catch the first twinge. There's a moment when the closing begins: a tightening in the chest, the shoulders, the gut. That moment is where you have a choice.
  • Relax and lean in. Relax your shoulders, relax your heart, and lean into the feeling rather than away from it. Let it come up and pass.
  • Commit to not closing. Singer suggests deciding, as a lifelong commitment, that you won't close no matter what happens.
  • It gets easier. Each time you stay open, the next time is a bit easier. Each time you close, the pattern gets deeper.
From the book

Singer describes someone cutting you off in traffic. You feel a jolt, and immediately something inside starts to tighten and the voice begins: "Who does he think he is?" He says there's a gap between that jolt and the reaction that follows. If you relax right there, letting the feeling rise without adding to it, it passes through in seconds. If you don't, you'll spend the next half hour reliving it.

In plain words

Pick one recurring trigger: a person, a topic, a type of email. Next time it happens, feel for the first tightening and drop your shoulders. That's the whole practice.

Next: Let go now or fall →
Part three · freeing yourself

Freeing Yourself

Now the practical work. Singer shows how small disturbances grow into big ones if you hold on, how we build whole lives around protecting old hurts, and why being willing to feel a little pain is the price of getting free of it.

The idea behind this part

Freedom comes from letting things pass through you, early and without a fight, instead of storing them and arranging your life around them.

Chapter 8 · The longer you wait, the harder it gets

Let Go Now or Fall

In short

A disturbance starts small. If you let go right away, it's over. If you hold on, it pulls you down step by step: from a twinge, into thoughts, into words, into actions you regret. Singer's title is the whole lesson.

The big ideas

  • It starts as a twinge. First there's only a small inner reaction. At this point letting go costs almost nothing.
  • Then it grabs your thoughts. Hold on and the voice starts explaining, blaming and planning. Now you're in the story.
  • Then your mouth and hands. A bit further and you say something or do something, and now it's out in the world with consequences.
  • The choice is at the top. You don't have to understand the disturbance or fix it. You just have to let go before it takes you down.
From the book

Singer compares it to someone standing at the edge of a slope. A small slip is easy to recover from. If you keep holding on to the thing that's pulling you, you slide further and faster until you can't stop. Jealousy is his example: a small pang when your partner talks to someone. Let go there and it's gone. Hold on and an hour later you're reading their messages and starting a fight you'll be sorry for.

In plain words

You don't have to win the argument with yourself. When you notice you're upset, let go first and think later. Most of the time there won't be anything left to think about.

Next: Removing your inner thorn →
Chapter 9 · Protect it, or take it out

Removing Your Inner Thorn

In short

Imagine a thorn in your arm that hurts whenever anything touches it. You can spend your life protecting it, or you can pull it out. Singer says most of us do the first with our inner hurts, and it slowly takes over our lives.

The big ideas

  • Protecting runs your life. To avoid touching the thorn you stop sleeping on that side, avoid crowds, build a guard for your arm. Each fix shapes your life around the thorn.
  • Inner thorns work the same way. Loneliness, insecurity, a fear of rejection: we arrange our friendships, jobs and habits so nothing touches them.
  • Removing hurts, briefly. Taking out a thorn hurts once. Protecting it hurts for ever, and limits you too.
  • Let it come up. When something touches your thorn, don't rearrange the world. Let the pain come up and pass, and the thorn loosens.
From the book

Singer tells it as a story. A man has a thorn that hurts when brushed. He can't sleep, so he invents a padded device to protect his arm at night. He can't walk in crowds, so he builds a frame around himself. Soon his whole life is the device: where he can go, who he can see. Then he realises he could simply have taken the thorn out. Singer's point: people do the same with loneliness, picking partners and friends whose main job is to keep that thorn from being touched.

In plain words

Notice something you avoid because it hurts: a topic, a place, a kind of person. That avoidance is a device around a thorn. Next time, let the feeling come and go, and see if the thorn is smaller than you thought.

Next: Stealing freedom from your soul →
Chapter 10 · The self-concept you defend

Stealing Freedom from Your Soul

In short

We build a mental model of who we are and how things should go, then spend our lives defending it. Anything that doesn't fit the model hurts. Singer says this self-concept quietly steals the freedom of the one who built it.

The big ideas

  • A model of yourself. You collect thoughts about who you are, what you like and how people should treat you. Together they form a self-concept.
  • The world rarely complies. Reality isn't obliged to fit your model. Each event that doesn't fit feels like a threat and needs defending.
  • Defence costs freedom. The more you have to protect, the more of life is off limits and the more time you spend managing it.
  • You can loosen it. Notice the model as just thoughts you're holding. The fewer conditions you place on reality, the less there is to defend.
From the book

Singer notes how strange it is that someone can ruin your day by not saying good morning. Nothing happened to your body. What was touched was a picture you hold of yourself as someone who gets greeted. He describes the self-concept as built for safety: if you know who you are and how the world should behave, it feels manageable. The price is that you are now the guard of that picture, and the guard never gets a day off.

In plain words

When something small upsets you, ask "which picture of myself just got touched?" Seeing the picture is the first step to holding it more loosely.

Next: Pain, the price of freedom →
Chapter 11 · Let it hurt, and let it go

Pain, the Price of Freedom

In short

To get free of a stored hurt, you have to be willing to feel it as it leaves. Singer says pain is the price of freedom: not suffering for its own sake, but letting the discomfort pass through instead of pushing it back down.

The big ideas

  • Pain is energy passing. When a stored block is stirred, it hurts as it moves. That pain is the block on its way out.
  • Avoiding it stores it again. If you push it down, distract yourself or close around it, the block goes back into storage, ready for next time.
  • Be willing. Decide that you're willing to feel it. Willingness alone takes much of the fear out of the pain.
  • Use it as fuel. Every bit of discomfort becomes a chance to grow. Instead of asking how to stop it, ask how to let it pass.
From the book

Singer compares inner pain to a fire that burns away what's in the way. When someone rejects you and it stings, there are two moves. One is to close, blame them and avoid that kind of person in future. The other is to relax and let the sting move through you. The second hurts more at the time, he admits. But afterwards the old hurt it touched is a bit smaller, and the next rejection lands with less to catch on.

In plain words

Next time something stings, try saying silently, "I'm willing to feel this." Then relax and let it move. It usually passes faster than you expect.

Next: Taking down the walls →
Part four · going beyond

Going Beyond

Most of us live inside walls we built for safety: a comfort zone made of ideas about who we are and how things should be. These chapters are about noticing those walls, relaxing at their edges and stepping past them.

The idea behind this part

The limits you feel are mostly made of thought. Each time you relax at the edge instead of retreating, the walls give way.

Chapter 12 · The house you built for safety

Taking Down the Walls

In short

We build walls in the mind to feel safe: ideas about what we can do, where we fit and what we avoid. Inside it feels secure but it's dark. Singer says you meet the walls as discomfort at the edges, and that's exactly where to relax and keep going.

The big ideas

  • A house with no windows. Picture someone who builds a house to escape the weather, then seals every window. Safe, but they've shut out the sun as well.
  • Artificial light. Inside, you make do with substitutes: approval, distraction, small comforts. They're a poor replacement for what's outside.
  • You feel the edges. When life asks you to do something new, you feel uneasy. That unease is you touching a wall.
  • Relax at the edge. Don't retreat and don't force it. Relax into the discomfort and step through. Walls are made of thought, and they give.
From the book

Singer tells of someone so afraid of storms that they built a house, then shuttered it, then sealed the cracks. Eventually it was pitch dark inside and they wired up lamps so they could see. They became so used to the lamps that they forgot there was a sun. That, he says, is the psyche: we keep out the things that frighten us and, with them, the light. The walls aren't evil. They're just in the way now.

In plain words

The discomfort you feel before something new isn't a stop sign. It's the wall. Notice it, relax your body, and take one step anyway.

Next: Far, far beyond →
Chapter 13 · Past every edge

Far, Far Beyond

In short

Once you see that your limits are walls of thought, Singer asks you to keep going, past each edge as you meet it. There's no final wall. What you find beyond is vast, and the small worries inside look different from out there.

The big ideas

  • Don't stop at the first wall. Past one comfort zone is another. Going beyond means never treating any edge as final.
  • Your world is tiny. The things that take up your whole mind are a speck in a vast universe. That isn't a reason to despair, it's a relief.
  • Beyond is not empty. Singer describes what lies past the walls as open, alive and full of light, not a void.
  • Keep walking. The journey beyond yourself isn't a single step. You keep relaxing and moving past each edge for the rest of your life.
From the book

Singer asks you to picture your situation from further and further away. Here you are, upset about what someone said. Now see the room, the street, the town. Now the Earth spinning in space, one planet around one star among billions. From there, the thing that felt like the centre of the world is barely visible. He doesn't mean your life doesn't matter. He means your psyche has been treating a speck as the whole sky.

In plain words

When something feels huge, step back in your imagination: the room, the city, the planet. You aren't dismissing it, you're putting it in its true size.

Next: Letting go of false solidity →
Chapter 14 · Held together by effort

Letting Go of False Solidity

In short

The sense of a solid, fixed "me" is something you keep building and maintaining, thought by thought. Singer says it only feels solid because you're constantly holding it together. When you stop clutching, it loosens, and that's freedom rather than loss.

The big ideas

  • You hold it together. Your self-image takes constant work: defending, explaining, comparing. The effort is what makes it feel solid.
  • Fear of falling apart. When the structure is shaken, by a loss or a failure, it feels like you might fall apart. What's falling is the structure, not you.
  • Let it fall. Instead of rushing to rebuild, let it fall and stay present. The awareness you are doesn't fall with it.
  • What's left is light. Without the effort of holding up a self, what remains is the simple, open awareness of chapter three.
From the book

Singer describes what happens when life knocks the structure: a relationship ends, a job goes, a belief is proved wrong. Most people scramble to put the pieces back as fast as possible, because without them they don't know who they are. He suggests a different move. Sit still and watch the pieces fall. Notice that you are still here, watching. The thing that felt solid was a pile of thoughts held up by effort.

In plain words

When something shakes your sense of who you are, don't rush to rebuild. Sit with it. You'll find you're still here even while the picture of you is in pieces.

Next: The path of unconditional happiness →
Part five · living life

Living Life

The last five chapters bring it back to ordinary days: choosing to be happy without conditions, meeting each moment without resistance, using death as a teacher, avoiding extremes, and seeing the world with loving eyes.

The idea behind this part

A spiritual life isn't somewhere else. It is how you meet this moment, the next one and the one after that.

Chapter 15 · Happy, no matter what

The Path of Unconditional Happiness

In short

Ask people if they want to be happy and they say yes, then add conditions: if the job goes well, if they're treated fairly. Singer asks for a different answer: decide to be happy no matter what happens, and treat everything that comes up as something to let go of.

The big ideas

  • The question. Do you want to be happy? Really, no matter what? Most of us quietly say "yes, as long as..."
  • Conditions make it fragile. Every condition is a way for life to take your happiness away. The more you have, the more often it goes.
  • It's a commitment. Unconditional happiness isn't a feeling you wait for. It's a decision not to close, whatever happens.
  • Then practise. When something tries to take it away, you notice and let it go. Over time, the decision becomes your nature.
From the book

Singer says the biggest step on the spiritual path is a simple question with a hard answer: do you want to be happy, no matter what? Not "as long as my partner stays" or "unless I lose my job". He admits most people find this hard to say yes to, because it feels like losing the right to be upset. But that right, he says, is just a habit of closing. Once you decide, every disturbance becomes a reminder of the decision.

In plain words

Try saying it once, privately: "I want to be happy no matter what." Notice what in you objects. That objection is the next thing to let go of.

Next: The spiritual path of nonresistance →
Chapter 16 · This moment took billions of years

The Spiritual Path of Nonresistance

In short

The moment in front of you is the result of forces that have been working for billions of years. Resisting it doesn't change it, it only stores the disturbance inside you. Singer's path is to accept each moment as it is, then act from that acceptance.

The big ideas

  • Every moment is huge. For this exact moment to arrive, the whole history of the universe had to happen. It isn't personal.
  • Resistance stores it. Pushing against what has already happened doesn't undo it. It just keeps the energy in you.
  • Accept, then act. Nonresistance isn't passivity. Let the moment be, then do what needs doing with a clear head.
  • Let it pass through. Experiences that aren't resisted move through you and leave nothing behind.
From the book

Singer describes standing in a long checkout queue, getting more irritated by the minute. The queue is the end of an almost endless chain: the shop's staffing, the person in front's day, the weather, the economy, all the way back to the Big Bang. Your irritation can't touch any of it. It can only make the next ten minutes worse. Seeing the scale of what led here makes resisting it look slightly ridiculous, and makes letting it be much easier.

In plain words

When you catch yourself resisting something that's already happened, say "this is what's here now". Then ask what to do next. The acceptance comes first.

Next: Contemplating death →
Chapter 17 · The best teacher

Contemplating Death

In short

Death is certain and its timing isn't. Singer says keeping that in mind isn't morbid, it's clarifying. If you knew you had a week left, you'd stop wasting time on grudges and small worries. You can live that way now.

The big ideas

  • It could be any time. Nobody is promised tomorrow. Remembering that puts petty things in their place.
  • The one-week test. If you had a week to live, what would you stop doing, and who would you call?
  • Every moment counts. Death makes each ordinary moment precious, including the boring ones.
  • Nothing to hold on to. Since you can't keep anything, there's no reason to cling. That makes letting go easier now.
From the book

Singer imagines the angel of death appearing and saying you have one week. Suddenly the argument with your sibling doesn't matter. You don't care about the traffic. You'd want to see the people you love and look at the sky. Then he asks: why wait for the angel? You could live that week now, and the next one, without the news. People who've come close to death often say it changed how they lived. He suggests getting the lesson without the brush.

In plain words

Once a week, ask the one-week question and do one thing from your answer. It is a quick way to see what actually matters.

Next: The secret of the middle way →
Chapter 18 · Extremes swing back

The Secret of the Middle Way

In short

Singer draws on the Tao Te Ching: every extreme creates its opposite. Swing hard one way and you'll swing back just as hard. The middle, where the swing is smallest, takes the least energy and gives the most balance.

The big ideas

  • Like a pendulum. Push a pendulum to one side and it swings just as far to the other. Extremes come with a built-in return trip.
  • Diets and binges. Starve yourself and you'll binge. Work non-stop and you'll crash. The extreme creates the swing.
  • The middle is not dull. The middle isn't timid compromise. It's balance, where you have the most energy and the clearest view.
  • Notice the push. When you feel yourself pushing to one extreme, ease back toward the centre before the swing starts.
From the book

Singer uses the Tao's image of the pendulum. Someone decides to give up all sweets forever. For a week it works, then they find themselves eating a whole cake. Someone else swings from total indulgence to strict discipline and back, every few months. Each extreme took a lot of energy to reach and created the force that threw them the other way. The person in the middle eats some sweets, some days, and simply doesn't swing.

In plain words

If you notice yourself saying "never again" or "from now on, always", that's a push toward an extreme. Find the gentler middle version and do that instead.

Next: The loving eyes of God →
Chapter 19 · Seeing with love

The Loving Eyes of God

In short

Singer ends with what life looks like once you are free: you see everyone and everything with the kind of compassion a loving parent has, or as he puts it, through the loving eyes of God. Not judging, not needing anything, just seeing clearly and with care.

The big ideas

  • Judgement needs a self to defend. Most of our judging comes from the self-concept: people are good or bad depending on how they touch our thorns.
  • Love sees the whole person. When there's nothing to defend, you see why people do what they do, and compassion comes naturally.
  • Not passive. Seeing with love doesn't mean approving of everything. It means you respond without closing.
  • The end of the journey. The soul that is untethered has nothing to defend and nothing to grab. What's left is a steady, open love.
From the book

Singer asks you to imagine how a loving parent sees a small child having a tantrum. They don't take it personally. They see a tired little person who doesn't have the words for what's wrong, and they respond with care while still being firm. He says that's how you'll see everyone when you've let go of enough: the rude colleague, the difficult relative, yourself. Not because they deserve it, but because there's nothing in you left that needs them to be different.

In plain words

Pick one person who irritates you and try seeing them the way you'd see a tired child. You don't have to agree with them. Just notice what changes in you.

Afterword

Relax, release, repeat

Singer's whole practice fits in one move you can make a hundred times a day: notice the tightening, relax your shoulders and your heart, and let the energy pass through before it builds into a story. Keep a small log of the moments you caught. What you write stays in this browser only.
