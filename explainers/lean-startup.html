A chapter-by-chapter guide to Eric Ries's book

The Lean Startup,
Explained

Eric Ries co-founded the virtual-world company IMVU and learned, the expensive way, that most new products fail not because they're built badly but because nobody wants them. The Lean Startup borrows ideas from Toyota's factories to offer a different method: treat every plan as a set of guesses, test the riskiest ones with a minimum viable product, measure honestly, and decide on purpose whether to pivot or persevere. This guide covers the introduction, all twelve chapters across the book's three parts and the epilogue, with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

Don't ask whether it can be built. Find out, as cheaply and quickly as you can, whether it should be.

Part one · vision

Vision

Ries opens by redefining what a startup is and what counts as progress inside one. A startup is not a smaller version of a big company, and it is not a free-for-all either. It is an experiment, and it needs a kind of management built for not knowing the answer.

The idea behind this part

The point of a startup is to learn what customers really want, as fast and as cheaply as possible, and to prove that learning with real data.

Introduction · Why most startups fail, and what to do about it

Introduction

In short

Most startups don't fail because the product is badly built. They fail because they build something nobody wants. Ries offers a method for finding out early, built on five principles.

The big ideas

  • Two bad stories. Startups tend to fall into one of two traps. Some write a detailed plan and follow it faithfully, as if the future were known. Others reject planning altogether and just do it, trusting instinct. Neither works when nobody yet knows who the customer is or what they will pay for.
  • Entrepreneurs are everywhere. You don't need a garage. Anyone building something new under real uncertainty is an entrepreneur, whether in a two-person company, a corporate department, a charity or a government agency.
  • Entrepreneurship is management. A startup is an institution, not just a product, so it needs management. But it needs a kind suited to extreme uncertainty, not the kind built for running a known business.
  • Validated learning. Startups exist to learn how to build a sustainable business. That learning can be tested by running experiments, so progress can be measured.
  • Build, measure, learn. The core activity is turning ideas into products, measuring how customers respond, and learning whether to change course or carry on. All the processes in the book exist to speed up that loop.
  • Innovation accounting. To hold innovators to account, you need a new kind of accounting: how to measure progress, set milestones and decide what to work on next when the usual numbers like revenue are still tiny.
From the book

Ries had been through failure before IMVU. As a young programmer he co-founded a dot-com company that ran out of road, and he later worked at a startup that spent years building a virtual world in secret before launch. At IMVU, which he co-founded in 2004 and where he was chief technology officer, the team tried the opposite: ship early, watch customers, change quickly. The lessons from that company, and from applying ideas from Toyota's factories to software, became the method in this book.

Try it this week

Name one project you're on where nobody yet knows who the customer is. Write down how you'd find out in two weeks instead of six months.

In plain words

If the problem and the solution are both known, plan it and run it well. If either is unknown, your real job is to learn which guesses are wrong, and you should organise everything around learning that quickly.

Chapter 1 · A startup needs a steering wheel

Start

In short

Ries named the method after lean manufacturing at Toyota. Instead of a rocket launch plan that has to be right from the start, a startup should steer like a car: adjusting all the time based on what it sees.

The big ideas

  • Lean manufacturing. Toyota's production system, built up by Taiichi Ohno and Shigeo Shingo, changed how things are made: small batches, just-in-time stock, using the knowledge of every worker, and treating anything that doesn't add value for the customer as waste.
  • A new kind of progress. In a factory, progress means making high-quality physical goods. In a startup, the unit of progress is learning: finding out what is true about customers and the business.
  • Rocket or car. Many startups plan like a rocket launch. Every assumption is set in advance, so a small error at the start becomes a huge miss later. A car has a steering wheel instead: the driver keeps a destination in mind and corrects all the time.
  • Vision, strategy, product. The vision is where you want to go and rarely changes. The strategy, meaning the business model and the route, can change; when it does, that's a pivot. The product changes all the time as you optimise it.
  • Why plans fail here. General management grew up in a world of stable markets and long track records where forecasting works. A startup has no history to forecast from, so forecasts look precise and are mostly guesswork.
From the book

Ries describes walking into many companies that had followed the textbook: a solid plan, a good team, a launch on schedule, and then silence. Customers didn't come, and the plan had no instructions for what to do next. The car analogy is his answer. A driver heading somewhere new doesn't calculate the whole trip at the start; she looks out of the windscreen and turns the wheel every few seconds. For a startup, the windscreen is customer data and the wheel is the Build-Measure-Learn loop.

Try it this week

For one plan you're following, list the three assumptions it depends on most. Put a date in the diary to check each one against reality.

In plain words

Keep the destination fixed and the route flexible. Check where you really are often, and correct a little each time, rather than betting everything on a plan drawn up before you knew anything.

Chapter 2 · Who, exactly, is an entrepreneur?

Define

In short

Ries defines a startup as a human institution designed to create a new product or service under conditions of extreme uncertainty. Nothing in that definition says how big, how old or what sector.

The big ideas

  • Human institution. A startup is more than a product or an idea. It is people, processes and culture: hiring, coordinating, building a team that can do something new.
  • New product or service. The word is used broadly: any source of value for customers, including a new business model, a new way of reaching people or a new use for an old technology.
  • Extreme uncertainty. This is the key part. A new restaurant that copies a proven model is a small business, not a startup in this sense. If you can forecast the market, you're not in startup territory.
  • Startups inside big companies. Many of the book's entrepreneurs work at large companies. They need the same methods as founders, plus protection from the parent organisation's usual rules.
From the book

Ries tells the story of SnapTax, a small team inside Intuit, the large company behind TurboTax and QuickBooks. The idea was to let people photograph their tax form on a phone and file a simple return in minutes. The team was set up to work like a startup: a small group with the freedom to experiment directly with customers, rather than going through Intuit's usual long planning cycle. Ries also describes how Intuit's founder, Scott Cook, pushed the whole company toward rapid experiments, to the point where the TurboTax team was running hundreds of tests in a single tax season.

Try it this week

Check whether your own work is "extreme uncertainty" or a known model. If it's known, borrow a proven playbook. If it isn't, plan for experiments.

In plain words

Being a startup is about uncertainty, not size or age. A team in a giant firm, a charity or a government office can be one. A new branch of a known chain is not.

Chapter 3 · Validated learning

Learn

In short

At IMVU, Ries spent months building features customers turned out not to want. The work that mattered was what the team learned. Validated learning means proving what you've learned with real customer behaviour.

The big ideas

  • The add-on nobody wanted. IMVU began as a 3D avatar add-on for existing instant messaging networks, so people could bring their current friends. The team spent months making it work across several networks.
  • Customers had other ideas. In sessions with real users, people didn't want to invite their friends into something unproven. They were happy to use it with strangers. And they were happy to download a new, standalone messenger. The plan's central assumption was backwards.
  • "At least we learned something" isn't enough. Every failed project claims lessons. Validated learning is different: it is backed by data, shown in improved numbers on the things that matter, rather than in a story told afterwards.
  • Which work is waste? Ries asks a hard question of his own team: if the goal was to learn what customers wanted, could they have learned it with far less effort? Mostly, yes. The rest of the work, however well done, didn't create value.
From the book

Ries tells the story against himself. He had written much of the code that let IMVU's avatars talk across other messaging networks, and he was proud of it. When customers made it clear that this was not what they wanted, the team switched to a standalone product, and most of that code was thrown away. What bothered him was not the lost code but the realisation that a few cheap experiments early on would have revealed the same thing. After the switch, IMVU's numbers began to improve, and that improvement was the evidence the learning was real.

Try it this week

Ask what the cheapest way would be to learn the main thing you're trying to learn right now. Could you do it this week?

In plain words

Progress in a startup is learning, and learning only counts when customers' behaviour proves it. Build the least you need to find out, find out early, and treat anything that doesn't teach you something as waste.

Chapter 4 · Value and growth hypotheses

Experiment

In short

Treat your strategy as a set of hypotheses and test them. The two most important are the value hypothesis (does this deliver value once people use it?) and the growth hypothesis (how will new customers find it?).

The big ideas

  • Start with the riskiest part. Instead of building the whole thing, ask which assumption, if wrong, would sink everything. Design the smallest experiment that tests it with real customers.
  • The value hypothesis. Will people actually get value from the product? Ask them to do something, not just say something: pay, sign up, come back.
  • The growth hypothesis. How will the product spread from early users to many? Word of mouth, repeat use, paid advertising? Test that too, early.
  • Experiments are products. A good experiment is also the first version of the product. If it works, you've started; if it fails, you've learned cheaply.
From the book

Ries retells how Zappos began. Its founder, Nick Swinmurn, wanted to test whether people would buy shoes online at a time when most people assumed they wouldn't. Instead of building warehouses and stock, he went to local shoe shops, photographed their shoes and put the pictures on a simple website. When someone ordered a pair, he went back to the shop, bought them at full price and posted them. It lost money on each sale, but it answered the key question with real customers paying real money. Zappos was later bought by Amazon for over a billion dollars.

Try it this week

Write your value hypothesis and your growth hypothesis in one sentence each. Then name the cheapest test that would prove the first one wrong.

In plain words

Don't argue about whether an idea is good; test it. First check that people really value it, by what they do rather than what they say. Then check how it will spread.

Part two · steer

Steer

Once a startup has a vision, it has to steer toward it. These four chapters walk around the Build-Measure-Learn loop: name the riskiest assumptions, build the smallest thing that tests them, measure honestly, and then decide whether to change course or keep going.

The idea behind this part

Every product is an experiment. Find the leap-of-faith assumptions, test them with a minimum viable product, and let the numbers decide whether you pivot or persevere.

Chapter 5 · Leap-of-faith assumptions

Leap

In short

Every business plan rests on a few assumptions that must be true for it to work. Find these leap-of-faith assumptions, test the riskiest first, and go and see customers for yourself.

The big ideas

  • Not every assumption is equal. Most parts of a plan have plenty of precedent. A few don't, and if they're wrong the whole plan collapses. These are the leaps of faith, and they deserve almost all of your early attention.
  • Analogs and antilogs. Look for past examples that support your assumption and ones that argue against it. Ries borrows this from Randy Komisar: the Sony Walkman showed people would carry music around, and Napster showed they'd download it; neither showed they'd pay for downloads. That was the iPod's leap of faith.
  • Go and see. The Toyota principle genchi gembutsu means going to the place where the problem is, rather than reading reports about it. Steve Blank calls it getting out of the building.
  • Customer archetypes. Early contact with customers should produce a clear picture of who you're serving, a working sketch that guides design and changes as you learn.
  • Beware analysis paralysis. Research can turn into another way to avoid testing. At some point you have to build something and put it in front of people.
From the book

For the 2004 redesign of the Sienna minivan, aimed at North America, Toyota put Yuji Yokoya in charge, an engineer with little experience of that market. Rather than study reports, he drove existing models through all fifty US states, all of Canada's provinces and territories and much of Mexico. He noticed things like how roads, winds and parking differed, and that in America it was children, not parents, who spent most of the time in the back. That understanding shaped a far more successful van. Ries also notes that when Facebook raised early money, the striking number was not size but engagement: more than half its users came back every day, evidence for its leap of faith about value.

Try it this week

List the assumptions in your plan and circle the two with the least evidence. Arrange to watch three real customers this week, in their setting.

In plain words

A plan is a stack of guesses, and a few of them carry all the weight. Find those, look for evidence on both sides, and go and see customers in person before you build much.

Chapter 6 · The minimum viable product

Test

In short

A minimum viable product is the version that lets you complete a full turn of the Build-Measure-Learn loop with the least effort. It is often far smaller, and rougher, than founders are comfortable with.

The big ideas

  • Minimum for learning. An MVP is not the smallest product you could sell. It is the quickest way to start learning: anything extra that doesn't help you test the leap of faith is waste, however useful it looks.
  • The video MVP. Before building a tricky product, show what it would do and measure whether people want it.
  • The concierge MVP. Serve a handful of customers personally, by hand, to learn exactly what they need before you automate anything.
  • The Wizard of Oz MVP. Customers think they're using a working product, but people behind the curtain are doing the work. You learn whether it's wanted before building the technology.
  • Quality is a guess too. If you don't yet know who the customer is, you don't know what they'll count as quality. Early adopters forgive rough edges if the core is right. Be careful about legal and safety limits, and about your brand; you can launch under a different name.
From the book

Dropbox's founder Drew Houston had a hard product to demonstrate: file syncing that worked seamlessly across computers, which needed a lot of engineering before a prototype could be shown. So he made a short video of it working, full of in-jokes for the early-adopter tech community he was aiming at. The beta waiting list jumped from about five thousand people to seventy-five thousand almost overnight. In Austin, Manuel Rosso's Food on the Table started with one customer, whom he served in person each week, building her shopping list and meal plan by hand. And Aardvark, a question-answering service later bought by Google, ran early versions in which people answered the questions behind the scenes.

Try it this week

Strip your next release down to the one assumption it tests. Ask what you'd build if you had to learn the answer in a week.

In plain words

Build the least you need to learn whether your big assumption is true. A video, a hand-run service or a product with humans behind it is often enough, and much faster than the real thing.

Chapter 7 · Innovation accounting

Measure

In short

To know if you're making progress, you need innovation accounting: set a baseline with your MVP, tune the product toward an ideal, then decide whether to pivot or persevere. And measure with actionable metrics, not vanity ones.

The big ideas

  • Three steps. First, use an MVP to measure where you really are. Second, run experiments to move the numbers toward where the business needs them. Third, if they won't move enough, pivot.
  • Vanity metrics. Totals like registered users or page views tend to go up and to the right whatever you do. They make a team feel good and tell it nothing about whether its work made a difference.
  • Cohorts. Look instead at each new group of customers on its own: of the people who joined this month, how many signed up, used it, paid, came back? If those rates don't change, your work isn't working.
  • Split tests. Show two versions to different customers at the same time and compare their behaviour. It's the most direct way to see cause and effect.
  • The three A's. Good metrics are actionable (they show cause and effect), accessible (everyone can understand them) and auditable (anyone can check them against real customers).
From the book

At IMVU, the overall charts looked encouraging for a long time: more registered users, more revenue each month. But when the team broke the numbers into monthly cohorts, the rates at which new customers moved through the funnel barely changed, despite months of new features. The team had been busy, not effective. Ries describes a similar moment at Grockit, an online learning company that followed agile development by the book. When it switched to cohort metrics and split tests, it found that many features it had been sure of made no difference to how students behaved.

Try it this week

Take your headline number and ask: if we'd done nothing last month, would it still have gone up? If yes, find a cohort or split-test number instead.

In plain words

Totals going up can hide a product that isn't improving. Measure each new group of customers separately, test changes against a control, and use numbers everyone can understand and check.

Chapter 8 · Changing course without losing the vision

Pivot (or Persevere)

In short

A pivot is a structured change of course designed to test a new hypothesis about the product, strategy or engine of growth. Your runway is best measured not in months but in how many pivots you have left.

The big ideas

  • Hold regular meetings to decide. Ries recommends a scheduled pivot-or-persevere meeting, every few weeks or months, where the team looks at the data and decides deliberately, rather than drifting.
  • The land of the living dead. The worst outcome is not failure but limbo: a product that gets just enough traction to keep going and never enough to succeed. Teams stay there because pivoting feels like admitting defeat.
  • Runway is pivots. Cash divided by burn gives months. What matters more is how many chances to change course that buys. Getting to each decision faster gives you more chances with the same money.
  • A catalogue of pivots. Ries lists kinds of pivot: zoom in (one feature becomes the product), zoom out, customer segment, customer need, platform, business architecture, value capture, engine of growth, channel and technology.
From the book

Ries follows David Binetti, who started Votizen to help verified voters connect with each other about politics. Each version taught him something, and the numbers kept falling short. Rather than drift, he pivoted several times, changing which customers he served and what the product did, until he arrived at a simple service that let people send messages to their representatives in Congress. Each pivot kept the vision, helping citizens take part in politics, and changed the strategy. Ries also notes that IMVU's move from an add-on to a standalone messenger was itself a pivot.

Try it this week

Put a date in the calendar for a pivot-or-persevere meeting. Decide now which numbers you'll look at, and what result would make you change course.

In plain words

Decide on purpose, on a schedule, whether your strategy is working. If it isn't, change one part of it and test again. The faster you can get to each decision, the more chances your money buys.

Part three · accelerate

Accelerate

The faster a team gets around the loop, the faster it learns. Ries borrows from lean manufacturing to show how small batches, the right engine of growth, a habit of asking why, and protected space for experiments let a company speed up without losing its way.

The idea behind this part

Speed comes from small batches and fast feedback, and it lasts only if the organisation learns from its mistakes and protects its experiments.

Chapter 9 · Small batches

Batch

In short

Doing work in small batches feels slower and is usually faster. It finds problems sooner, wastes less when you're wrong and lets you learn from customers continuously.

The big ideas

  • Envelope stuffing. Fold all the letters, then stuff them all, then seal them all, and it feels efficient. Doing one complete letter at a time is often faster overall, and you discover at once if the letters don't fit the envelopes.
  • Problems show up immediately. In big batches a mistake hides until late, by which time it's been repeated hundreds of times. In small batches it shows up on the first one.
  • Continuous deployment. IMVU shipped changes to its live product as many as fifty times a day, with automated tests and a system that stopped and rolled back if anything broke. Each change was small, so each problem was easy to find.
  • Stop the line. Toyota's andon cord lets any worker stop production to fix a problem at its source. In software, a broken test or a fall in key numbers should do the same.
  • The large-batch death spiral. When batches grow, problems get worse, so teams make them bigger still to be safe, and releases slow to a crawl. Pull work from customer demand instead of pushing out a big plan.
From the book

Ries borrows a story from James Womack and Daniel Jones's book Lean Thinking. Asked to help stuff newsletters into envelopes, two children wanted to do it the obvious way: fold every newsletter first, then stuff them all, then seal and stamp them. Doing them one at a time from start to finish turned out to be faster, even though each step had to be switched more often, because nothing piled up waiting and any problem showed up on the first envelope rather than the last. For a startup, the same logic means getting each small change to customers quickly so it can be measured.

Try it this week

Take the biggest piece of work on your list and cut it into a slice that a customer could see within a week.

In plain words

Do work in small pieces and get each piece to the customer quickly. You'll find mistakes earlier, waste less when you're wrong and learn faster, even if it feels less efficient.

Next: Grow →
Chapter 10 · Engines of growth

Grow

In short

Sustainable growth means new customers come from the actions of past ones. Ries describes three engines that can drive it, sticky, viral and paid, and suggests focusing on one at a time.

The big ideas

  • Where new customers come from. Word of mouth, a side effect of use (others see you using it), paid advertising funded by revenue, and repeat purchase. Growth from one-off stunts doesn't count.
  • The sticky engine. Products that keep customers for a long time grow when they gain new customers faster than they lose existing ones. Watch the churn rate.
  • The viral engine. Products that spread through use grow when each customer brings in, on average, more than one new customer. That number is the viral coefficient, and above one it compounds.
  • The paid engine. Products that buy customers grow when what a customer is worth over their lifetime is more than it costs to acquire them. The gap pays for the next customers.
  • Product/market fit, and running out. Every engine eventually runs out of fuel as a market saturates. Ries borrows Marc Andreessen's idea of product/market fit, and warns that a startup may need to switch engines later.
From the book

Ries retells how Hotmail grew. Its founders added a single line to the bottom of every email sent from the service, a postscript saying, in effect, "Get your free email at Hotmail", with a link. Each message was an advertisement, so every user who sent email recruited more users without trying. Hotmail grew to millions of accounts quickly and was bought by Microsoft. It's the textbook viral engine: the product spreads as a side effect of being used.

Try it this week

Decide which engine your product is really meant to run on. Find the one number that tells you whether it's running, and check it this month.

In plain words

Know which engine you're running and watch its key number: churn against new customers for a sticky product, the viral coefficient for a viral one, lifetime value against acquisition cost for a paid one.

Chapter 11 · The Five Whys

Adapt

In short

To go fast without breaking things, a company needs to adapt its processes to what's actually going wrong. The Five Whys find the human root of a problem, and you invest in a fix in proportion to how much it hurts.

The big ideas

  • Ask why five times. Taiichi Ohno's technique: when something goes wrong, keep asking why until you reach the cause behind the cause. Technical problems usually lead back to a human or process problem.
  • A proportional investment. At each level of the chain, make a fix in proportion to the harm. Small problems get small fixes. If the problem keeps coming back, the investment grows.
  • The curse of the five blames. Asked badly, the Five Whys turn into finding someone to blame. Ries's rules: everyone affected should be in the room, and if a mistake happens, the system made it easy, so fix the system.
  • First time, any mistake; never the same one twice. Be tolerant of mistakes the first time, and use the process to make sure the same one doesn't happen again.
  • Start small. Use it for a narrow class of problems at first, with one person running it, so it builds trust before it's used everywhere.
From the book

Ries walks through an example from IMVU. A new release disabled a feature for customers. Why? A particular server failed. Why? An obscure subsystem had been used the wrong way. Why? The engineer who used it didn't know how to use it properly. Why? He had never been trained. Why? His manager didn't believe in training new engineers, because the team was too busy. What started as a technical fault ended with a management habit, and the fix included both a repair to the server and a change to how new engineers were brought up to speed.

Try it this week

Next time something goes wrong at work, ask why five times with everyone involved, and make one small fix at every level.

In plain words

When something breaks, keep asking why until you find the human cause. Fix each level a little, more if it keeps happening, and blame the system, not the person.

Next: Innovate →
Chapter 12 · Startups inside big companies

Innovate

In short

Established companies can innovate if they give internal startups three things: scarce but secure resources, independent authority to build, and a personal stake in the result. A sandbox lets them experiment without putting the core business at risk.

The big ideas

  • Scarce but secure resources. A startup team needs a small budget that can't be raided by the rest of the company. Too much money can be as harmful as too little.
  • Independent authority. The team must be able to build, ship and test without asking permission at every step. Handing off to other departments slows the loop to a stop.
  • A personal stake. Something has to tie the team to the outcome: equity, a bonus, or at the very least public credit as the people behind it.
  • The innovation sandbox. Rules that let any team run a real split test on a limited part of the product, for a limited time and a limited number of customers, judged on a standard set of metrics, and stopped if something goes badly wrong.
  • Entrepreneur as a job title. Ries argues that companies should treat entrepreneurship as a career track, with its own accountability, rather than a side project for whoever has spare time.
From the book

Ries describes the problem from the parent company's side. A healthy organisation has something like an immune system: managers who rightly protect the brand, the customers and the revenue. Put a startup inside it with no boundaries and that immune system attacks every unfamiliar experiment. Hide the startup away completely and it learns nothing the company can use. The sandbox is his middle path: the experiments are real and reach real customers, but they're contained, timed and measured on terms everyone has agreed in advance.

Try it this week

If you're innovating inside an organisation, write down your sandbox: which customers, how long, which metrics, and what would make you stop.

In plain words

Give internal startups a small, protected budget, the freedom to ship on their own and a stake in the outcome. Agree on the boundaries of their experiments so the rest of the company can relax.

Next: Waste not →
Epilogue

Waste Not

Ries ends by stepping back. A century ago, scientific management changed how work was done by measuring it. The new challenge, he argues, is not doing work efficiently but knowing which work is worth doing at all.

The idea behind this part

The biggest waste is building something nobody wants. Lean thinking is a way of finding that out before you've spent everything.

Chapters 13–14 · Epilogue

Waste Not, and Join the Movement

In short

A century ago, Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management made work efficient by measuring it. Ries argues the next step is to apply the same rigour to deciding what work is worth doing, and he invites readers to join a movement doing just that.

The big ideas

  • Taylor's revolution. In 1911 Taylor's The Principles of Scientific Management argued that work could be studied and improved systematically. It made the twentieth century's productivity boom possible, and also its rigid, top-down workplaces.
  • Efficiency in the wrong place. We have become very good at building things efficiently. Ries's worry is that much of that efficiency goes into products nobody wants, which is the biggest waste of all.
  • Not pseudo-science. Lean Startup is not about running a few tests to justify what you already wanted to do. Its experiments have to be able to prove you wrong.
  • Join the movement. Ries points readers to the community of practitioners, meetups and resources that grew up around the ideas, and asks them to treat their own work as an experiment too.
From the book

Ries closes with a question he hopes readers will take with them: what if, instead of asking whether something can be built, we asked whether it should be built, and whether we can build a sustainable business around it? Taylor asked how to do a task most efficiently. The Lean Startup asks how to avoid doing tasks that don't need doing at all. Answering it, he argues, is the job of every entrepreneur, manager and team working under uncertainty.

Try it this week

Look at last week's work and mark anything that neither delivered value to a customer nor taught you something you can prove. That's the waste to cut first.

In plain words

Being efficient at building the wrong thing is still waste. Before you ask how to build something faster, ask whether you've proved it should be built at all.
