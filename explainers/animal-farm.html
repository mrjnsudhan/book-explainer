A chapter-by-chapter guide to George Orwell's book

Animal Farm,
Explained

In 1945 George Orwell published a short fable about farm animals who drive out their drunken farmer to run the farm for themselves, only to watch the pigs slowly become the masters they replaced. A child can follow the story, and it is also a close allegory of the Russian Revolution and Stalin's rise. Orwell, a democratic socialist who had seen Soviet-backed communists turn on their own allies in Spain, wanted to show how a revolution for equality can be betrayed one small edit at a time. This guide covers all ten chapters and Orwell's preface on the freedom of the press, with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

Power rarely overthrows a revolution. It edits it, a few words at a time, until the new masters look just like the old ones.

Part one · the dream and the rebellion

The Dream and the Rebellion

Orwell's fable opens on a run-down English farm, where an old boar tells the animals that their misery is not natural but man-made. Within months they have driven the farmer out, renamed the farm and painted their new rules on the barn wall. Almost everything that goes wrong later is already there in seed: the pigs who do the thinking, and the milk that disappears.

The idea behind this part

A revolution starts with a true complaint about injustice. Whether it stays true depends on who holds the paintbrush afterwards.

Chapter 1 · Why the animals are miserable

Old Major's Dream

In short

On Manor Farm, once the drunken Mr Jones has gone to bed, the animals gather in the big barn to hear Old Major, a prize boar near the end of his life. He tells them their lives are short, hungry and full of work not because nature made them so, but because Man takes almost everything they produce. One day, he says, there will be a Rebellion.

The big ideas

  • A diagnosis, not a plan. Major says no animal in England is free: they are worked until their strength runs out and then slaughtered. He blames a single creature, Man, the only animal that consumes without producing. He gives no date or method for the Rebellion, only a direction.
  • Never become like Man. Major warns that in fighting Man the animals must never copy him: no living in a house, sleeping in a bed, wearing clothes, drinking alcohol, smoking, trading or touching money. All animals are equal, and no animal must ever rule over another. Every one of these warnings is broken by the end of the book.
  • The rats vote. Mid-speech, the dogs spot some rats and give chase. Major calls a vote on whether wild creatures like rats are comrades. A large majority say yes; only the three dogs and the cat vote against, and the cat, it turns out, has voted on both sides. Equality is already harder in practice than in a speech.
  • A song to carry the dream. Major remembers a song from his piglet days, Beasts of England, about a golden future without Man. The animals learn it at once and sing it so loudly that they wake Jones, who fires his gun into the dark. The song becomes the Rebellion's anthem.
From the book

Major is twelve years old, a Middle White boar who was shown at fairs under the name Willingdon Beauty. He has had a strange dream and wants to pass on what he has learned before he dies. He describes the farm as a place where every animal knows hunger and exhaustion: the cows' milk goes to Jones's table, the hens' eggs are sold at market, and the mare Clover's foals are sold off before they are grown. Boxer, the huge, gentle cart-horse, will be sold to the knacker the day his great muscles give out. Even Major, for all his prizes, will one day meet the butcher's knife. Three nights later he dies peacefully in his sleep, and is buried at the foot of the orchard.

The history behind it

Orwell said the book was about the Russian Revolution and what became of it. Old Major mixes Karl Marx, whose ideas inspired the revolution, with Lenin, who led it and died soon after. Mr Jones, careless and drunk, stands for the last Tsar, Nicholas II. Beasts of England plays the part of the Internationale, the socialist anthem.

In plain words

Major is right about the farm: the animals do all the work and get very little. His speech is the hope every revolution starts with. Keep an eye on his list of things never to do, because the rest of the book is the story of each one being done.

Chapter 2 · Animalism and the Seven Commandments

The Rebellion

In short

The cleverest animals, the pigs, turn Major's speech into a system called Animalism. When Jones forgets to feed the animals one Midsummer's Eve, they break into the store-shed and drive him and his men off the farm. They rename it Animal Farm and paint seven rules on the barn wall.

The big ideas

  • The pigs take the lead. Teaching falls to the pigs, generally agreed to be the cleverest animals. Snowball is lively and inventive. Napoleon, a large, fierce-looking boar, talks little but has a reputation for getting his own way. Squealer is a brilliant talker who, the others say, could turn black into white.
  • Distractions from the cause. Mollie, the vain white mare, wants to know whether there will still be sugar and ribbons. Moses, Jones's tame raven, tells of Sugarcandy Mountain, a paradise animals go to when they die. Boxer and Clover, slow to think things out for themselves, become the pigs' most faithful disciples.
  • An accidental revolution. The Rebellion comes sooner and more easily than anyone expected. Jones, drunk in Willingdon, leaves the animals unfed. When they break into the store-shed and his men set about them with whips, they fight back, and within minutes the men are running down the road.
  • Rules on the wall. Snowball paints the Seven Commandments on the end wall of the big barn, in white letters on tarred wood, as an unalterable law: two legs bad, four legs or wings good, no clothes, no beds, no alcohol, no killing other animals, and all animals are equal.
From the book

The morning after the Rebellion the animals race to the top of the knoll to gaze at the farm that is now theirs. They throw the knives, nose-rings, whips and reins into the fire, and burn the ribbons too, since ribbons count as clothes. They tiptoe through the farmhouse in awe and agree to keep it as a museum that no animal will ever live in. Snowball, who has taught himself to read from an old spelling book of the Jones children's, paints out MANOR FARM on the gate and paints in ANIMAL FARM. Then the cows, unmilked all day, begin to low in pain. The pigs milk them, and five buckets of frothing milk stand in the yard. Napoleon tells everyone to go and start the harvest and not to worry about the milk. When they come back in the evening, it has gone.

The history behind it

Snowball stands for Leon Trotsky, the brilliant organiser of the Red Army, and Napoleon for Joseph Stalin. Squealer is the Soviet propaganda machine, especially the official newspaper Pravda. Moses the raven, who preaches of heaven and is later welcomed back by the pigs, is usually read as the Russian Orthodox Church.

In plain words

The animals win their freedom almost by accident, and they set it down in seven short rules anyone can learn. The question is who did the writing. The very same evening, the first thing goes missing: the milk.

Part two · the young republic

The Young Republic

For a while Animal Farm works. The harvest is the best the farm has known, everyone learns what they can, and the animals beat off the farmer's attempt to take it back. Underneath, the pigs quietly take the milk and the apples, and a slogan replaces the rules for those who can't read.

The idea behind this part

Early success hides early privilege. The first small exception is the one that makes all the later ones possible.

Chapter 3 · Who gets to read, and who gets the milk

The Harvest and the Apples

In short

The first harvest under the animals is the best the farm has known. Everyone works, Boxer hardest of all. Snowball sets up committees and reading classes, but most animals barely learn the alphabet, so he boils the Commandments down to one slogan. Meanwhile the pigs keep the milk and the windfall apples for themselves.

The big ideas

  • Boxer's answer. Boxer has a cockerel wake him half an hour before everyone else, and answers every problem with the same words: I will work harder. Benjamin the donkey, the oldest animal on the farm, works as slowly and steadily as before and, asked whether things are better now, says only that donkeys live a long time.
  • A ladder of reading. The pigs learn to read and write perfectly. The dogs read fairly well but care only for the Commandments; Muriel the goat reads scraps of newspaper; Benjamin reads as well as any pig and chooses not to. Clover learns the alphabet but can't put words together, Boxer never gets past D, and Mollie learns only the letters of her own name.
  • Four legs good, two legs bad. For the animals who can't read, Snowball reduces the seven rules to a single maxim that, he says, holds the essence of Animalism. The sheep love it and bleat it for hours. A slogan is easier to learn than a law, and much easier to bend.
  • Brainworkers need apples. When the animals find that the milk and windfall apples go to the pigs, Squealer explains it isn't selfishness: pigs are brainworkers, science shows they need these foods, and if the pigs failed in their duty, Jones would come back. Nobody wants that, so nobody argues.
From the book

Napoleon takes little interest in Snowball's committees, such as the Egg Production Committee for the hens, the Clean Tails League for the cows and the Whiter Wool Movement for the sheep. He says the education of the young matters more than anything that can be done for the grown animals. When the dogs Jessie and Bluebell give birth to nine puppies, he takes them away as soon as they are weaned, saying he will see to their education himself. He keeps them in a loft reached only by a ladder from the harness-room, and the rest of the farm soon forgets they exist. The chapter ends with Squealer's speech about the milk and apples, which every pig, Snowball included, has agreed to. Two quiet decisions, about puppies and about food, will decide the farm's future.

The history behind it

The pigs' privileges mirror the rise of a new Party elite in the Soviet Union, with better food, housing and shops than the workers it ruled in their name. Boxer echoes the cult of the tireless worker, later celebrated in the Stakhanovite movement of the 1930s.

In plain words

The farm works because everyone pulls together, but knowledge doesn't spread evenly. The pigs can read, write and argue; most animals can only remember a slogan. That gap is where the pigs' first privileges come from, and every later one.

Chapter 4 · Defending the farm, and who gets the credit

The Battle of the Cowshed

In short

News of the Rebellion spreads, and neighbouring farmers fear their own animals will catch the idea. In October Jones and his men come back with sticks and a gun. Snowball, who has studied Julius Caesar's campaigns, springs an ambush and the men are routed. Snowball and Boxer are decorated as heroes.

The big ideas

  • Two nervous neighbours. Pilkington of Foxwood is an easy-going gentleman farmer whose land is overgrown and neglected. Frederick of Pinchfield is tough and shrewd, and always in a lawsuit. They dislike each other too much to agree on anything, but both spread stories that the animals on Animal Farm are starving and fighting among themselves.
  • Snowball's plan. Snowball sends the pigeons and geese first to harass the men, then the sheep and goats, then fakes a retreat that draws the men into the yard. There the horses, cows and pigs burst out of the cowshed behind them. Within minutes the men are fleeing down the road.
  • The cost of victory. Snowball is wounded by Jones's gun and a sheep is killed. Boxer strikes a stable-lad who lies still in the mud, and Boxer is grief-stricken: he never wanted to take a life, even a human one. The lad, it turns out, was only stunned, and he slips away.
  • Medals and a gun. The animals create a military decoration, Animal Hero, First Class, for Snowball and Boxer, and Second Class for the dead sheep. Jones's gun is set at the foot of the flagstaff, to be fired twice a year: on the anniversary of the battle and on Midsummer Day, the anniversary of the Rebellion.
From the book

Mollie is missing during the battle and is found hiding in her stall with her head buried in the hay. The fight is named the Battle of the Cowshed, since that is where the ambush was sprung. Notice where Napoleon is during the fighting: the chapter doesn't mention him at all. Snowball leads the final charge himself and is hit, with stripes of blood along his back where the pellets struck. Hold on to this scene, because in later chapters Squealer will tell it very differently.

The history behind it

The battle stands for the Russian Civil War (1918 to 1921), when the White armies, helped by foreign powers, tried to overturn the revolution and were beaten by the Red Army that Trotsky built. Pilkington and Frederick stand for the capitalist powers, roughly Britain and Nazi Germany.

In plain words

The animals defend their farm bravely, and the plan that wins is Snowball's. Napoleon isn't in the story. Remember that, because the history of this battle is about to be rewritten, twice.

Part three · napoleon takes over

Napoleon Takes Over

Two pigs argue about everything, above all about a windmill. When Napoleon cannot win the argument he ends it, with nine dogs he has raised in secret. Then come the long working weeks, trade with humans, a fallen windmill blamed on an absent enemy and, at last, confessions and executions in the farmyard.

The idea behind this part

Power that can't win an argument abolishes the argument, and then needs an enemy to explain every failure.

Chapter 5 · The debate that ended debate

Snowball and the Windmill

In short

Snowball and Napoleon disagree about everything, above all about Snowball's plan for a windmill to bring electricity and a three-day week. On the day of the vote, Napoleon lets out nine huge dogs he has raised in secret, and they chase Snowball off the farm. Napoleon abolishes the Sunday debates, and soon announces that the windmill was his idea all along.

The big ideas

  • Mollie leaves. Mollie is seen letting a man from Foxwood stroke her nose, and Clover finds sugar and ribbons hidden under the straw in her stall. Soon she vanishes, and is later seen between the shafts of a smart little cart outside a pub, with a ribbon in her forelock, looking contented. No one mentions her again.
  • Two campaigns. Snowball, with plans chalked over the floor of an old shed, promises electric light, heating and a three-day week. Napoleon says the farm needs food now, and one day urinates over the plans. The farm splits into two slogans: vote for Snowball and the three-day week, or vote for Napoleon and the full manger.
  • Agreeing with the last speaker. The pigs split on defence too. Napoleon says the animals must get guns and learn to use them; Snowball wants to send out pigeons to stir up rebellion on other farms. The animals listen to one and then the other, and find themselves agreeing with whichever one spoke last.
  • The dogs. Just as Snowball's speech is winning the room, Napoleon gives a high-pitched whimper and nine enormous dogs in brass-studded collars rush in and chase Snowball out through a hole in the hedge. They are Jessie's and Bluebell's puppies, now grown, and they wag their tails at Napoleon as the old dogs once did at Jones.
From the book

Napoleon then announces that the Sunday morning meetings are over. From now on a special committee of pigs, with him in the chair, will settle all questions in private and hand down its decisions. Four young pigs start to protest, the dogs growl, and the sheep break into Four legs good, two legs bad for a quarter of an hour, until the chance to talk has passed. Boxer, troubled, takes on a second maxim: Napoleon is always right. Three weeks later Napoleon announces that the windmill will be built after all. Squealer explains that Napoleon never really opposed it; Snowball had stolen the plans from Napoleon's own papers. Pretending to oppose it was a clever move to get rid of a dangerous character. Tactics, comrades, tactics.

The history behind it

This is Stalin's victory over Trotsky in the 1920s. Trotsky argued for spreading revolution abroad, Stalin for socialism in one country; Trotsky was expelled from the Party, exiled in 1929 and murdered in Mexico by a Soviet agent in 1940. The dogs are the secret police, and the windmill echoes the Five-Year Plans for rapid industrialisation, which Stalin adopted after defeating the rivals who had argued for them.

In plain words

Napoleon can't out-argue Snowball, so he removes Snowball and the arguing. Then he takes Snowball's best idea as his own. Once the people who disagree are gone, a leader can claim anything, including their work.

Chapter 6 · Work, trade, beds and a scapegoat

The Fallen Windmill

In short

All year the animals work like slaves on the windmill: a sixty-hour week, plus Sunday afternoons that are strictly voluntary. Napoleon starts trading with humans through a solicitor, Mr Whymper, and the pigs move into the farmhouse and sleep in the beds. When a November gale knocks the half-built windmill down, Napoleon blames Snowball.

The big ideas

  • Voluntary work. Napoleon announces Sunday afternoon work, entirely voluntary, but any animal that stays away will have its rations cut by half. Boxer, as strong as three horses, drags boulders up the quarry slope and adds a little extra work, alone, before the others are up and after they have gone to bed.
  • Trade with the enemy. Napoleon decides to sell hay, wheat and, later, the hens' eggs, to pay for things the farm can't make. The animals have a vague memory of resolutions against trade and money; Squealer assures them no such resolution was ever written down. Whymper, a sly little solicitor, visits every Monday.
  • With sheets. The pigs move into the farmhouse and sleep in the beds. Clover asks Muriel to read her the fourth commandment, which now says no animal shall sleep in a bed with sheets. Squealer explains: a bed is just a place to sleep; the rule was against sheets, a human invention, and the pigs have taken them off.
  • Snowball did it. After the storm, Napoleon sniffs the ground, declares that Snowball crept in by night and wrecked the windmill out of spite, and sentences him to death. He offers a medal and apples to any animal who brings Snowball to justice. Pig footprints are found leading from a gap in the hedge.
From the book

The windmill is far harder to build than anyone imagined. There is plenty of stone in the quarry, but no way to break it without picks and crowbars, which animals can't hold. They solve it by dragging huge boulders to the top of the quarry with ropes and toppling them over the edge to shatter below. Boxer does more than anyone, and is once nearly dragged back down the slope by a falling boulder. By late summer the windmill is half built. Then, on a stormy November night, the wind shakes the farm buildings, and in the morning the windmill lies in ruins. Napoleon paces in silence, and then, in a terrible voice, names the culprit: Snowball.

The history behind it

Stalin blamed the failures of the 1930s on wreckers and saboteurs, often said to be working with the exiled Trotsky. The trade through Whymper mirrors the Soviet Union's dealings with capitalist countries in the 1920s and 1930s.

In plain words

Napoleon now does several things the animals once swore against, and each is explained away. When something goes wrong he blames someone who isn't there to answer. A scapegoat turns every failure into proof that the leader is needed.

Chapter 7 · Hunger, a hens' strike and the purge

The Confessions

In short

Through a bitter winter the half-starved animals rebuild the windmill with thicker walls, while Napoleon hides the hunger from the outside world. He orders the hens to give up their eggs for sale, and when they rebel he starves them into surrender. Then Snowball is blamed for everything, animals confess to plotting with him, and the dogs kill them in front of the whole farm.

The big ideas

  • Fooling Whymper. To stop word getting out that the farm is short of food, Napoleon has the nearly empty bins in the store-shed filled almost to the brim with sand, topped with the last of the grain and meal, and lets Whymper walk past them. The trick works: the outside world hears that Animal Farm has no food shortage.
  • The hens' rebellion. Told to give up four hundred eggs a week, the hens fly up to the rafters and lay their eggs there, so they smash on the floor. Napoleon stops their rations and orders that any animal who gives them so much as a grain of corn will be punished by death. After five days and nine dead hens, they give in.
  • Snowball everywhere. Every broken window and blocked drain is now Snowball's work. Squealer announces that Snowball was Jones's secret agent from the start and tried to lose the Battle of the Cowshed. Boxer says he doesn't believe it; when told that Napoleon himself has said so, he accepts it. A few days later, dogs go for Boxer at a meeting.
  • The executions. Napoleon calls the animals together. Four pigs, the ones who protested when the debates were abolished, confess to plotting with Snowball, and the dogs tear their throats out. Then hens, a goose and sheep confess to crimes of their own, and are killed too, until there is a pile of bodies at Napoleon's feet.
From the book

Afterwards the shaken animals huddle on the knoll that looks out over the farm. Clover gazes down at the fields in the spring evening light and thinks this is not what they hoped for on the night Major spoke. She had pictured a society where the strong protected the weak, as she once sheltered a brood of lost ducklings with her foreleg. Instead, nobody dares to say what they think, and fierce dogs roam everywhere. She has no thought of rebelling; things are still better than under Jones. The animals begin to sing Beasts of England, slowly and sadly. Squealer arrives to say the song is now banned. The Rebellion is complete, he explains, so a song about a better future is no longer needed. A new song by the poet pig Minimus takes its place.

The history behind it

The confessions and executions recall the Moscow show trials of 1936 to 1938, where leading Bolsheviks confessed to absurd crimes, often to plotting with Trotsky, before being shot. The hens' revolt echoes the peasants who resisted the forced collectivisation of farms, which helped cause the famines of 1932 and 1933, including the Holodomor in Ukraine.

In plain words

Fear spreads silence faster than force does. Once a few animals die for speaking, everyone else learns to keep quiet, and banning the old song removes even the memory of what they hoped for.

Part four · some are more equal

Some Are More Equal

The rules on the barn wall shift a few words at a time, the figures keep improving while the troughs stay empty, the most loyal worker is sold to the knacker, and finally the pigs stand up on two legs. The last scene, pigs and men at one table, is one of the best-known endings in English fiction.

The idea behind this part

Betrayal rarely arrives all at once. It is edited in a word at a time, and accepted because each step looks small.

Chapter 8 · Figures, forged notes and whisky

The Battle of the Windmill

In short

After the executions, Clover thinks the sixth commandment forbade all killing, and finds it now says without cause. Squealer reads out figures showing production soaring while the animals stay hungry. Napoleon sells timber to Frederick for banknotes that turn out to be forged; Frederick's men attack and blow the windmill up. The pigs celebrate the victory with whisky, and another commandment changes.

The big ideas

  • Figures in place of food. Squealer reads out long lists showing that production of every kind of food has gone up by two hundred, three hundred or five hundred per cent. The animals see no reason to doubt him, especially as they can no longer clearly remember what things were like before the Rebellion.
  • Napoleon, far away. Napoleon rarely appears in public now, and when he does it is with his dogs and a black cockerel who trumpets before he speaks. He is given grand titles such as Father of All Animals and Terror of Mankind. Minimus writes a poem in his praise, which is painted on the barn wall beside his portrait.
  • Two buyers, one deal. Napoleon plays Pilkington and Frederick off against each other over a pile of timber, finally selling it to Frederick, who must pay in banknotes rather than a cheque. The notes turn out to be forged. Days later Frederick's men attack and blow the windmill to pieces with blasting powder.
  • A victory, of sorts. The animals drive the men off at a terrible cost: a cow, three sheep and two geese are killed and nearly everyone is wounded. Squealer calls it the Battle of the Windmill and a great victory. The animals aren't sure what they have won, but the gun is fired and Napoleon awards himself a new decoration.
From the book

A few days after the battle, the pigs find a case of whisky in the farmhouse cellar. That night loud singing comes from the house, and Napoleon is seen galloping round the garden in Jones's old bowler hat. Next morning Squealer announces gravely that Napoleon is dying; his last order, it is said, is that drinking alcohol shall be punished by death. By evening he is better, and the following day he asks Whymper for books on brewing and distilling. A few nights later there is a loud crash in the yard. The animals find Squealer sprawled beside a broken ladder at the end wall of the barn, with a lantern, a paintbrush and an overturned pot of white paint. Only Benjamin seems to understand, and he says nothing. Some days later Muriel reads the fifth commandment: no animal shall drink alcohol to excess.

The history behind it

The timber deal with Frederick is the 1939 pact between Stalin and Hitler, which Germany broke when it invaded the Soviet Union in 1941. The ruined windmill stands for the devastation of that war, and the costly victory for the enormous Soviet losses in winning it.

In plain words

When the facts are bad, Napoleon changes the facts the animals see: the figures, the titles, the history and the words on the wall. Each step is small. Together they leave the animals unable to trust their own memory.

Chapter 9 · The faithful worker and the knacker's van

Boxer's Last Load

In short

Boxer's split hoof is slow to heal, but he works on, hoping to see the windmill finished before he retires. Rations are cut for everyone except the pigs and dogs, while the farm is declared a Republic with Napoleon its only candidate. When Boxer collapses, a van comes to take him, the pigs say, to hospital. Benjamin reads what is painted on its side.

The big ideas

  • Readjusted, not reduced. Squealer explains that rations aren't being cut, only readjusted, and proves with figures that the animals have more oats, hay and turnips than in Jones's day, work shorter hours, drink better water and live longer. The animals believe him.
  • Spontaneous Demonstrations. Napoleon orders weekly processions to celebrate the farm's struggles and triumphs. The animals march in formation and listen to songs, speeches and more of Squealer's figures. Many find it comforting to be reminded that, whatever else, they are now their own masters.
  • The Republic and the raven. In April the farm is proclaimed a Republic and Napoleon is elected President, the only candidate, unanimously. Moses the raven returns after years away, talking of Sugarcandy Mountain again. The pigs call his stories lies, yet let him stay and give him a daily allowance of beer.
  • The pension that never came. The animals had agreed retirement ages, twelve for a horse, with a pension of grain and talk of a corner of the pasture for retired animals. So far, no animal has actually retired. Boxer, close to twelve, plans to spend his retirement learning the rest of the alphabet.
From the book

One summer evening Boxer falls while hauling stone for the windmill and cannot get up. Squealer announces that Napoleon has arranged for him to be treated at the hospital in Willingdon. Two days later a closed van arrives while the animals are at work. Benjamin, who has never been seen to gallop, gallops into the yard braying for everyone to come, and reads out the lettering on the van: it belongs to a horse slaughterer and glue boiler. The animals run after it shouting to Boxer to get out. His face appears at the small window, and they hear his hooves drumming on the inside of the van, but his strength is gone. Three days later Squealer tearfully reports that Boxer died in hospital, praising Napoleon to the end. The van, he says, had been bought from the knacker by the vet, who had not yet painted out the old name. Soon afterwards a crate of whisky is delivered to the farmhouse.

The history behind it

Boxer stands for the loyal working class, whose labour built the new state and whose trust it betrayed. Orwell's first readers might also have seen Moses' return in Stalin's decision in 1943, in the middle of the war, to let the Orthodox Church reopen when religion became useful to the regime.

In plain words

Boxer gives everything and believes what he is told, and the regime uses up his trust and sells what is left. His answer to every problem, to work harder, makes him the perfect servant of whoever is in charge.

Chapter 10 · From pig to man

Some Animals Are More Equal

In short

Years pass. Most animals who remember the Rebellion are dead, and so is Jones. The farm is richer, but only the pigs and dogs are better off. Then the pigs come out walking on their hind legs, and the wall holds a single commandment: all animals are equal, but some are more equal than others. At a dinner with the neighbouring farmers, the animals look from pig to man and can't tell them apart.

The big ideas

  • The windmill, at last. The windmill is finished, but it is used to mill corn for profit, not for the electric light and short working week Snowball promised. Napoleon now says the truest happiness lies in working hard and living frugally.
  • Paperwork. Squealer explains that the pigs work very hard at things called files, reports, minutes and memoranda: big sheets of paper covered in writing which are then burnt in the furnace. The animals can't say whether life is better than under Jones. Only Benjamin claims to remember everything, and says things never were, and never will be, much better or worse.
  • Four legs good, two legs better. Squealer spends a week alone with the sheep, teaching them a new song. When Napoleon walks out of the farmhouse on his hind legs, a whip in his trotter, and the animals are about to protest, the sheep burst into Four legs good, two legs better, and drown out any chance of objection.
  • One commandment. Clover asks Benjamin to read the wall to her. Only one commandment is left: all animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others. After that, nothing seems strange: the pigs buy a wireless and a telephone, take out newspaper subscriptions and wear Jones's clothes.
From the book

Pilkington and other farmers are invited to tour the farm, and that evening they drink and play cards with the pigs in the farmhouse. Pilkington makes a toast: he once feared Animal Farm, but now sees that its animals work harder and eat less than any in the county, a lesson for every farmer. The pigs, he jokes, have their lower animals to deal with, just as the farmers have their lower classes. Napoleon replies that the farm will go back to its old name, the Manor Farm, that animals will no longer call each other comrade, that the hoof and horn will be taken off the flag, and that Major's skull, once set up for the animals to file past, has been buried. Outside, the animals creep up to the window to watch. Then a furious quarrel breaks out: Napoleon and Pilkington have each played an ace of spades at the same moment. The animals look from the pigs to the men and back again, and can no longer tell which is which.

The history behind it

The closing dinner recalls the Tehran Conference of 1943, where Stalin met Churchill and Roosevelt. Orwell later explained that he meant the ending to strike a note of discord, not harmony, because he expected the wartime friendship to break down, as it did in the Cold War. The two aces of spades suggest both sides are cheating.

In plain words

By the end the pigs have become everything Major warned against, and the farm is run much as it was under Jones, by a different species. The revolution didn't fail in one moment; it was traded away rule by rule, until the new rulers looked just like the old ones.

Behind the Book

Animal Farm nearly wasn't published. Orwell wrote a preface about why, which went missing for a quarter of a century, and a second preface for Ukrainian readers explaining where the story came from.

The idea behind this part

The greatest danger to free speech, Orwell argued, is not the censor's pen but our own fear of saying the unfashionable thing.

Appendix · Orwell's two prefaces

The Freedom of the Press

In short

Orwell wrote a preface for Animal Farm about the trouble he had getting it published, but it was left out and only found and printed in 1972. In it he argues that in wartime Britain the threat to free speech came less from the government than from writers and publishers who would not criticise the Soviet Union, then a popular ally. A second preface, for Ukrainian readers in 1947, explains where the story came from.

The big ideas

  • Turned down. Orwell finished the book in early 1944. Victor Gollancz, his usual publisher, refused it. Jonathan Cape accepted it, then backed out after an official at the Ministry of Information advised against it. Faber and Faber, where T. S. Eliot read it, turned it down too. Secker and Warburg finally published it in August 1945.
  • Fear, not law. Orwell's point is that nobody banned the book. The pressure was quieter: an unwritten agreement among respectable people that certain things were not said, at a time when the Soviet Union was fighting Hitler alongside Britain. Unpopular ideas, he wrote, can be silenced without any need for force.
  • Liberty means the unwelcome. The preface's best-known line says, in effect, that if liberty means anything at all, it means the right to tell people what they don't want to hear. Defending free speech only for views you already share, he argues, is not defending it at all.
  • A boy and a cart-horse. In the Ukrainian preface, Orwell says the idea came to him after watching a small boy whip a huge cart-horse along a narrow path. If animals ever became aware of their strength, he thought, we would have no power over them; and men exploit animals much as the rich exploit the poor.
From the book

Orwell had seen the problem first-hand. In 1937 he fought in the Spanish Civil War with a small left-wing militia, the POUM, which the Soviet-backed communists then hunted down as traitors; he and his wife only just escaped from Spain. Back in England, he found that much of the left-wing press would not print what he had seen. That, he said, showed him how easily propaganda in a free country could shape what people believed about the Soviet Union. Animal Farm was his attempt to break that myth in a story anyone could follow and that would translate easily into other languages. When it finally appeared, in August 1945, the war in Europe was over and the mood was turning. The first printing sold out within weeks, and the book made Orwell famous.

The history behind it

Orwell remained a democratic socialist all his life. Animal Farm attacks a dictatorship that called itself socialist, not the idea of equality. The Ministry of Information official who advised Cape, Peter Smollett, was later revealed to have been a Soviet agent. Orwell followed Animal Farm with Nineteen Eighty-Four in 1949, and died of tuberculosis in January 1950, aged 46.

In plain words

A book can be silenced without anyone banning it. Orwell's warning is about all of us: about the subjects we quietly avoid because raising them would cost us friends, readers or standing.

The book's lesson as a habit: write down the promises that people with power make to you, word for word, and read them again later. Betrayal is rarely announced. It is painted in at night, a few words at a time.
