Part one · the story

Where I'm Coming From

Before any rules, Dalio tells the story of how he earned them: a kid buying his first stock, a young founder who was spectacularly wrong in public, and the decades of writing down what he learned each time reality punished him.

The idea behind this part

Principles are not handed down from on high. They are what is left over after you have been wrong enough times and taken the trouble to work out why.

The story · How a bad bet became a method

Where I'm Coming From

In short

Dalio's career is a string of hard lessons. The biggest came in 1982, when he bet publicly on a depression and was badly wrong. Being humbled taught him to ask "How do I know I'm right?" instead of "Am I right?"

The big ideas

  • Started young. He bought his first stock at twelve with money from caddying, and he was hooked on markets from then on. He founded Bridgewater from his two-bedroom apartment in New York in 1975.
  • The big miss. In 1982 he was so sure a debt crisis would cause a depression that he said so in front of Congress and on television. Instead the US economy and stock market began a long boom. He lost so much he had to let his staff go and borrow $4,000 from his father.
  • From "Am I right?" to "How do I know?" Instead of quitting, he changed his approach. He went looking for the smartest people who disagreed with him, so he could see what he was missing, and he wrote down his decision rules so they could be tested.
  • The Holy Grail. He found that a handful of good bets that do not move together is far safer than one great bet. He calls this the Holy Grail of investing, and it became the basis of Bridgewater's funds.
From the book

On the trading floor in August 1971, a young Dalio watched the US cut the dollar's link to gold. He was sure stocks would crash. They jumped instead. It was the first of many lessons that the future rarely matches what you expect, and that being surprised is a signal to go back and study how the machine really works.

In plain words

Dalio's whole book grows out of one painful year. He was confident, public and wrong. What he took from it was not "be less ambitious" but "be more humble about what you know". Seek out people who disagree, test your thinking, and spread your bets so that no single mistake can knock you out.

Part two · life principles 1 to 5

Life Principles

Five big principles for running your own life: accept reality as it is, use a five-step loop to get what you want, open your mind, understand how differently people are built, and make decisions well.

The idea behind this part

Reality does not care how you wish it worked. The people who do best learn its rules faster than everyone else and stop fighting the ones they cannot change.

Life Principle 1 · See the world as it is

Embrace Reality and Deal with It

In short

Truth, meaning an accurate picture of how things really work, is the foundation of every good outcome. Face it, especially when it hurts, and treat pain as a signal that there is something to learn.

The big ideas

  • Be a hyperrealist. Dreams matter, but only when matched with a clear view of reality and the determination to act. Dalio sums this up as: dreams plus reality plus determination make a successful life.
  • Pain + Reflection = Progress. Pain is a message that you have hit a limit or made a mistake. If you avoid it, you repeat the mistake. If you reflect on it, you learn a principle and come out stronger.
  • Look to nature. Nature is not good or bad by our standards; it optimises for the whole, through evolution. The useful question is not "Is this fair?" but "How does this work, and how do I deal with it?"
  • Own your outcomes. Whatever happens to you, you are the one who must deal with it. Complaining about how reality should be changes nothing. Asking what you could do differently changes a lot.
  • Weigh second- and third-order effects. The first effect of a choice is often the opposite of its later ones. Exercise hurts today and helps for years; junk food is the reverse. People who look further ahead win.
From the book

Dalio describes life as a series of loops. You go after something, hit a wall, feel the pain, reflect, and come out with a better principle. Then you go after something bigger and hit a new wall. Done well, the loops climb upward like a spiral. Done badly, you go round the same loop again and again.

In plain words

When something hurts, the natural move is to look away. Dalio says that is the worst thing you can do. Painful moments are the cheapest teachers you will ever get, as long as you stop, ask why it happened, and write down what you would do next time.

Next: The five-step process →
Life Principle 2 · A loop for getting what you want

Use the Five-Step Process to Get What You Want Out of Life

In short

Set clear goals, find the problems in the way, diagnose their root causes, design a plan, then push it through. Repeat. Most people are strong at one or two steps and weak at others; success comes from covering the weak ones.

The big ideas

  • 1. Have clear goals. Choose what you really want, knowing you cannot have everything. Do not confuse goals (the destination) with desires (things that feel good now but may pull you off course).
  • 2. Find problems and don't tolerate them. Problems are obstacles between you and your goal. Treat each one as a puzzle. Do not shrug it off just because it is hard or because it has been there for a while.
  • 3. Diagnose to the root cause. Ask why the problem happened until you reach the real cause, often a habit, a skill gap or a bad design. Fixing the surface without the root means it will come back.
  • 4. Design a plan. Picture who will do what, when, to get past the root cause. A good plan reads like a film script.
  • 5. Push through to completion. Do the work. Know which tasks move you toward the goal and do them reliably, even the dull ones.
From the book

Dalio admits he has always been good at imagining and designing, and bad at the routine follow-through. Rather than pretend otherwise, he paired himself with people who were strong where he was weak. His point: weaknesses do not matter much if you find a way around them, but they matter a lot if you deny them.

In plain words

This is the engine of the whole book. Know what you want, notice what is stopping you, find out why, plan around it, and do the plan. Then check the result and go round again. If one step is your weak spot, do not just try harder; bring in someone who is good at it.

Next: Be radically open-minded →
Life Principle 3 · Get out of your own way

Be Radically Open-Minded

In short

Two barriers block good decisions: your ego, which hates being wrong, and your blind spots, which you cannot see. Getting past both means sincerely believing you might not know the best answer, and asking people who see things differently.

The big ideas

  • The ego barrier. Deep, emotional parts of the brain treat being wrong as a threat. So when someone challenges us, we feel attacked and defend instead of listen.
  • The blind spot barrier. Everyone's mind sees some things well and misses others. You cannot fix what you cannot see, so you need people whose minds work differently from yours.
  • Thoughtful disagreement. The goal of a disagreement is not to win but to find out what is true. Approach it curious about why the other person sees it differently.
  • Triangulate. Check your view with several believable people who are willing to disagree with you. If they all see it differently, take that very seriously.
  • Know the signs. Closed-minded people mostly make statements; open-minded people ask a lot of questions. Closed minds want to be proven right; open minds want to get it right.
From the book

Dalio describes a "higher-level you" that can watch yourself think, and a "lower-level you" driven by quick emotional reactions. When you feel a flash of anger at being challenged, he suggests treating it as a cue: that is the lower-level you defending its ego. Pause, and let the higher-level you ask what you might be missing.

In plain words

Being open-minded is not having no opinions. It is holding your opinion while seriously asking, "What if I'm wrong?" The fastest way to learn is to find smart people who disagree with you and genuinely try to understand their reasons.

Next: People are wired differently →
Life Principle 4 · Know how you and others are built

Understand That People Are Wired Very Differently

In short

People are born with different brains and are shaped by different experiences. Knowing how you and others think, using honest feedback and good personality tests, lets you put each person where they can shine.

The big ideas

  • Different, not better. Some people see the big picture, others the details. Some are creative, others reliable. Most conflict at work is between people who are simply wired differently and do not realise it.
  • Thinking versus feeling brain. There is a constant tug of war between our reasoning and our emotions. Good habits help the reasoning side win more often.
  • Habits are powerful. Doing something repeatedly turns it into a habit, and habits eventually feel natural. Choose them deliberately, and train the emotional you with kindness and persistence.
  • Find out what you are like. Dalio uses personality assessments at Bridgewater to understand how people think. They are not perfect, but they are far more objective than gut feeling.
  • Shapers. He describes rare people he calls Shapers, who can picture something new and actually build it, pushing through doubts and obstacles.
From the book

Dalio tells how, early on, he assumed people disagreed with him because they were stubborn or not paying attention. Personality tests showed him something else: they were literally seeing different things. That changed how he built teams. Instead of expecting everyone to think like him, he started matching people's strengths to the jobs that needed them.

In plain words

The people you clash with are often not wrong, just built differently. Once you know your own style and theirs, you stop expecting a detail person to brainstorm or a dreamer to check spreadsheets, and you can build teams where everyone's strengths cover someone else's gaps.

Worth knowing

This lab borrows the broad idea of team roles from one of the assessments Dalio mentions. It is a quick illustration, not a real test. Personality tests in general are useful for starting conversations but are not precise measurements.

Next: Make decisions well →
Life Principle 5 · Learn first, then decide

Learn How to Make Decisions Effectively

In short

Good decisions come in two steps: first learn what is true, then decide what to do. Keep emotions from hijacking the process, look beyond the first consequences, and treat each decision as a bet with odds and payoffs.

The big ideas

  • Emotions are the biggest threat. Strong feelings, like fear, anger or pride, can override reason. Notice them and give your thinking brain time to catch up.
  • Learning comes before deciding. Most bad decisions are made on a poor picture of reality. Synthesise what is really going on before you choose.
  • Look through time. Ask what happens next, and after that. The first-order effects of a choice are often the opposite of the later ones.
  • Think in expected value. Every decision is a bet. Multiply how likely each outcome is by how good or bad it would be. A small chance of a huge payoff can be worth taking; a small chance of ruin rarely is.
  • Use the 80/20 rule. A few things matter most. Get those right and do not lose time perfecting the rest.
From the book

Dalio compares good decision makers to good chess players: they look several moves ahead. He also describes writing his decision criteria down as rules and then turning them into computer models, so that his own logic could be tested against history and run without the emotions of the moment.

In plain words

Before deciding, slow down and get the facts straight. Then ask what happens next, and after that. Many choices that feel great now cost a lot later, and many that hurt now pay off for years.

Next: Radical truth →
Part three · work principles 1 to 6

Get the Culture Right

Dalio describes an organisation as a machine with two main parts, its culture and its people. The first six work principles are about culture: say what you really think, learn from mistakes, disagree well, and weigh opinions by who has earned the right to be heard.

The idea behind this part

An idea meritocracy is a place where the best idea wins, not the boss's idea or the loudest one. That only works if people can see the truth and are allowed to say it.

Work Principle 1 · Say what you really think

Trust in Radical Truth and Radical Transparency

In short

Say what you really believe, to people's faces, and let people see what is going on. There is nothing to fear from the truth; problems you cannot see are the ones that hurt you.

The big ideas

  • Nothing to fear from the truth. Knowing what is really happening, including what people think of you, lets you deal with it. Hidden problems grow.
  • Integrity means one story. Do not say things about people you would not say to them directly. Talking behind backs poisons trust.
  • Everyone can make sense of things. People have the right, and the duty, to question things that do not make sense to them, whatever their level.
  • Radical transparency. At Bridgewater most meetings are recorded and available to staff. Seeing how decisions are really made builds trust and lets people learn.
  • Share the hardest things. The things that are hardest to say are usually the most important. Keep exceptions to transparency to a minimum.
From the book

After a meeting with an important prospective client, a colleague emailed Dalio a frank review: he had rambled, been disorganised, and done poorly, and the colleague graded him close to failing. Others were copied in. Instead of being offended, Dalio asked the others in the meeting to grade him too. He says that kind of honest feedback, from anyone to anyone, is what keeps people improving.

In plain words

Most workplaces run on polite fictions. Dalio's runs on honesty. That can feel harsh at first, but it means problems get found early, people know where they stand, and nobody wastes energy on gossip.

Worth knowing

Bridgewater's culture is famously intense. Dalio himself acknowledges that many new hires find it too much and leave early. Radical transparency works best with clear norms about tone and with people who have chosen it.

Next: Meaningful work and relationships →
Work Principle 2 · Why people stay

Cultivate Meaningful Work and Meaningful Relationships

In short

The best places to work combine a mission people care about with relationships that feel like family. Each makes the other stronger, and both are worth protecting.

The big ideas

  • Be loyal to the mission. Loyalty should go to the shared mission, not to individuals who are not living up to it.
  • Be crystal clear on the deal. Make sure everyone knows what they are giving and getting. Fair deals avoid resentment.
  • Be more generous than fair. Dalio suggests giving others more consideration than you demand for yourself. Generosity builds deep relationships.
  • Watch size. As organisations grow, meaningful relationships get harder to keep. Small teams inside large organisations help.
  • Treat people who matter like family. When people feel truly cared for, they are willing to hear hard truths, because they know where they come from.
From the book

Dalio calls Bridgewater's culture "tough love". The toughness is the high standards and honest feedback; the love is the care for people. He argues you need both. Toughness without care feels brutal; care without toughness produces a pleasant team that does not improve.

In plain words

People do their best work when they believe in what they are doing and trust the people they are doing it with. Dalio says these are not nice extras but the core of a great organisation.

Next: Learning from mistakes →
Work Principle 3 · Make mistakes useful

Create a Culture in Which It Is Okay to Make Mistakes and Unacceptable Not to Learn from Them

In short

Mistakes are part of how everyone, and every organisation, evolves. Hiding them is the real failure. Log them openly, look for patterns, and learn.

The big ideas

  • Mistakes are natural. Anyone doing hard things will make them. Dalio says the people who make the most progress are the ones who are most comfortable looking at their own.
  • Don't feel bad, learn. Beating yourself up wastes energy. Ask what the mistake teaches and what you will do differently.
  • Look for patterns. One mistake is a data point. The same mistake again and again is a pattern that usually points to a weakness or a bad design.
  • Accurate, not blame. Get past "blame" and "credit" and focus on "accurate" and "inaccurate". The aim is to fix the machine, not punish the person.
  • Use an issue log. At Bridgewater, every mistake or problem goes in a shared log. Not logging a problem you saw is treated as worse than causing one.
From the book

Dalio describes a trader who forgot to place a trade for a client, a costly slip. Rather than fire them, which would have taught everyone to hide mistakes, he used it to create the issue log: a place where every problem is recorded, owned and fixed. Failing to log a problem became the serious offence.

In plain words

If people are punished for mistakes, they hide them, and hidden mistakes grow. If people are expected to own and record them, the whole team learns faster. The test of a culture is not whether mistakes happen but whether they get looked at.

Next: Get in sync →
Work Principle 4 · Disagree well

Get and Stay in Sync

In short

Conflict is healthy when it is used to get to the truth. Get in sync by understanding each other's reasoning, not by arguing louder or pulling rank, and once a decision is made, get behind it.

The big ideas

  • Conflict is essential. Hiding disagreements does not make them go away; it just pushes them underground. Open disagreement, handled well, makes relationships stronger.
  • Open-minded and assertive. Hold your view firmly enough to state it clearly, and loosely enough to change it when you hear something better.
  • Every story has another side. Before deciding who is right, make sure you can state the other person's view so well that they agree.
  • Watch out for topic slip. Conversations drift from one problem to another without settling any. Stay on one issue until it is resolved.
  • Once it's decided, back it. People can still disagree after a decision, but everyone should act on it. Reopen it through proper channels, not by dragging your feet.
From the book

Dalio distinguishes between idle complaints and complaints meant to lead to improvement. Grumbling to a friend changes nothing. Raising the issue with the people who can fix it, with your reasoning, is how the organisation gets better. He also suggests letting each person speak for two minutes without interruption when tempers rise.

In plain words

A good disagreement is a joint search for the truth. Ask questions, explain your reasons, repeat back what you heard, and agree on how you will decide if you still disagree. Raising your voice or pulling rank might end the argument, but it does not get anyone in sync.

Next: Believability weighting →
Work Principle 5 · Not all opinions count equally

Believability Weight Your Decision Making

In short

Give more weight to the views of people who have done the thing successfully and can explain how. This is neither democracy nor autocracy; it is an idea meritocracy.

The big ideas

  • Who is believable? Dalio's test: people who have done the thing in question successfully at least three times and can give a convincing explanation of their reasoning when asked.
  • Not every opinion is equal. Everyone may speak, but the views of people with strong records and clear logic should count for more.
  • Know your role. In any discussion, ask whether you are a student, a teacher, or a peer debating. A student should ask questions, not argue.
  • Listen to believable people who disagree. The most valuable input often comes from experienced people who see it differently from you.
  • The responsible party still decides. Believability weighting informs a decision; the person accountable for the result has the final call and must be able to explain it.
From the book

Bridgewater rates people on many traits during meetings, building up a picture over time of where each person is believable. Dalio describes cases where the weighted view of the room disagreed with his own. In those cases, he says, he generally went with the room, because he believed the system was more likely to be right than he was alone.

In plain words

If you are making a medical decision, you weigh your doctor's view more than your neighbour's. Dalio applies the same logic at work: listen to everyone, but give extra weight to people who have proven they know what they are talking about.

Next: Get past disagreements →
Work Principle 6 · When you still disagree

Recognize How to Get Beyond Disagreements

In short

An idea meritocracy needs clear rules for resolving disagreements, so that arguments end in decisions. The principles and the agreed process are above any one person.

The big ideas

  • Principles apply to everyone. No one, including the founder, is above the rules for resolving disputes.
  • Complaining is not deciding. Everyone has the right to question and argue. That is different from the right to decide, which belongs to the responsible party.
  • Escalate when stuck. If two people cannot agree after a fair try, take it to someone with authority over both, or to the believability-weighted view.
  • Don't leave big conflicts unresolved. Small disagreements can be let go. Important ones left unresolved fester and slow everything down.
  • Then move on. Once a decision is made, everyone gets behind it, and the rare reopening goes through the proper process.
From the book

Dalio notes that open disagreement can easily turn into endless debate. So he insists on paths for resolution: talk it through, bring in believable people, and if needed, let the responsible party decide. A culture of radical truth only works if it also knows how to stop arguing and act.

In plain words

Arguing is useful until it is not. Know when to stop: if the issue is small, drop it; if it is big, follow a clear process to a decision, then support that decision even if it is not the one you wanted.

Next: The who matters most →
Part three · work principles 7 to 9

Get the People Right

The second part of the machine is people. Dalio argues that choosing who does a job matters more than any plan, then explains how to hire, train, test and, when needed, move people on.

The idea behind this part

A brilliant plan run by the wrong person fails. A decent plan run by the right person usually gets fixed along the way.

Work Principle 7 · People before plans

Remember That the WHO Is More Important Than the WHAT

In short

The most important decision is who you put in charge of what. Right people with an imperfect plan usually beat wrong people with a great one.

The big ideas

  • Choose responsible parties carefully. Each goal or task needs one person who owns the outcome. Picking that person well matters more than any detail of the plan.
  • The ultimate owner bears the consequences. Whoever lives with the result should hold the final responsibility for it.
  • Everyone needs oversight. Every person, however senior, should be overseen by someone believable with high standards.
  • Good people fix plans. Strong people notice what is not working and change course. Weak people follow a plan off a cliff.
From the book

Dalio writes that his biggest mistakes in business were usually about people rather than plans: putting someone in a role they were not suited to and then being surprised by the result. That is why he spends so much of the work principles on choosing, evaluating and placing people.

In plain words

When something important needs doing, spend most of your effort choosing the right person to own it. Then let them shape the plan. Choosing the wrong person and then managing every step rarely works.

Next: Hire right →
Work Principle 8 · Getting people in the door

Hire Right, Because the Penalties for Hiring Wrong Are Huge

In short

Hiring the wrong person costs far more than the time it takes to hire well. Match the person to the role, look hard at track records and values, and hire people you would want to share your life with.

The big ideas

  • Match the person to the design. Start with what the role needs, then find someone whose abilities, values and skills fit, rather than hiring someone impressive and finding a role later.
  • Values, abilities, skills. Values are hardest to change, then abilities, then skills. Get the first two right; skills can be taught.
  • Think like a sports manager. No one has every quality. Build teams like a coach builds a squad, with complementary strengths.
  • Look at track records. Past performance, checked carefully with people who know the candidate, is one of the best predictors.
  • Look for great questions. Candidates who ask lots of thoughtful questions tend to be curious and open-minded.
From the book

Bridgewater keeps "baseball cards" for its people: a summary of each person's strengths and weaknesses, built from tests, reviews and rated observations, a bit like a player's stats. They make it easier to see who fits a role, and they are open to the people they describe.

In plain words

Hiring is like buying a house: it is easy to fall for something impressive that does not suit how you will live. Decide what the role really needs first, then look for the person who fits it, especially on values, which are hard to change later.

Next: Train, test, sort →
Work Principle 9 · Growing and placing people

Constantly Train, Test, Evaluate, and Sort People

In short

Give constant, honest feedback, evaluate people accurately rather than kindly, and keep moving them to roles where they fit. It takes many observations to really know someone.

The big ideas

  • Personal growth changes careers. As people learn what they are good at, the right role for them often changes. That is a good thing.
  • Evaluate accurately, not kindly. Kind but false reviews hurt the person in the long run. Honest ones, given with care, help them grow.
  • Tough love is the hardest love. Telling someone a hard truth takes more care than telling them what they want to hear.
  • Look for patterns, not one-offs. One bad day says little. A pattern of the same behaviour over many observations says a lot. Getting to know someone well takes a long time.
  • Don't lower the bar. If someone does not fit their role after a fair chance, move them to one that fits, or out. Keeping them in place hurts everyone, including them.
From the book

At Bridgewater, people rate each other on specific qualities during meetings using a tool called the Dot Collector. Each rating, or dot, is tiny on its own. Over hundreds of them, a reliable picture of each person's strengths and weaknesses appears, which feeds into their baseball card.

In plain words

First impressions are noisy. Judge people on many observations, give them honest feedback along the way, and keep adjusting their role until it fits what they are actually good at.

Next: Run the machine →
Part three · work principles 10 to 16

Build and Evolve Your Machine

The last seven principles turn the five-step loop into a way of running a team: watch the machine from above, catch problems early, dig to their roots, redesign, follow through, build tools, and make sure no one is above the rules.

The idea behind this part

Managers are engineers of a machine made of people and processes. When the outcome is wrong, the job is to find which part of the machine produced it and change that part.

Work Principle 10 · See your team from above

Manage as Someone Operating a Machine to Achieve a Goal

In short

Picture your team as a machine of people and processes that produces outcomes. Your job is to compare those outcomes to the goals and keep improving the machine, not to do every task yourself.

The big ideas

  • Look down from a higher level. See yourself and your team as parts of a machine. From above, it is easier to see which part is causing the problem.
  • Compare outcomes with goals. Constantly. The gap between them tells you what to work on.
  • A manager is an organisational engineer. Great managers design the machine and pick its parts. They are not artists improvising each day.
  • Build great metrics. Good measures let you see how the machine is working without checking every part by hand.
  • Don't get lost in the cases. Pay attention to the machine, not only to the problems coming at you. Each case is a chance to fix the machine so the case does not happen again.
From the book

Dalio's picture is simple: goals go into a machine, the machine produces outcomes, and you compare those outcomes with the goals. The machine has two parts: design, meaning how the work is organised, and people. When outcomes fall short, the question is always which of the two is responsible.

In plain words

Stop thinking about your job as doing the work and start thinking of it as building the thing that does the work. When results disappoint, step back and ask whether the process is wrong, the people are wrong, or both.

Next: Don't tolerate problems →
Work Principle 11 · Catch problems early

Perceive and Don't Tolerate Problems

In short

Problems that are noticed and fixed early are small. Problems that are tolerated grow and become normal. Make finding problems part of everyone's job.

The big ideas

  • Assign people to find problems. Do not leave it to chance. Make problem-spotting someone's explicit job, and reward it.
  • Beware the boiling frog. A frog in slowly warming water does not notice until it is too late. Teams get used to bad conditions the same way.
  • Be specific. Do not say "we" or "they" have a problem. Name the specific problem and the specific person responsible.
  • Watch out for group-think. Just because nobody seems worried does not mean nothing is wrong.
  • Compare outcomes to goals. The clearest way to spot a problem is to measure results against what you were aiming for.
From the book

Dalio warns that the hardest problems to see are the ones you have lived with for a long time. They stop feeling like problems and start feeling like how things are. That is why he wants fresh eyes, clear metrics and a culture where pointing out a problem is welcomed rather than resented.

In plain words

Small problems are cheap to fix and expensive to ignore. Get into the habit of naming them early and specifically, before they quietly become the way things are done.

Next: Diagnose to the root →
Work Principle 12 · Find the real reason

Diagnose Problems to Get at Their Root Causes

In short

Don't stop at what happened. Keep asking why until you reach the root cause, which is usually a person's capacity or a flaw in the design, not a single action.

The big ideas

  • The most common mistake is skipping diagnosis. People jump from a problem straight to a fix. Without the root cause, the fix rarely holds.
  • Three questions. Was the outcome good or bad? Who is responsible for it? If it was bad, is the responsible person not capable, or is the design flawed?
  • Proximate versus root. A proximate cause is the action that led to the problem ("the numbers weren't checked"). The root cause is why that happened ("nobody owns checking, and the owner is weak on detail").
  • Root causes are reasons, not actions. "I forgot" is an action. "I'm not reliable with details and have no system" is a root cause.
  • Stay calm about it. Diagnosis is about getting it right, not about blame. Keep an emotional distance from the problem while you dig.
From the book

Dalio describes drilling down until you can describe the root cause with an adjective about a person or a design, such as careless, inexperienced, or poorly organised. If your answer is still a verb, something someone did, you probably have not dug far enough.

In plain words

When something goes wrong, keep asking why until the answer describes a person's capability or the way the work is set up. That is the level where a fix actually stops the problem coming back.

Next: Design improvements →
Work Principle 13 · Redesign the machine

Design Improvements to Your Machine to Get Around Your Problems

In short

Once you know the root cause, redesign the machine: put the right people in the right roles, organise around goals, and add guardrails where people have weaknesses. Expect it to take a few tries.

The big ideas

  • Build around goals, not tasks. Organise teams by what they are trying to achieve, so each has clear ownership of an outcome.
  • Don't build the organisation to fit the people. Design the roles the goals need, then find people for them. Bending roles around individuals creates a mess.
  • Create guardrails. If someone is great at most of their job but weak at one part, pair them with someone strong there. But someone who needs many guardrails is probably in the wrong role.
  • Design is iterative. Between a bad now and a good then there is a messy working-through period. Expect it.
  • A plan is a film script. Picture who does what, and when, in enough detail to see where it might break.
From the book

Dalio uses himself as the example: a strong creative thinker who is not naturally organised. His guardrails were people with the opposite strengths, given real authority to keep him on track. He argues every strong person needs such guardrails somewhere, and that designing them is part of a manager's job.

In plain words

Once you know why something is going wrong, change the structure rather than just asking people to try harder. Move people into roles that fit them and add checks where they need them.

Next: Do what you set out to do →
Work Principle 14 · Follow through

Do What You Set Out to Do

In short

Work toward goals you are excited about, keep tasks connected to those goals, recognise when you are overloaded, and use simple tools like checklists so nothing important slips.

The big ideas

  • Work for goals you care about. Connect each task to the goal it serves. It is far easier to push through dull work when you know why it matters.
  • Everyone has too much to do. Don't let that frustrate you. Prioritise ruthlessly and say no to things that do not serve the goals.
  • Use checklists. Checklists are not a sign of weakness. They stop smart people forgetting simple things.
  • Allow for rest and renovation. Build in time to recover and fix things. Running flat out forever breaks people and machines.
  • Ring the bell. When you achieve something, celebrate it. Recognising wins keeps energy up.
From the book

Dalio uses the image of a juggler. Every person can keep only so many balls in the air. When you add one too many, it is rarely just the new ball that drops: the whole act gets wobbly. A good manager watches for overload in themselves and their people before the balls start falling.

In plain words

Follow-through is mostly about honest capacity. Know how much you can really do, link everything to a goal, write things down, rest, and celebrate the wins.

Next: Tools and protocols →
Work Principle 15 · Bake principles into tools

Use Tools and Protocols to Shape How Work Is Done

In short

Principles stick when they are built into the daily tools and routines people use. Good tools turn good intentions into habits and gather the data that shows what is working.

The big ideas

  • Systemise your principles. A principle that lives only in a book gets forgotten. Turn it into a step in a process or a feature of a tool.
  • Tools gather evidence. Ratings, logs and check-ins create a record, so decisions about people and processes rest on facts rather than memory.
  • Make the right thing the easy thing. If logging a problem takes two taps, people will do it. If it takes a form and a meeting, they won't.
  • Bridgewater's toolkit. The book describes tools such as the Dot Collector for real-time ratings, Baseball Cards that summarise people, the Issue Log, the Pain Button for reflecting on painful moments, the Dispute Resolver and the Daily Update.
From the book

Dalio explains that he and his team did not invent tools for their own sake. Each one began with a principle that people kept forgetting or applying unevenly. Building it into an app made the principle part of the workflow, and the data it collected let them check whether it was working.

In plain words

Good habits survive when they are built into the way work gets done. If you want people to log problems, rate ideas or reflect on pain, give them a simple tool that makes it the natural thing to do.

Next: Governance →
Work Principle 16 · Nobody above the system

And for Heaven's Sake, Don't Overlook Governance!

In short

Every organisation needs checks and balances. No one, including the founder or the CEO, should be more powerful than the system or so important that they cannot be replaced.

The big ideas

  • Checks and balances. Power should always be overseen. Every leader needs someone who can hold them accountable.
  • A great group beats a single leader. In an idea meritocracy, a team of strong leaders who challenge each other is better than one all-powerful boss.
  • No one is irreplaceable. If the organisation would collapse without one person, that is a risk to fix, not a compliment to them.
  • Beware fiefdoms. Departments that put their own interests above the organisation's undermine the whole machine.
  • Clear reporting lines. Everyone should know who they answer to and who answers to them.
From the book

Dalio wrote much of this while handing over control of Bridgewater. He describes the transition as one of the hardest things he did, and argues that the governance system, the rules and the people who enforce them, has to be stronger than any individual, including him.

In plain words

Good intentions are not enough to protect an organisation. Make sure everyone, especially the people at the top, answers to someone, and that the system would keep working if any one person left.

Your own principles ·

Write Your Own

Dalio's real advice is not to adopt his principles but to write your own. Each time something goes wrong, or right, ask what you would do the next time a situation like it comes up, and write that down as a rule. Over the years, the rules become a playbook you can trust when you are under pressure.
