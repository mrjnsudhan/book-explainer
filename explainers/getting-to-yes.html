A chapter-by-chapter guide to Roger Fisher and William Ury's book

Getting to Yes,
Explained

Roger Fisher and William Ury ran the Harvard Negotiation Project, and Getting to Yes is their answer to a choice most people think they're stuck with: be soft and get taken advantage of, or be hard and wreck the relationship. They offer a third way, principled negotiation, built on four points: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests rather than positions, invent options for mutual gain, and insist on objective criteria. Then they tackle the "yes, but" cases: a stronger opponent, one who won't play, and one who plays dirty. This guide takes the introduction, all eight chapters, the conclusion and the ten questions readers ask, with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

Be soft on the people and hard on the problem: look past what people demand to why they want it, and settle on what's fair, not on who pushes hardest.

Part one · the problem

The Problem

Everyone negotiates, many times a day, and most of us know only two ways to do it: give ground to keep the peace, or dig in to win. Both are versions of the same thing, bargaining over positions, and the book opens by showing why that habit produces worse deals, wastes time and strains relationships.

The idea behind this part

The trouble isn't whether you play soft or hard. It's that you're playing the positional game at all.

Introduction · Everyone negotiates

Introduction

In short

You negotiate all the time, with family, colleagues, landlords and shops. The usual choice between soft and hard leaves you either giving in or fighting. The book offers a third way, judged by three tests: a wise agreement, reached efficiently, that leaves the relationship intact.

The big ideas

  • Negotiation is everyday life. Agreeing a price, splitting chores, setting a deadline, deciding where to eat: any back-and-forth between people who want different things and need each other is a negotiation, whether or not anyone calls it one.
  • Soft or hard, and both hurt. The soft negotiator wants to avoid conflict and makes concessions to reach agreement, then feels exploited. The hard negotiator sees every situation as a contest of wills, wins some battles, and wears out themselves and the relationship.
  • Three tests for any method. A good method should produce a wise agreement if one is possible, be efficient, and improve or at least not damage the relationship between the parties. A wise agreement meets each side's legitimate interests as far as possible, resolves conflicts fairly, lasts, and takes the wider community into account.
  • Principled negotiation. The authors' method, developed at the Harvard Negotiation Project, is to decide issues on their merits rather than by haggling. Look for mutual gains where you can, and where interests conflict, insist that the result rest on fair standards independent of either side's will.
From the book

The book grew out of the Harvard Negotiation Project, where the authors and their colleagues studied negotiations of every kind: family quarrels, business deals, labour disputes and talks between countries. They kept seeing the same pattern. People thought their only choices were to be nice or to be tough, and either way the result was worse than it needed to be. What made the difference in the best negotiations wasn't toughness or charm. It was a set of habits anyone could learn, and the book sets out to teach them in plain language, with no tricks and no posturing.

In plain words

Before choosing how to negotiate, ask what you want at the end: a deal that actually works for you, without wasting weeks, with a relationship you can still use tomorrow. Being nice doesn't guarantee that, and nor does being tough.

Chapter 1 · The haggling trap

Don't Bargain Over Positions

In short

Most negotiations are a series of positions: I'll pay this, you want that, and we inch toward each other. It feels natural, but it locks people into their stated positions, wastes time and tests wills rather than finding a good deal.

The big ideas

  • Positions lock you in. The more clearly you state a position and defend it, the more your pride depends on it. Saving face starts to matter more than the actual deal, and agreements end up reflecting the split between final positions rather than what either side really needed.
  • Positional bargaining is slow. Each side starts extreme, holds on stubbornly and gives as little as possible each round. Every small concession is a fight, and there's every reason to drag things out.
  • It endangers the relationship. When the negotiation becomes a contest of wills, one side ends up feeling it has given in to the other. Anger and resentment follow, and with many parties it gets worse: coalitions, blocking and a lowest-common-denominator deal.
  • Being nice is no answer. Playing soft, trusting the other side and conceding to keep the peace, makes you vulnerable to anyone who plays hard, and the agreement you get may not be wise. The alternative is to change the game: negotiate on the merits.
From the book

The authors use two pictures of positional bargaining. One is small: a customer and the owner of a second-hand shop haggle over a brass dish, starting far apart and edging toward the middle, each arguing about the price rather than about what the dish is worth to either of them. The other is enormous. In the early 1960s, the United States and the Soviet Union came close to a comprehensive ban on nuclear tests, then deadlocked over how many on-site inspections a year to allow. The Soviets would accept three; the Americans insisted on at least ten. Talks broke down over the numbers, and no one had worked out what an inspection would actually be: one person looking around for a day, or a hundred people searching for a month.

In plain words

If you're trading numbers back and forth, you're bargaining over positions. Stop and ask a different question: what would a good deal look like for both of us, and how would we know it was fair?

Part two · the method

The Method

Principled negotiation, or negotiation on the merits, rests on four points, one per chapter. Each answers a different part of the problem: the people, what they actually want, the range of possible deals, and how to choose among them without a battle of wills.

The idea behind this part

People, interests, options, criteria: four points that work with almost anyone, in almost any negotiation.

Chapter 2 · Negotiators are people first

Separate the People from the Problem

In short

Negotiators are people, with emotions, values and different ways of seeing things. Deal with the people problem directly, through perception, emotion and communication, and keep it apart from the substance.

The big ideas

  • Two kinds of interest. Every negotiator wants a good deal and also a working relationship. The danger is that the two get tangled, so a comment on the problem is heard as an attack on the person, and people give up substance to protect the relationship or the other way round.
  • Perception. Conflict lies less in objective reality than in people's heads. Put yourself in their shoes; don't assume their intentions from your fears; don't blame them for your problem; talk about each side's perceptions; give them a stake by involving them early; and let them save face by framing a deal that fits their principles.
  • Emotion. Recognise and understand emotions, theirs and yours, and make them explicit. Let the other side let off steam without reacting to it, and use symbolic gestures: an apology, a note of sympathy, a shared meal can defuse a lot at little cost.
  • Communication. Listen actively and acknowledge what's being said, even if you disagree. Speak to be understood, not to score points. Talk about yourself, not them: "I feel let down" rather than "You broke your word". And speak for a purpose: know what you want to get across before you say it.
  • Prevention works best. Build a working relationship before you need it, and think of yourselves as partners side by side, attacking the problem rather than each other. Be soft on the people and hard on the problem.
From the book

Words can carry meanings you never intended. The book tells how, in 1980, the United Nations Secretary-General Kurt Waldheim flew to Iran to seek the release of the American hostages. On arrival he said he had come as a mediator to work out a compromise. In Persian, the authors explain, compromise lacks its English sense of a middle ground both sides can accept and carries the sense of compromising one's integrity, and a mediator is someone who meddles uninvited. Iranian broadcasts carried his words, and within an hour his car was stoned by angry Iranians. A small misunderstanding about perception set back his efforts badly.

In plain words

When things get heated, don't fight the person. Let them speak, show you heard them, say how things look from your side without blaming, and steer back to the shared problem. You're on the same side of the table, facing the issue together.

Chapter 3 · Ask why

Focus on Interests, Not Positions

In short

A position is what someone says they want. Interests are why they want it: their needs, worries, hopes and fears. Behind opposed positions there are usually interests that are shared or compatible, and there's more than one way to satisfy them.

The big ideas

  • Interests define the problem. The real conflict isn't between positions but between each side's needs and concerns. Reconcile the interests, not the positions, because for every interest there are usually several possible positions that would satisfy it.
  • Ask why, and why not. Find interests by asking why they take their position, and why they don't take the one you'd like. Remember each side has many interests, and the most powerful are basic human needs: security, economic well-being, a sense of belonging, recognition, and control over one's own life.
  • Write them down. Make a list of each side's interests as they emerge. It helps you remember them, improve your understanding and think of better ways to meet them.
  • Talk about interests. Be specific about yours and explain them vividly. Acknowledge theirs as part of the problem. Put the problem before your answer, look forward to what you want rather than back at what went wrong, and be concrete but flexible.
  • Hard on the problem. You can push hard for your interests and still be supportive of the people. Attacking the problem rather than the person means you can be firm without being hostile.
From the book

Two men are arguing in a library. One wants the window open, the other wants it shut, and they bicker about how far to open it: a crack, halfway, three quarters. Nothing satisfies either. The librarian walks in and asks one of them why he wants it open: to get some fresh air. She asks the other why he wants it closed: to avoid the draught. After thinking a moment, she opens a window in the next room, bringing in fresh air without a draught. The book sets this beside the Egypt-Israel talks at Camp David in 1978, where the two positions over the Sinai looked impossible to reconcile until the interests underneath, security for Israel and sovereignty for Egypt, pointed to a deal: return the Sinai to Egypt, but keep large parts of it demilitarised.

In plain words

When someone says what they want, ask yourself, and often them, "Why?" Then ask "Why not what I'm proposing?" The answers are their interests, and interests leave far more room for a deal than positions ever do.

Chapter 4 · Make the pie bigger

Invent Options for Mutual Gain

In short

People rarely see many options in a negotiation. They judge ideas too early, search for the single right answer, assume a fixed pie, and treat the other side's problem as theirs alone. The cure is to invent first, decide later, and look for deals that give both sides more.

The big ideas

  • Four obstacles. Premature judgement kills ideas before they form; searching for one answer narrows the field; assuming a fixed pie turns every gain into someone's loss; and thinking "solving their problem is their problem" stops you from helping them say yes.
  • Separate inventing from deciding. Hold a brainstorm with no criticism allowed, ideally sitting side by side and facing the problem. Invite wild ideas. Only afterwards pick the most promising and improve them.
  • Broaden the options. Move between the specific and the general with the Circle Chart. Look through the eyes of different experts. Invent agreements of different strengths, such as provisional, partial or in principle, and change the scope by breaking the problem into smaller pieces or making it bigger.
  • Dovetail differences. Many good deals come from differences, not similarities. If you care most about one thing and they care most about another, each can give what matters less to them and get what matters more. Differences in interests, beliefs, value placed on time, forecasts and attitude to risk can all be traded.
  • Make their decision easy. Put yourself in their shoes: whose decision is it, and what would make yes easy for them to say and defend? Draft options they could accept as is, a yesable proposition, and favour offers over threats.
From the book

The book's best-known story is about two sisters who quarrel over an orange. They agree to cut it in half. One eats her half and throws the peel away; the other throws away the fruit and uses the peel to bake a cake. Each could have had the whole of what she wanted, if either had asked why the other wanted it. To help people find options like that, the authors offer a tool they call the Circle Chart. Start with what's wrong, the problem. Step back to analysis: what's causing it, what's missing? Then to approaches: what general strategies might help? Finally to action ideas: what specific steps could we take? Moving round the circle turns one good idea into many.

In plain words

Before you argue about which option to pick, make more of them. Find out what the other side values that costs you little, and what you value that costs them little, and trade.

Chapter 5 · Settle it on the merits

Insist on Using Objective Criteria

In short

However good you are at inventing options, some interests will still conflict. Instead of settling it by who's more stubborn, agree on a fair standard or a fair procedure, independent of either side's will, and decide on that basis.

The big ideas

  • A battle of wills is costly. Deciding by who pushes harder strains the relationship and rewards stubbornness. Basing the result on standards both sides can respect makes it more likely to be wise and fair, and easier to accept without losing face.
  • Fair standards. Market value, precedent, scientific judgement, professional standards, efficiency, costs, what a court would decide, moral standards, equal treatment, tradition, reciprocity. The best standards are independent of either side's will, legitimate and practical, and would apply to both sides.
  • Fair procedures. When there's no obvious standard, use a fair procedure: one cuts and the other chooses; take turns; draw lots; or let a third party decide.
  • Negotiate with criteria. Frame each issue as a joint search for objective criteria: "How did you arrive at that figure?" Reason and be open to reason about which standards apply. And never yield to pressure, only to principle.
From the book

At the international Law of the Sea conference, India, speaking for many developing countries, proposed an initial fee of $60 million per site for companies mining the deep seabed. The United States proposed no fee at all. The positions were deadlocked, until someone discovered that the Massachusetts Institute of Technology had built a model of the economics of deep-seabed mining. The model showed that a fee of that size would make mining unprofitable for years to come. Faced with an objective measure rather than an American demand, India reconsidered its figure, and the US delegation in turn accepted that some initial fee was reasonable. The standard did what pressure couldn't.

In plain words

When you hit a real conflict, don't ask "who wins?" Ask "what's the fair way to decide this?" Find a standard you'd both accept, such as market price or what the law says, or a procedure, like one cuts and the other chooses. Then stick to the principle, not the pressure.

Part three · yes, but

Yes, But

Fine in theory, readers say, but what if the other side holds all the cards, refuses to do anything but bargain, or plays dirty? These three chapters are the book's answers, and they turn on the same idea: you can change the game even if the other side doesn't want to.

The idea behind this part

Know your best alternative, deflect attacks onto the problem, and negotiate about the rules when they cheat.

Chapter 6 · Develop your BATNA

What If They Are More Powerful?

In short

If the other side holds the stronger cards, no method can guarantee a good deal. What you can do is protect yourself from a bad one and make the most of what you have. Both depend on your BATNA, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement.

The big ideas

  • The trouble with a bottom line. Many people protect themselves with a bottom line, the worst deal they'll accept. It stops you being talked into disaster, but it's rigid, usually set too high or too low, and it discourages you from inventing better options.
  • Know your BATNA. The real measure is what you'll do if you walk away. Any offer better than your BATNA is worth considering; any offer worse should be refused. If you haven't thought about it, you're negotiating with your eyes closed.
  • Develop it. Make a list of actions you might take if there's no agreement, improve the most promising ones into practical alternatives, and pick the best. A trip wire, a figure short of your BATNA that makes you stop and think, can warn you before you agree too fast.
  • Your BATNA is your power. The better your alternative, the stronger you are. Resources the other side has matter less than whether you can walk away. Also think about their BATNA: if it's weaker than they believe, tell them; if it's strong, you may need to change the game.
From the book

The book takes the example of selling a house. A seller with a bottom line says, "I won't take less than this." A seller who has thought about a BATNA asks a more useful question: what will I do if I haven't sold by a certain date? Keep it on the market indefinitely? Rent it out? Tear it down and turn the lot into a car park? Let someone live in it rent-free in return for painting it? Once you've looked at those alternatives and picked the best, you know exactly which offers to accept, and you can negotiate calmly, because walking away is a real choice rather than a disaster.

In plain words

Before any important negotiation, ask: if this falls apart, what will I actually do? Work that out, make it as good as you can, and compare every offer to it. That's your protection and your strength.

Chapter 7 · Use negotiation jujitsu

What If They Won't Play?

In short

Sometimes the other side insists on positional bargaining and attacks you. Don't push back. Like a jujitsu fighter, step aside and turn their force toward the problem: look behind their position, invite criticism, and use questions and silence.

The big ideas

  • Three ways to change the game. Concentrate on the merits yourself, which can be contagious. If that doesn't work, use negotiation jujitsu. And if that fails, bring in a third party to run a one-text procedure.
  • Don't attack their position, look behind it. Treat their position as one option and look for the interests it reflects. Ask what's behind it, and how it might be improved.
  • Don't defend your ideas, invite criticism. Ask what's wrong with your proposal and what they'd do in your place. Turn criticism of your idea into advice for improving it.
  • Recast attacks on you as attacks on the problem. When they blame you, let them finish, then treat it as a statement about the problem. Ask questions instead of making statements, and use silence: an unreasonable proposal often sounds weaker after a pause.
  • The one-text procedure. At Camp David in 1978, the United States drafted a single text, asked Egypt and Israel for criticism rather than concessions, and revised it again and again, through more than twenty drafts in thirteen days, until there was nothing left to improve. Then it asked each side for a yes.
From the book

The book ends this chapter with a long case. Frank Turnbull rented a flat and later discovered it was under rent control and he'd been paying well above the legal maximum. He went to see Mrs Jones of the property company, who refused to budge. Rather than accusing her, he asked to be corrected if he had his facts wrong, thanked her for what she'd done for the tenants, said the issue for them was fairness, not trust, and asked what principle lay behind the rent. When she pushed, he asked questions and let the silences sit. When she offered a figure, he asked how it was worked out. He proposed a fair standard, offered to fit the timing to her convenience, and closed by saying how much he'd enjoyed dealing with her. The result was a settlement based on what the law and the facts supported.

In plain words

When someone attacks your idea or you, don't hit back and don't defend. Ask a question, invite their advice, say "help me understand", or just pause. You're redirecting their energy onto the problem.

Chapter 8 · Taming the hard bargainer

What If They Use Dirty Tricks?

In short

Some people lie, intimidate or squeeze. Don't reply in kind and don't just put up with it. Recognise the tactic, raise it openly, and negotiate about the rules of the game, applying the same principles you'd use for the substance.

The big ideas

  • Negotiate the rules. There are three steps: recognise the tactic, raise the issue explicitly, and question whether the tactic is legitimate and desirable. Then negotiate over it like any other issue, with the four points.
  • Deliberate deception. Phoney facts, ambiguous authority (letting you believe they can decide, then saying the boss must approve) and doubtful intentions. Separate the people from the problem: verify claims without accusing anyone of lying. Not telling you everything is not the same as deceiving you.
  • Psychological warfare. Stressful surroundings, personal attacks, the good-cop bad-cop routine, and threats. Name them: "Is this a good guy, bad guy routine?" Suggest a break, a change of room, or that you'll only respond to reasons, not to threats.
  • Positional pressure. Refusing to negotiate, extreme demands, escalating demands, lock-in tactics, a "hardhearted partner" offstage, calculated delay, and "take it or leave it". Don't be rushed or bluffed: ask the principle behind each move, and think about your BATNA.
From the book

One trick the book describes is the escalating demand. The other side agrees a point, then reopens it, or raises a fresh demand every time you make a concession, hoping you'll agree to the rest quickly just to stop the slide. Another is the hardhearted partner: "I'd love to agree, but my partner will never accept it." The authors' advice is the same in every case. Point out what seems to be happening, without accusing anyone of bad faith, and ask whether it's a good way to reach agreement. Then propose a fairer rule, such as settling on a list of all outstanding issues, or talking with the partner directly, and if necessary take a break to reconsider whether you want to continue.

In plain words

If you think you're being played, say so calmly: "I may be mistaken, but it looks as though..." Then ask whether that's how both of you want to negotiate. Most tricks only work when nobody points them out.

Part four · in conclusion

In Conclusion

The book ends briefly: most of this you knew already, the way to get good at it is to practise, and winning means getting a good result without paying for it in the relationship. In later editions the authors added answers to the ten questions readers ask them most.

The idea behind this part

Principled negotiation is a skill, not a trick. It gets better each time you try it.

Conclusion · You knew it all the time

In Conclusion

In short

The authors end with three points. Most of this matches what you already knew from experience. You only learn it by doing it, so practise, and review afterwards. And winning should mean getting a good result while keeping the relationship, not beating the other side.

The big ideas

  • You knew it all the time. Little in the book is new. It organises common sense and shared experience into a framework you can think with and act on, and you may well recognise times you've done it already.
  • Learn from doing. Reading about negotiation won't make you good at it any more than reading about tennis will. Try it, review what happened, and try again. Each attempt makes the next one easier.
  • Winning. Negotiation isn't a contest you win by defeating the other side. It's a joint search for an outcome that serves you both. Being principled lets you get what you're entitled to and still be decent.
  • Soft, hard or principled. Chapter 1's famous table sets three columns side by side. The soft negotiator sees friends and wants agreement; the hard one sees adversaries and wants victory; the principled one sees problem-solvers and wants a wise outcome reached efficiently and amicably.
From the book

Throughout the book the authors sum up the method with a table of three columns. Soft negotiators trust the other side, change position easily, make offers, disclose their bottom line and yield to pressure. Hard negotiators distrust the other side, dig in, make threats, mislead about their bottom line and apply pressure. Principled negotiators do something different on every row: they proceed independent of trust, focus on interests, explore interests, avoid having a bottom line, invent options for mutual gain and insist on objective criteria. The conclusion's message is that the third column isn't a halfway point between the other two. It's a different game.

In plain words

Next time you negotiate, try one principle on purpose: ask why, or name a fair standard, or work out your walk-away. Afterwards, spend two minutes on what worked. That's how the book becomes a habit.

Ten questions · The answers readers wanted

Ten Questions People Ask About Getting to Yes

In short

Added in the second edition, these answers deal with fairness, people, tactics and power: when positional bargaining makes sense, what to do when the people are the problem, how to handle culture and personality, and how to negotiate from a weaker position.

The big ideas

  • Does positional bargaining ever make sense? Sometimes, for a one-off deal over a single issue with little at stake and low costs to haggling. But even then, principled negotiation usually does better, and it's a better habit to build.
  • Fairness and the people. If the other side has a different idea of what's fair, explore both standards and look for the most persuasive or a fair way to choose. If the people are the problem, build a working relationship that can handle disagreement: be unconditionally constructive, whatever they do.
  • Personality, gender and culture. Adapt to the people in front of you, their pace, formality and way of speaking, but don't stereotype. The principles stay the same; how you apply them changes.
  • Tactics. On where to meet, who makes the first offer and how high to start: let principle guide even those small calls. Make a first offer only once you understand interests, base it on criteria, and don't start so extreme that you lose credibility. Move from options to commitment by drafting a framework agreement early.
  • Power. The way you negotiate can make a big difference even against a stronger side. The book names six sources of negotiating power: skill and knowledge, a good relationship, a good alternative, an elegant solution, legitimacy, and commitment, a well-crafted offer you can make.
From the book

Readers kept asking the authors the same thing: does any of this work when the other side is far more powerful? Their answer is that power isn't just money, size or rank. Much of it comes from things any negotiator can build before and during a negotiation: knowing the people and the issue, having a relationship that lets you talk honestly, developing a strong alternative, finding an elegant option that meets both sides' interests, appealing to standards that make your case legitimate, and making a clear, well-crafted commitment. The other side may have the money, the authors argue, but you can still have most of the power that matters.

In plain words

If you feel weaker, don't give up and don't bluff. Work on the power you can build: learn more, improve the relationship, strengthen your alternative, find a better option, ground your case in fair standards, and make a clear offer.
