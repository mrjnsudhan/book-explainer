A chapter-by-chapter guide to David J. Schwartz's book

The Magic of Thinking Big,
Explained

David J. Schwartz taught marketing and ran workshops for salespeople and managers, and he kept noticing the same thing: the people who got furthest weren't the cleverest or the luckiest. They simply thought bigger about what they could do, and then acted on it. The Magic of Thinking Big turns that into thirteen chapters of plain, practical habits, from curing your favourite excuse to sitting in the front row. This guide takes each chapter in turn, with a lab in every one where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

How far you go is set less by your brains or your luck than by the size of your thinking, and thinking can be trained.

Part one · belief

Belief

Schwartz starts with the foundation. Before you can think big, you have to believe big things are possible for you, stop hiding behind excuses, and get a grip on the fear that keeps you small. None of this needs talent. It needs practice.

The idea behind this part

Success starts in the head: belief opens the door, excuses close it, and action is the cure for fear.

Chapter 1 · Belief is where it starts

Believe You Can Succeed and You Will

In short

Believing you can do something isn't magic, but it is the switch that sets your mind looking for ways to do it. Doubt does the opposite: it hands your mind a job of finding reasons why not.

The big ideas

  • Belief finds the how. When you truly believe something can be done, your mind starts working on methods. When you doubt it, your mind works just as hard, only on excuses.
  • A thermostat, not a wish. Schwartz compares belief to a thermostat. It sets the level you will reach. People who expect a little get a little; people who expect a lot reach for a lot.
  • Ordinary people, big belief. Successful people are mostly ordinary people who have come to believe in themselves and what they do. The belief came first, and the skill followed.
  • Three habits. Think success, not failure. Remind yourself often that you are better than you think. And believe big, because the size of your success is set by the size of your belief.
From the book

Schwartz admits nobody moves a mountain just by wishing for it. But look at what people who believed it could be done have built: tunnels through mountains, bridges over bays, roads over passes. The belief didn't move the rock. It made them ask how, and the answer to how was engineering. He finds the same thing in his sales workshops: the people who expect to climb are the ones who go looking for the ladder.

In plain words

Before you start anything big, check which question you're asking. "Can I?" invites your mind to argue. "How can I?" puts it to work. Believing first is what makes the planning possible.

Chapter 2 · The four favourite alibis

Cure Yourself of Excusitis, the Failure Disease

In short

Unsuccessful people tend to carry a ready excuse for why they can't. Schwartz calls the habit excusitis and names its four common forms: health, intelligence, age and luck. Each one has a cure.

The big ideas

  • Health excusitis. "I'd do it, but my health isn't good." The cure: refuse to talk about your health, refuse to worry about it, be grateful it's as good as it is, and remember it's better to wear out than to rust out.
  • Intelligence excusitis. "You need brains for that." The cure: never underrate your own intelligence or overrate other people's. Your attitude matters more than your IQ, and knowing how to think beats storing facts.
  • Age excusitis. "I'm too old" or "too young". The cure: look at your age positively, work out how many productive years you still have, and spend that time on what you actually want.
  • Luck excusitis. "They just got lucky." The cure: accept that results have causes. People who look lucky usually prepared, planned and kept at it. You can't wish your way to success.
From the book

Schwartz retells a well-known moment from Henry Ford's life. In a court case, lawyers tried to show Ford was ignorant by quizzing him on history and general knowledge. Ford, as the story goes, said he had a row of buttons on his desk, and if he needed a fact he could press one and get someone who knew it in minutes. Why fill his head with facts when he could keep it free for thinking? Schwartz's point: what pays off is not how much you've memorised, but how well you use your mind.

In plain words

Listen for your favourite "but". Whatever form it takes, it's comfortable because it lets you off the hook. Name it, then do the opposite of what it tells you.

Chapter 3 · Action cures fear

Build Confidence and Destroy Fear

In short

Fear is real, but it can be cured, and the cure is action. Waiting and worrying feed fear. Naming it and doing something about it shrink it. Small daily habits build confidence from the outside in.

The big ideas

  • Action cures fear. Hesitating makes fear grow. Isolate exactly what you fear, then find the action that answers it, and take it.
  • Manage your memory. Your mind is like a bank. Deposit positive thoughts and replay them; refuse to keep withdrawing the unpleasant ones.
  • People are just people. If others intimidate you, remember they are human too, with the same worries. Try to understand them rather than fear them.
  • Do what's right. Acting against your conscience breeds guilt, and guilt eats confidence. Doing what you know is right keeps it intact.
From the book

Schwartz noticed that in meetings, lectures and church, the back rows fill up first. People choose them because they don't want to be noticed, and that is fear talking. So his first confidence builder is to sit at the front. The others are just as plain: look people in the eye, walk about a quarter faster than usual as if you have somewhere to be, speak up at least once in every meeting, and smile a big, full smile. Each one feels awkward at first, and each one sends a message back to your own head: I'm confident.

In plain words

When you're afraid, don't sit and wait for the feeling to pass. Pin down what exactly scares you, do the one thing that deals with it, and practise looking confident until you feel it.

Part two · thinking big

Thinking Big

With belief in place, the next three chapters are about the size and quality of your thinking: how big thinkers talk and see, how they find better ways to do things, and how the way you see yourself shapes what you do.

The idea behind this part

Big thinkers aren't born with bigger brains. They use bigger words, picture what could be, and treat themselves and their work as important.

Chapter 4 · The size of your thinking

How to Think Big

In short

People are measured by the size of their thinking. To think bigger, take honest stock of your strengths, use words that paint big pictures, see things as they could be, and stop sweating the small stuff.

The big ideas

  • Measure yourself fairly. Most people sell themselves short. List your main strengths, then think of people who have them and aren't doing better than you. You're bigger than you think.
  • Use a big thinker's vocabulary. Words make pictures in people's heads. Choose ones that are positive, hopeful and cheerful, and avoid ones that paint failure or defeat.
  • See what can be. Big thinkers look past how a thing is to how it could be. Practise adding value in your head to things, to people and to yourself.
  • Rise above the trivial. Before you fight about something, ask: will it really make a difference? Petty quarrels and small irritations eat the energy big goals need.
From the book

One of Schwartz's examples is about selling property. Someone who sees a run-down place only as it stands, with peeling paint and an overgrown yard, struggles to sell it. Someone who sees and describes what it could become, with a fresh coat, a tidy garden and a family living there, gives the buyer a picture worth paying for. Schwartz says the same "see what can be" habit works on a job, on a colleague and on yourself.

In plain words

When you size something up, including yourself, add the question "what could it become?". Talk in words that point forward. Save your fight for things that will matter in a year.

Chapter 5 · Finding better ways

How to Think and Dream Creatively

In short

Creative thinking isn't just for artists. It means finding new and better ways to do anything. It starts with believing a thing can be done, and grows when you stay open, ask how to do better, and listen.

The big ideas

  • Believe it can be done. Once you believe something is possible, your mind starts to find the way. Say "impossible" and the thinking stops.
  • Beat tradition's grip. Welcome new ideas, try new things, and ask "how can we do this better?" rather than "we've always done it this way".
  • Capacity is a state of mind. How much you can do depends on how much you think you can do. Ask "how can I do more?" and you'll find ways to make room.
  • Listen more, and write ideas down. Big people do more listening than talking. Spend time with people who stretch your thinking, and capture every idea before it slips away.
From the book

Schwartz describes asking a training group whether prisons could be done away with within thirty years. At first the group listed every reason it couldn't happen. Then he asked them to assume it could be done, and to think about how. The ideas began to flow: better youth programmes, more education, help for people at risk before they turn to crime. The question was the same; only the starting belief had changed.

In plain words

The next time someone says it can't be done, try saying "suppose it could. How?" And when your plate looks full, ask how to do more rather than whether you can. You'll often find the room.

Next: You are what you think →
Chapter 6 · Think yourself important

You Are What You Think You Are

In short

Others see you the way you see yourself. If you think of yourself and your work as important, you act that way, and people respond. So look the part, value your work, and give yourself a pep talk.

The big ideas

  • Look important. Your appearance speaks to you as much as to others. Dress well and you feel more capable. Pay twice as much and buy half as many, so what you own is good.
  • Think your work is important. People who see their job as a stepping stone to something worthwhile do it better, get noticed and move up. People who think it's nothing stay put.
  • Sell yourself to yourself. Write a short commercial about your strengths and your goals, and play it to yourself several times a day. It pushes out the negative self-talk.
  • Upgrade your thinking. In any situation, ask yourself: is this how an important person would think? Then think and act that way.
From the book

Schwartz's advice on clothes is blunt: pay twice as much and buy half as many. It isn't about vanity. When you look right, he argues, you think and act with more confidence, and people treat you with more respect, which feeds the confidence again. He makes the same point about work: a person who sees their job, however humble, as important does it with care, and care is what gets noticed.

In plain words

Treat yourself as someone who matters, because people take their cue from you. Dress for the person you're becoming, take pride in your work, and talk to yourself the way a good coach would.

Part three · your world

Your World

Thinking doesn't happen in a vacuum. Schwartz turns to the things that feed it every day: the people and places around you, the attitudes you carry, and the way you think about others.

The idea behind this part

Your mind takes on the colour of what surrounds it. Choose your surroundings, your attitudes and your view of people on purpose.

Chapter 7 · What you feed your mind

Manage Your Environment: Go First Class

In short

Your mind takes on the shape of what it's fed. The people you spend time with, what you read and listen to, and the places you go all push your thinking bigger or smaller. So choose them on purpose.

The big ideas

  • Environment shapes thinking. We pick up the attitudes of those around us. Spend your days with small thinkers and your goals shrink to match.
  • Beware the people who pull you down. Some people will tell you it can't be done, often because they never tried. Don't let them set your limits.
  • Get advice from the successful. If you want guidance, ask people who have done what you want to do. Asking a failure how to succeed is asking for more failure.
  • Avoid thought poison, go first class. Gossip and constant complaining are poison for your thinking. And in what you buy, eat and do, go for quality over quantity.
From the book

Schwartz describes how ambition gets worn down by the people around it: the co-workers who tease anyone who studies at night, the friends who say a plan is unrealistic, the gatherings where the talk is mostly complaints about the boss. None of it is dramatic, but it adds up day by day. His answer is to make friends in more than one circle, seek out people with big ideas, and make a habit of going first class, even if that means doing fewer things, so your mind gets used to quality.

In plain words

Look at who and what you spend your time with, and ask whether it makes you think bigger or smaller. Add more of the first, cut back on the second, and don't let gossip set your mood.

Chapter 8 · Three attitudes that pay

Make Your Attitudes Your Allies

In short

People can read your attitude in how you speak, look and act, and they respond to it. Schwartz names three that work in your favour: being activated, making others feel important, and putting service first.

The big ideas

  • "I'm activated." Enthusiasm is catching. To grow it, dig deeper into anything you find dull, put life into everything you do, and broadcast good news rather than bad.
  • "You are important." Everyone wants to feel they matter. Show appreciation, use people's names, give credit freely and never hog the glory.
  • "Service first." Give people more than they expect. Put service first and the money tends to follow; put money first and both slip away.
  • Attitudes show. You can't hide a bad attitude for long. It leaks out in your voice, your face and your work, so fix the attitude, not the mask.
From the book

Schwartz's cure for being bored by something is to learn more about it. A subject that seems dull from the outside often turns fascinating once you dig in, and the interest you find becomes real enthusiasm that others can feel. He pairs this with a simple daily habit: be the person who brings good news. Lead with what went right. People are drawn to those who leave them feeling better, and the habit lifts your own mood too.

In plain words

If something bores you, find out more about it. Walk in with energy, make the people around you feel noticed, and give a little more than you're asked. Those three habits change how people respond to you.

Chapter 9 · Be easy to support

Think Right Toward People

In short

Nobody gets far alone. Success depends on other people supporting you, and people support those they like. So take the first step with others, accept their flaws, look for their good side, and let them talk.

The big ideas

  • Be the one who starts. Don't wait to be greeted. Introduce yourself, learn names and take the first step in building a friendship.
  • Accept differences. Nobody is perfect, including you. Accept people's quirks and limits instead of expecting them to be like you.
  • Tune in to channel P. Think of your mind as a radio with two stations: P for positive and N for negative. Tune to P and look for what's good in people.
  • Let them talk, and don't blame. Practise conversation generosity: ask questions and listen. Be courteous to everyone. When things go wrong, look at your own part before blaming others.
From the book

Schwartz's picture of the mind as a radio is one of the book's best known. Every person you know has good and bad points. On channel N you notice only the irritations: the colleague who talks too much, the neighbour who's always late. On channel P you notice that the talker is generous and the late neighbour would help anyone. The person is the same either way. What changes is how you treat them, and how they come to treat you.

In plain words

Make the first move with people, forgive their rough edges and go looking for their good side. Ask about them, listen properly, and stay polite even when it's hard. People back those who make them feel liked.

Part four · action

Action

The last four chapters move from thinking to doing. Start before conditions are perfect, treat defeat as a lesson, steer by written goals, and when you lead, put yourself in the other person's place.

The idea behind this part

Ideas only count when you act on them. Big thinkers start now, adjust as they go and bring other people with them.

Chapter 10 · Do it now

Get the Action Habit

In short

Good ideas are worth nothing until someone acts on them. Successful people are doers: they don't wait for perfect conditions, they start before they feel ready, and they make "now" their working word.

The big ideas

  • Don't wait for perfect. Conditions will never be perfect. Expect problems and deal with them as they come, rather than waiting for the day when nothing is wrong.
  • Ideas need action. An idea alone doesn't win anything. Plenty of people had the same idea as someone who got rich from it; the difference is that one of them acted.
  • Start your mind mechanically. Don't wait to feel inspired. Sit down and start, and the mind usually warms up once the work is under way.
  • Think now, and take the initiative. "Tomorrow" and "someday" are the words of people who don't act. Use "now". Volunteer, speak up and get on with it.
From the book

Schwartz tells of a young couple who wanted their own home but never seemed to have enough saved, and kept waiting for the right moment. When they finally decided to act instead of wait, they found a house, worked out how to raise the down payment, and moved in, discovering they could manage what had seemed out of reach. Waiting would have felt safer, and they would still be waiting.

In plain words

Pick the thing you've been waiting to start and do a small part of it today. Don't wait to feel ready or for everything to line up. Action creates the confidence and the conditions you were waiting for.

Chapter 11 · Setbacks as lessons

How to Turn Defeat into Victory

In short

Everyone meets setbacks. The difference is what you do next. Study each defeat, criticise yourself honestly, stop blaming luck, and combine persistence with trying new approaches.

The big ideas

  • Study the setback. Treat each defeat as information. Ask what it teaches, and use it to plan a better attempt.
  • Be your own constructive critic. Look for your own weaknesses and faults, then fix them. Doing that is how professionals improve.
  • Stop blaming luck. Bad luck is a convenient story that teaches nothing. Look for the cause, because a cause can be fixed.
  • Persist, but experiment. Persistence alone can mean hitting the same wall forever. Keep the goal, but try a new approach when one fails, and look for the good side of every situation.
From the book

Schwartz is careful to say that persistence is not enough on its own. He describes people who keep doing the same thing, harder and longer, and keep failing. His advice is to step back from the problem, look at it fresh and try a different route. Many breakthroughs, he notes, come from people who refused to give up on the goal but were willing to give up on the method.

In plain words

When something fails, ask what it taught you and what you'd do differently. Keep the goal fixed, change the approach, and don't hand the result over to luck.

Next: Use goals →
Chapter 12 · Know where you're going

Use Goals to Help You Grow

In short

A goal is a clear picture of where you want to be. Without one, you drift. Schwartz asks you to write a ten-year plan for work, home and social life, then take it a step at a time, with a 30-day improvement guide.

The big ideas

  • Set a ten-year plan. Picture yourself ten years from now in three departments: work, home and social. Write down what you want in each.
  • Let goals give you energy. People with a clear goal have more drive. Deep desire for something makes you act, think and push harder.
  • One step at a time. Big goals are reached through small ones. Break the plan into 30-day steps, and treat each task, however small, as part of the climb.
  • Invest in yourself, accept detours. Spend on education and on things that feed your mind. If a road is blocked, take a detour, but don't drop the destination.
From the book

Schwartz gives the reader a simple form to fill in each month, a 30-day improvement guide. It has a few headings: habits to break, habits to acquire, ways to become more valuable at work, ways to become more valuable at home, and ways to sharpen your mind. At the end of the month you review it and write the next one. It turns a ten-year dream into something you can do this week.

In plain words

Decide where you want to be in ten years, in work, home and life. Then decide what you'll do this month to move one step closer, write it down, and check it daily.

Next: Think like a leader →
Chapter 13 · Four rules for leading

How to Think Like a Leader

In short

Leaders aren't pushed up by luck. They're lifted by people who want to follow them. Schwartz gives four rules: trade minds with the people you want to influence, ask what the human way is, think progress, and take time to think alone.

The big ideas

  • Trade minds. Before you try to persuade anyone, ask what you'd think and want in their place. Then speak to that.
  • Find the human way. When dealing with people, ask what the kind and decent way to handle this is. Firmness and fairness can go together.
  • Think progress. Believe in improvement, push for it, and set high standards. People follow those who expect things to get better.
  • Confer with yourself. Spend some time alone each day thinking. Schwartz calls it managed solitude, and he credits it for many leaders' best decisions.
From the book

Schwartz suggests a simple test before any attempt to persuade: trade minds. A salesperson asks what the customer is worried about, not what commission is at stake. A manager announcing a change asks how it looks from the shop floor. He adds a daily habit for leaders: an hour of quiet, alone, with no distractions, to think things through. Big decisions, he says, are rarely made well in a crowd.

In plain words

Before you ask anyone for anything, spend a minute in their shoes. Treat people decently, push for things to get better, and give yourself some quiet time each day to think.
