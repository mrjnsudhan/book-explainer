System Design Interview,
Explained

In a system design interview you get forty-five minutes, a whiteboard and a prompt like "design YouTube". Alex Xu's System Design Interview: An Insider's Guide teaches the moves: how to grow a system from one server to millions of users, how to do the maths on the back of an envelope, a four-step plan for the interview itself, and twelve worked designs from a rate limiter to Google Drive. This guide takes all sixteen chapters one at a time, with a lab in each where you can poke at the idea yourself.

The book in one line

There is no single right answer. Clarify the problem, sketch a design, defend its trade-offs, and think out loud the whole way.

Part one · the toolkit

Foundations

Before any design, Xu lays down three tools. A tour of how a real system grows from one box to millions of users, the habit of rough maths with powers of two and latency numbers, and a four-step plan for spending the forty-five minutes.

The idea behind this part

Every design question is the same question underneath: what breaks first as you grow, and what do you add to fix it?

Chapter 1 · How a system grows up

Scale from Zero to Millions of Users

In short

A system starts as one server. As users arrive, you split out the database, add load balancers, replicas, caches and a CDN, make the web tier stateless, then spread across data centres, queues and database shards.

The big ideas

  • Start with one box. Web app, database and cache all run on a single server. Users find it through DNS and talk to it over HTTP. It works, until it doesn't.
  • Scale out, not just up. Vertical scaling (a bigger machine) is simple but has a hard ceiling and no failover. Horizontal scaling (more machines behind a load balancer) is how large systems grow.
  • Copy the data, cache the hot parts. Database replication gives one master for writes and several replicas for reads. A cache keeps frequently read data in memory, and a CDN serves static files from servers near the user.
  • Stateless, then spread out. Move session data out of the web servers into a shared store, so any server can handle any request. Then add more data centres, message queues between parts, monitoring, and finally sharding to split the database itself.
In the interview

Interviewers love this chapter because it is the vocabulary for everything else. If you are asked how to handle ten times the traffic, walk the path out loud: what fails first, what you add, and the new problem that addition brings. A cache raises the question of stale data. A replica raises the question of failover. Sharding raises the question of hot keys and joins. Naming the new problem is what shows you understand the old fix.

In plain words

Growing a system is a chain of fixes. Find the part that breaks first, add the standard piece that relieves it, and be ready to explain what that new piece costs you.

Chapter 2 · Rough numbers, fast

Back-of-the-envelope Estimation

In short

Good designers estimate. With powers of two, a feel for how long common operations take and a few stated assumptions, you can work out traffic, storage and server counts in a couple of minutes.

The big ideas

  • Powers of two. Data sizes go up in steps of about a thousand: a kilobyte is 2 to the 10 bytes, a megabyte 2 to the 20, then gigabyte, terabyte and petabyte. Knowing these makes storage sums quick.
  • Latency numbers. Memory is fast and disk is slow. Reading 1 MB from memory takes about 250 microseconds, from a spinning disk about 30 milliseconds. A packet from California to the Netherlands and back takes about 150 milliseconds. Avoid disk seeks, compress before sending, and put data near users.
  • Availability in nines. Uptime is quoted in nines. 99 percent allows about 3.65 days of downtime a year; 99.9 percent about 8.8 hours; 99.99 percent about 53 minutes; 99.999 percent about 5 minutes.
  • The worked example. A Twitter-like service with 300 million monthly users, half active each day, posting twice a day, gives about 3,500 posts a second and a peak near 7,000. If 10 percent carry 1 MB of media, that is 30 TB a day and about 55 PB over five years.
In the interview

Nobody checks your arithmetic to the decimal place. What the interviewer watches is the process. Round aggressively (99,987 divided by 9.1 becomes 100,000 divided by 10), write your assumptions down so you can revisit them, and label every number with its unit. The usual asks are queries per second, peak QPS, storage, cache size and number of servers.

In plain words

Estimation is about orders of magnitude, not exact answers. State your assumptions, round hard, keep units, and use the result to decide what the design must handle.

Chapter 3 · Forty-five minutes, four steps

A Framework for System Design Interviews

In short

The interview is a collaboration, not an exam. Spend the time in four steps: understand the problem and agree on scope, propose a high-level design, dive deep into the parts that matter, and wrap up.

The big ideas

  • Step 1: understand the problem (3 to 10 minutes). Don't rush to answer. Ask what features matter, how many users, how fast it must grow, what the existing tech stack is. Write the assumptions down.
  • Step 2: a high-level design (10 to 15 minutes). Sketch the main boxes, from clients and APIs to servers, caches and databases, and get the interviewer's agreement. Do rough sums to check the design fits the scale.
  • Step 3: the deep dive (10 to 25 minutes). With the interviewer, pick the components that matter most and go into detail. For a URL shortener it might be the hash function; for chat it might be latency and online status.
  • Step 4: wrap up (3 to 5 minutes). Name bottlenecks and improvements, recap, and talk about error cases, operations, monitoring and the next scale step. Never say the design is perfect.
In the interview

Xu lists what interviewers are really looking for: the ability to collaborate, to work under pressure, to resolve ambiguity and to ask good questions. They also watch for red flags. Over-engineering is a common one, since many engineers enjoy building for scale that will never come. Narrow-mindedness and stubbornness are others. Think out loud, offer more than one approach, and treat hints as help, not as failure.

In plain words

Spend the first minutes asking, not drawing. Agree on a simple design before polishing any part of it, go deep where it counts, and finish by pointing at the weak spots yourself.

Part two · the parts

Building Blocks

Four designs that show up inside almost every bigger system: a rate limiter to protect services, consistent hashing to spread data, a key-value store that survives failures, and a way to hand out unique IDs across many machines.

The idea behind this part

Big systems are built from a small set of reusable parts. Learn the parts well and the big designs become assembly.

Chapter 4 · Saying no, politely

Design a Rate Limiter

In short

A rate limiter caps how many requests a client can send in a period, to stop abuse, cut costs and protect servers. The interesting part is the algorithm, and making it work across many servers.

The big ideas

  • Where it lives. You can limit on the client (easy to bypass), on the server, or in a middleware such as an API gateway. Rejected requests get HTTP 429, Too Many Requests, with headers saying how many are left and when to retry.
  • Token bucket and leaking bucket. A token bucket refills at a steady rate and each request takes a token, so short bursts are allowed; Amazon and Stripe use it. A leaking bucket queues requests and processes them at a fixed rate, which smooths traffic; Shopify uses it.
  • Windows and logs. A fixed window counter is simple but lets twice the limit through around a window edge. A sliding window log keeps every timestamp, which is exact but heavy on memory. A sliding window counter blends the current and previous windows as a cheap approximation.
  • Counters in Redis. Counts are kept in an in-memory store such as Redis, with INCR and EXPIRE. Across many limiter servers you must handle race conditions (a Lua script or sorted sets) and keep them in sync by sharing one central store.
In the interview

The interviewer will usually push on the distributed case. Two requests read the counter at the same moment, both see 3, both write 4, and the limit leaks. Saying where the race is, and how you would close it, earns more credit than picking the perfect algorithm. Also be ready to say what happens to rejected requests: drop them, or queue them for later.

In plain words

A rate limiter is a counter with rules. Choose an algorithm by what you can tolerate: bursts, memory, or a small error, then keep the counters in one fast shared store.

Chapter 5 · Adding a server without chaos

Design Consistent Hashing

In short

If you pick a server with hash(key) mod N, adding or removing one server moves almost every key. Consistent hashing places servers and keys on a ring, so a change moves only a small slice of keys.

The big ideas

  • The rehashing problem. With hash mod N, going from 4 servers to 3 changes the answer for most keys. For a cache, that means a storm of misses all at once.
  • The hash ring. Map the whole hash space (for SHA-1, 0 to 2 to the 160 minus 1) onto a circle. Put servers on it by hashing their names. A key belongs to the first server found going clockwise from the key's spot.
  • Only neighbours move. Add a server and only the keys between it and the previous server move to it. Remove one and only its keys move to the next server. On average, k divided by n keys move.
  • Virtual nodes. With few servers the slices are uneven. Give each server many virtual nodes around the ring and the load evens out; the book cites a standard deviation of 5 to 10 percent of the mean with 100 to 200 virtual nodes.
In the interview

This is often asked as a building block inside a bigger design, like a distributed cache or a key-value store. Be ready to draw the ring, add a server and show which keys move, then explain why virtual nodes are needed. Mentioning who uses it, such as Amazon's Dynamo, Apache Cassandra, Discord and Akamai's CDN, shows you know it is not just theory.

In plain words

Put servers and keys on the same circle and let each key walk clockwise to a server. Changes then disturb only the neighbours, and virtual nodes keep the shares fair.

Chapter 6 · Big, fast, and still standing

Design a Key-Value Store

In short

A distributed key-value store like Dynamo or Cassandra spreads data with consistent hashing, copies it to several servers, and uses quorums to trade consistency against speed and availability.

The big ideas

  • CAP. A distributed system can't promise consistency, availability and partition tolerance all at once. Since networks do fail, the real choice is between consistency (CP, like a bank) and availability (AP) during a partition.
  • Replicate around the ring. Each key is stored on the first N distinct servers clockwise from it. Spreading replicas across data centres protects against a whole site going dark.
  • Quorums. A write must be acknowledged by W replicas and a read must hear from R. If W plus R is greater than N, every read overlaps the latest write, so you get strong consistency. W=1 makes writes fast; R=1 makes reads fast.
  • Living with failure. Vector clocks detect conflicting versions. A gossip protocol spreads heartbeats so nodes notice who is down. Sloppy quorums with hinted handoff ride out short outages, and Merkle trees find which data to repair after a long one.
In the interview

Here the interviewer wants trade-offs, said plainly. If you choose N=3, W=2, R=2, explain that you tolerate one slow or failed replica on each side and still read the latest value. If the product is a shopping cart, explain why availability might matter more and how you would merge conflicting carts. The book also walks the write path (commit log, then memory, then sorted files on disk) and the read path (memory, then a Bloom filter to skip files).

In plain words

Copy every key to N servers. Wait for W to confirm a write and R to answer a read. Make W plus R bigger than N when you need the latest value, smaller when you need speed.

Chapter 7 · 64 bits, no collisions

Design a Unique ID Generator in Distributed Systems

In short

A single database's auto-increment doesn't work across many machines. The book compares four ways to hand out unique IDs and settles on Twitter's Snowflake: a 64-bit number built from a timestamp, a data centre, a machine and a counter.

The big ideas

  • The requirements. IDs must be unique, numbers only, fit in 64 bits, roughly sortable by time, and the system must make over 10,000 of them a second.
  • Three options that fall short. Multi-master replication (each server counts up by k) breaks when servers are added. UUIDs are easy but 128 bits and not numeric or time-ordered. A single ticket server, as Flickr used, is a single point of failure.
  • Snowflake's layout. 1 sign bit, 41 bits of milliseconds since a chosen epoch, 5 bits of data centre ID, 5 bits of machine ID and 12 bits of sequence number. Each machine can make 4,096 IDs every millisecond.
  • Mind the clock. 41 bits of milliseconds last about 69 years from the epoch. Machines must keep their clocks in sync (with NTP), and the bit split can be tuned: fewer sequence bits and more timestamp bits suit low-volume systems.
In the interview

Interviewers often add a twist after you settle on Snowflake: what if a machine's clock jumps backwards? Talk about refusing to issue IDs until the clock catches up, or why NTP matters. Also note that the IDs are only roughly time-ordered across machines, since each one's clock differs a little. That honesty about limits is part of the answer.

In plain words

Pack the time, the place and a counter into one 64-bit number. Each machine can then mint IDs on its own, with no central server and no collisions.

Part three · the classics

Web Services

Four classic interview prompts, each worked from requirements to a deep dive: a URL shortener, a web crawler, a notification system and a news feed. Each one adds a new kind of pressure: reads, scale of the web, reliability, and fan-out.

The idea behind this part

Start from the numbers. How many reads, how many writes, how much data. The numbers tell you which part of the design deserves the deep dive.

Chapter 8 · Long link in, seven characters out

Design a URL Shortener

In short

A URL shortener maps a long link to a short code and redirects people back. The core choices are the redirect type, the code length, and whether codes come from a hash or from a unique ID converted to base 62.

The big ideas

  • The numbers. The book assumes 100 million new links a day: about 1,160 writes a second and, at ten reads per write, 11,600 reads. Over ten years that is 365 billion records, around 36.5 TB at 100 bytes each.
  • 301 or 302. A 301 redirect is permanent, so browsers cache it and later clicks skip your servers. A 302 is temporary, so every click comes back to you, which costs more but lets you count clicks.
  • Seven characters. Using 0 to 9, a to z and A to Z gives 62 symbols. 62 to the 7th is about 3.5 trillion, enough for 365 billion links, so seven characters will do.
  • Hash or base 62. Hashing (CRC32, MD5, SHA-1) and taking the first seven characters needs collision checks, made cheaper with a Bloom filter. Converting a unique ID to base 62 never collides, but the codes are guessable and it depends on an ID generator.
In the interview

The interviewer will often ask why you picked one approach over the other. A strong answer names what each costs: hashing gives fixed-length, unguessable codes but needs a database lookup on every collision; base 62 is simple and collision-free but leaks how many links exist and needs the ID generator from chapter 7. Then cover the read path: cache the hot mappings, because reads outnumber writes ten to one.

In plain words

Give each link a unique number and write it in base 62, or hash it and handle clashes. Seven characters cover hundreds of billions of links, and a cache handles the flood of clicks.

Chapter 9 · Reading the web, politely

Design a Web Crawler

In short

A crawler starts from seed URLs, downloads pages, extracts links and repeats. At a billion pages a month the hard parts are politeness, priority, freshness and not wasting work on duplicates and traps.

The big ideas

  • The scale. 1 billion pages a month is about 400 pages a second, 800 at peak. At 500 KB a page that is 500 TB a month, about 30 PB over five years.
  • The parts. Seed URLs feed a URL frontier. An HTML downloader (with a DNS resolver) fetches pages, a parser checks them, 'content seen?' drops duplicate pages, a link extractor finds new URLs, a filter cleans them, and 'URL seen?' (often a Bloom filter) stops repeats.
  • Breadth-first, but polite. The web is a graph, and breadth-first search is the natural walk. But most links on a page point to the same site, so a naive crawler hammers one host. The frontier keeps one queue per host and one worker per queue, with a delay between requests.
  • Priority and freshness. A prioritiser ranks URLs by usefulness (PageRank, traffic, update rate) into front queues, and pages are recrawled based on how often they change. Robots.txt is respected, and spider traps and noise are filtered out.
In the interview

The book points out that around 30 percent of web pages are duplicates, so 'content seen?' with a hash of the page saves a lot of storage. In the interview, the frontier is the part to dive into. Show the two layers: front queues sorted by priority, back queues split by host for politeness. Then mention the extras that show experience: cache DNS lookups, use short timeouts, spread crawlers geographically, and save crawl state so a crash doesn't start you over.

In plain words

A crawler is a loop: fetch, parse, extract links, filter, repeat. The design work is in the queue that decides what to fetch next, and the checks that stop you fetching the same thing twice.

Chapter 10 · Push, text and email, at scale

Design a Notification System

In short

A notification system sends push alerts, texts and emails through outside providers like Apple's APNs, Firebase, Twilio and SendGrid. The design adds queues between parts so one failing channel doesn't take down the rest, and a log so nothing is lost.

The big ideas

  • Three kinds, four providers. iOS pushes go through APNs, Android through Firebase Cloud Messaging, texts through a service like Twilio or Nexmo, and email through SendGrid or Mailchimp. Device tokens, phone numbers and emails are collected when users sign up.
  • The first design breaks. One notification server doing everything is a single point of failure, is hard to scale, and becomes a bottleneck when a provider is slow.
  • Queues per channel. Notification servers check settings and rate limits, then put each message on a queue for its channel. Workers pull from the queues and call the providers. If texting is down, push and email keep flowing.
  • Reliability. Messages are saved in a notification log and retried if sending fails. Exactly-once delivery is impossible to promise, so an event ID is used to drop duplicates. Templates, opt-out settings, rate limits and tracking finish the design.
In the interview

Expect the interviewer to ask, 'what if the third-party provider goes down?' or 'how do you make sure a user doesn't get the same alert twice?'. The answers are the two pillars of the chapter: retries from a durable log, and deduplication by event ID. It also helps to say that users must be able to opt out of each channel, and that too many notifications make people turn them off entirely.

In plain words

Put a queue between deciding to notify and actually sending, one per channel, and keep a log so failures can be retried. Use an ID on every event so retries don't become spam.

Chapter 11 · Push, pull, or both

Design a News Feed System

In short

A news feed shows posts from the people you follow. When someone posts, you can copy it to every follower's feed right away (fan-out on write) or assemble feeds when people open them (fan-out on read). Real systems mix the two.

The big ideas

  • Two flows. Feed publishing stores a new post and spreads it to friends. Feed building gathers a user's feed when they open the app. The book assumes up to 5,000 friends per user and 10 million daily users.
  • Fan-out on write (push). Precompute every follower's feed as soon as a post is made, so reading is fast. But a user with millions of followers turns one post into millions of writes, and many go to people who never log in.
  • Fan-out on read (pull). Build the feed when it is requested. Nothing is wasted on inactive users and celebrities are no problem, but opening the feed is slow because it has to gather posts from everyone you follow.
  • Hybrid. Push for most users, pull for celebrities: followers fetch a famous account's posts at read time and merge them in. The feed cache holds only post and user IDs, with a content cache, social graph cache, action cache and counter cache beside it.
In the interview

The celebrity problem is the trap in this question. If you only propose fan-out on write, a good interviewer will ask what happens when a pop star with 100 million followers posts. Getting ahead of it, by naming the hot key problem and proposing the hybrid, is the moment that separates a memorised answer from a reasoned one. Keep the feed cache small by storing IDs, not whole posts.

In plain words

Decide where to pay: when posting or when reading. Pay at posting time for ordinary users so feeds load instantly, and at reading time for celebrities so one post doesn't trigger millions of writes.

Big Systems

Chapter 12 · Messages in real time

Design a Chat System

In short

A chat app needs messages to arrive instantly in both directions. That rules out plain HTTP requests and points to WebSockets, stateful chat servers, a key-value store for history, and heartbeats for showing who is online.

The big ideas

  • The scope. One-to-one and group chat (up to 100 people), online status, multiple devices per user, push notifications, and 50 million daily users. Messages up to 100,000 characters.
  • Getting messages to the client. Polling asks the server over and over, mostly for nothing. Long polling holds each request open until there is a message or a timeout. WebSocket keeps one two-way connection open, which is the book's choice for sending and receiving.
  • Stateless and stateful parts. Login, profiles and service discovery (with ZooKeeper) are ordinary stateless services. Chat servers are stateful: each holds open connections, so service discovery assigns each user to the best server for them.
  • Storage and IDs. Chat history is huge and read mostly by recency, so a key-value store fits; Facebook Messenger uses HBase and Discord uses Cassandra. Message IDs must be sortable, from a global generator or a per-chat sequence. Each device tracks its latest message ID to sync.
In the interview

The online indicator is a favourite follow-up. Logging a user offline every time their connection drops would make them flicker on bad networks. Instead, the client sends a heartbeat every few seconds, and the user is shown as offline only if heartbeats stop for a while (the book uses 5 seconds and 30 seconds). Then say how status reaches friends: a publish and subscribe channel per friendship, which works for small groups but not for groups of thousands.

In plain words

Keep a live two-way connection to each online device, store messages in a store built for huge, recent-first data, and use heartbeats so a shaky network doesn't make people flicker offline.

Chapter 13 · Top five, in a tenth of a second

Design a Search Autocomplete System

In short

As you type, autocomplete suggests the five most popular searches that start with your letters, within about 100 milliseconds. The heart of it is a trie, with the top answers cached at every node, rebuilt from logs on a schedule.

The big ideas

  • The numbers. 10 million daily users making 10 searches a day of about 20 characters, with a request for each character typed, comes to about 24,000 queries a second and 48,000 at peak.
  • A trie. A tree of letters where each path spells a prefix. Finding suggestions means walking down to the prefix and then searching everything below it, which is slow if the subtree is big.
  • Two speed-ups. Cap the prefix length (people rarely type 50 characters) and cache the top five queries at every node. Then answering is just walking to the node and reading its list, at the cost of extra memory.
  • Built offline. Search logs are aggregated (weekly, in the book's design) and workers rebuild the trie, which is stored in a database and kept in a cache. Browsers cache results too; Google keeps them for an hour. Filters remove hateful or dangerous suggestions.
In the interview

When you scale this up, the interviewer may ask how to shard the trie. Splitting by first letter sounds neat but gives uneven shards, since far more words start with 's' than with 'x'. The book's answer is a shard map manager that splits by actual query volume. Also mention sampling the logs (keep one query in N) to cut processing costs, and what changes for real-time trends, where weekly rebuilds are too slow.

In plain words

Store searches as a tree of letters, keep the best few answers ready at every branch, and rebuild the tree from logs in the background so lookups stay instant.

Chapter 14 · Upload, transcode, stream

Design YouTube

In short

A video service has two flows: uploading, where videos are stored and converted into many formats, and streaming, where they are served from a CDN close to viewers. The biggest bill is the CDN, and the design looks for ways to cut it.

The big ideas

  • The numbers. The book assumes 5 million daily users watching 5 videos each, with 10 percent uploading one 300 MB video a day: 150 TB of new storage daily. Serving all views from a CDN at 2 cents a gigabyte costs about $150,000 a day.
  • Transcoding. Raw uploads are huge and every device wants something different, so each video is encoded into several resolutions, bitrates and formats. The work is modelled as a graph of tasks (a DAG), split across video, audio and metadata, run by a scheduler and many workers.
  • Faster uploads. Clients split videos into chunks by group of pictures and upload them in parallel, to upload centres near the user. Message queues between each step let them run in parallel too. Pre-signed URLs make sure only allowed users upload.
  • Cutting costs. Video popularity is a long tail: a few videos get most views. Serve the popular ones from the CDN and the rest from your own servers, encode fewer versions of rarely watched videos, keep regional hits regional, and partner with internet providers.
In the interview

This question rewards a clear split between the two flows, with a diagram for each. A good deep dive is the transcoding pipeline: why a DAG, why the steps are separated by queues, and how failures are handled (retry recoverable errors like a failed upload chunk, reject non-recoverable ones like a corrupt file). Bringing up the CDN bill unprompted shows you think like someone who will have to pay it.

In plain words

Store the original, convert it into many versions in a pipeline of small tasks, and stream from a CDN. Then save money by putting only the popular videos on the CDN.

Chapter 15 · Files that follow you

Design Google Drive

In short

A cloud drive stores files, keeps every device in sync, keeps old versions and lets people share. Files are split into blocks so only the changed parts are sent, and file metadata is kept strongly consistent.

The big ideas

  • The numbers. 50 million signed-up users, 10 million daily, 10 GB free each: 500 PB in total. Two uploads a day of about 500 KB each gives about 240 uploads a second, 480 at peak.
  • From one server to many. Start with a single server storing files under a folder per user. Then move files to cloud storage like Amazon S3 (replicated across regions), add load balancers and more API servers, and move metadata into a replicated database.
  • Block servers. Files are cut into blocks (Dropbox uses up to 4 MB), each compressed, encrypted and stored. Delta sync means only blocks that changed are uploaded. Identical blocks, found by their hash, are stored once.
  • Sync and conflicts. A notification service (using long polling) tells other devices when a file changes. If two people edit the same file, the first version processed wins and the second gets a conflict to merge or override. Offline devices catch up from a backup queue.
In the interview

The interviewer will probe the trade-off between strong and eventual consistency. For file metadata the book insists on strong consistency, since showing different versions of the same file on two devices is unacceptable, which is why it picks a relational database with ACID guarantees. For saving space, mention deduplication, limiting the number of stored versions, and moving rarely used data to cold storage like Amazon S3 Glacier.

In plain words

Split files into blocks, send only the blocks that changed, store identical blocks once, and keep the file list strictly consistent so every device agrees on what exists.

Chapter 16 · Where to go next

The Learning Continues

In short

The book closes by pointing beyond itself: read how real companies built their systems, follow their engineering blogs, and practise. Each design in the book reuses the same handful of building blocks, and spotting them is the skill.

The big ideas

  • Real-world systems. Xu lists write-ups of how large companies built their systems, from Facebook's timeline and Netflix's architecture to Twitter's timelines at scale and Uber's real-time market platform.
  • Engineering blogs. Companies like Airbnb, Amazon, Dropbox, LinkedIn, Netflix, Slack, Uber and many more publish how they solved real problems. They are free and current.
  • Same parts, new shapes. A ride-sharing app needs real-time connections like chat; a ticket sale needs rate limiting; a search box needs a trie. Most new prompts are combinations of designs you have already seen.
  • Practice, out loud. Knowing a design and explaining one under time pressure are different skills. Talk through designs with a friend or a timer, and use the four-step framework every time.
In the interview

Many interviewers deliberately ask questions that aren't in any book. That is not a trap; it is the point. They want to see you reach into a toolbox: 'this part needs fresh data at huge read volume, so I'll cache it'; 'this needs to survive a provider outage, so I'll put a queue in front'. If you can name the building block and its trade-off, an unfamiliar prompt becomes a familiar one.

In plain words

The book's twelve designs are a toolbox, not a script. Read how real systems were built, practise out loud, and for any new problem, ask which familiar parts it is made of.
