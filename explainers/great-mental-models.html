The Great Mental Models,
Explained

A mental model is a simple picture of how some part of the world works, one you can carry from problem to problem. Shane Parrish, who writes the Farnam Street blog, set out to collect the most useful of them from across physics, biology, economics and history. Volume 1: General Thinking Concepts covers the nine that apply to almost everything, from knowing that your map is not the territory to not assuming malice when a mistake will do. This guide takes each one in turn, with a lab in every chapter where you can put the model to work.

The book in one line

You think better with many simple models drawn from many fields than with one clever theory you apply to everything.

Start Here

Before the first model, the book makes its case for collecting models at all. Most bad decisions don't come from a lack of intelligence. They come from looking at a problem through one narrow lens, from ego, and from being too far from the results to learn.

The idea behind this part

The quality of your thinking depends on the models in your head. Collect many, from many fields, and use them together.

Introduction · Why collect models at all

Acquiring Wisdom

In short

The world is too complicated to understand all at once, so we rely on simplified pictures of how it works. The more good pictures you have, from more fields, the fewer things you miss. One model alone turns every problem into the same problem.

The big ideas

  • A model is a shortcut. Supply and demand, natural selection, compounding: each is a compact way of explaining a slice of reality. You already use models all the time; the question is how many good ones you have.
  • The man with a hammer. If the only tool you own is a hammer, every problem looks like a nail. A specialist who sees everything through one discipline will be confidently wrong whenever the problem lives somewhere else.
  • Build a latticework. Charlie Munger's phrase. Hang your experience on a lattice of models drawn from many fields, so each new fact connects to something and each problem can be seen from several angles.
  • Three things get in the way. Perspective (we can't see the whole system we're part of), ego (we would rather be right than learn), and distance (the further we are from the results of a decision, the less it can teach us).
From the book

The book's starting point is a 1994 talk Charlie Munger, Warren Buffett's partner, gave to business students. You can't really know anything, he said, if you only remember isolated facts. You need models from many disciplines, joined in a latticework, because to a man with a hammer every problem looks pretty much like a nail. Parrish spent years collecting such models on his Farnam Street blog. This volume is the first set: the general thinking tools that work on almost any problem, before the later volumes take on physics, biology, systems and economics.

In plain words

Before deciding anything that matters, ask which lenses you're using. If the answer is one, borrow another. The aim isn't to know every model, it's to stop seeing every problem as a nail.

Models 1 and 2 · see clearly

See Clearly

The first two models are about honesty: with the tools you use to describe the world, and with yourself. Every map leaves things out, and every person knows less than they think outside a fairly small area.

The idea behind this part

Know the limits of your map and the edge of your circle. Most expensive mistakes happen just past one or the other.

Model 1 · Every model leaves things out

The Map Is Not the Territory

In short

A map is useful precisely because it simplifies: it leaves out almost everything so you can see what matters. The trouble starts when we forget that, and treat the map as if it were the ground itself.

The big ideas

  • Simplifying is the point. A map the size of the land would be useless. Reports, budgets, scientific theories and job titles are all maps: they help because they compress.
  • Reality is the referee. When the map and the territory disagree, the territory wins. Check your maps against the ground, and update them when they're wrong.
  • Consider the cartographer. Maps carry the views and interests of whoever drew them. Ask who made it, for what purpose, and what they had reason to leave out.
  • Maps go stale. The ground keeps changing after the map is printed. A model that was right ten years ago can quietly turn wrong, and nothing on the paper will tell you.
From the book

The phrase comes from Alfred Korzybski, who used it in a paper in 1931. The book's best illustration is the commons. In 1968 Garrett Hardin described a pasture shared by herders: each gains by adding one more cow, so together they ruin it. His map said shared resources must be privatised or controlled from above. Elinor Ostrom went out and studied the territory: Swiss alpine meadows, Japanese forests and Spanish irrigation channels that communities had shared for centuries without ruining, using rules they had built themselves. Hardin's map was a useful warning, but it wasn't the ground. Ostrom won the Nobel prize in economics in 2009 for looking.

In plain words

Whenever you rely on a summary, a forecast or a rule of thumb, ask what it leaves out and when it was last checked against reality. Use maps, but keep looking up from them.

Next: Circle of competence →
Model 2 · Know where your knowledge ends

Circle of Competence

In short

Everyone has areas they truly understand, built from years of experience and feedback. Inside that circle you can decide quickly and well. The danger is not having a small circle; it's not knowing where its edge is.

The big ideas

  • Lifer versus stranger. Someone who has lived in a town all their life knows which roads flood, who to trust and what the locals mean. A visitor with a guidebook knows the names. Competence is that lived detail.
  • Size matters less than edges. A small circle you know well beats a large one you only think you have. Overconfidence lives right at the boundary.
  • Keep it growing, honestly. Circles shrink if you stop learning. Grow them with curiosity, a record of your own predictions, and feedback from people who will tell you when you're wrong.
  • Outside the circle, borrow. Learn the basics, ask an expert whose circle it is, and question them until you understand their reasoning. Admitting "I don't know" is a skill, not a weakness.
From the book

Most climbers who reach the summit of Everest do it behind Sherpa guides. The climbers may be rich, fit and brave, but the Sherpas have spent their lives on that mountain: they know the weather, the ice and how the body fails at altitude. That is a circle of competence, earned over years. The book sets beside it Queen Elizabeth I, who ruled England for 44 years in dangerous times. She was clever and well educated, and still she gathered capable advisers and listened to them on what she didn't know. Knowing the edge of her own circle was part of why she lasted.

Supporting idea · falsifiability

Karl Popper's test for a real theory: it has to say what would prove it wrong. "This stock will go up eventually" can never fail, so it tells you nothing. Ideas that can't be tested sit outside anyone's circle, however confidently they're held.

In plain words

Before a decision, ask honestly: have I done this before, did I get feedback, could I explain where I might be wrong? If not, you're outside the circle. Slow down, and find someone whose circle it is.

Models 3 to 5 · think it through

Think It Through

The next three models are ways of thinking a problem through more carefully than habit allows: break it down to what is certainly true, explore it safely in your head, and follow the consequences further than the first step.

The idea behind this part

Go down to the foundations, run the experiment in your head, and keep asking "and then what?"

Model 3 · Down to the foundations

First Principles Thinking

In short

Break a problem down into the parts you know are true, and set aside everything that's just assumption, habit or "how it's always been done". Then build back up. It's how you find answers nobody else has tried.

The big ideas

  • Separate facts from assumptions. Most of what we "know" about a problem is inherited: conventions, analogies, what others did. First principles are the pieces that are still standing after you question everything.
  • Socratic questioning. What exactly do I think, and why? How do I know it's true? What's the evidence? What if I'm wrong? What follows if it's right?
  • The five whys. Keep asking why. Each answer peels a layer off, until you either hit something solid or find the answer is "because that's what we do".
  • Incremental versus fresh. Improving on what exists keeps you inside its assumptions. Rebuilding from foundations can open options the incremental path never reaches.
From the book

For most of the twentieth century, doctors "knew" that stomach ulcers were caused by stress and acid, and that nothing could live in the stomach. Patients took antacids for years. In the early 1980s two Australian doctors, Robin Warren and Barry Marshall, kept seeing spiral bacteria in biopsies of inflamed stomachs. They questioned the assumptions instead of the evidence. When colleagues stayed sceptical, Marshall drank a culture of the bacteria, fell ill with gastritis and cured himself with antibiotics. Ulcers turned out to be largely caused by Helicobacter pylori and curable in weeks. The pair won the Nobel prize in 2005.

In plain words

When a problem feels stuck, list everything you believe about it. Ask of each item: do I know this, or did I inherit it? Keep only the facts, and see what new options appear.

Next: Thought experiment →
Model 4 · Test it in your head

Thought Experiment

In short

Some experiments are impossible, too expensive or too dangerous to run. You can still run them in your imagination, carefully: set up the situation, change one thing, and follow the logic to see what must happen.

The big ideas

  • A method, not a daydream. Ask a question, gather what you know, form a hypothesis, test it in your mind, and draw a conclusion. The discipline is what separates it from wishful thinking.
  • Imagine the impossible. Riding beside a beam of light. Being born into a society without knowing who you'll be. You can't do either, but you can reason about them.
  • Re-run history. Ask what would have happened if one thing had changed. It helps you separate what was skill from what was luck, and what really caused what.
  • Expose contradictions. A good thought experiment can break a theory without any equipment, just by showing it disagrees with itself.
From the book

Aristotle taught that heavier things fall faster, and for nearly two thousand years most people agreed. Galileo, in a book published in 1638, defeated the idea with a thought experiment. Take a heavy stone and a light one, and tie them together. If heavy things fall faster, the light stone should drag on the heavy one, so the pair falls slower than the heavy stone alone. But the pair is heavier than the heavy stone, so it should fall faster. It can't do both. The theory contradicts itself, so it must be wrong: all things must fall at the same rate, air aside. No tower needed. Einstein used the same tool at sixteen, imagining chasing a beam of light.

Supporting idea · necessity and sufficiency

What's necessary for an outcome isn't the same as what's enough. Being able to write well is necessary to become a famous novelist; it is nowhere near sufficient. Thought experiments help you sort the conditions you must have from the ones that would actually get you there.

In plain words

Before trying something big, run it in your head first. Change one variable at a time, follow the logic honestly, and look for the point where the story contradicts itself.

Model 5 · And then what?

Second-Order Thinking

In short

Almost anyone can predict the immediate result of a decision. Second-order thinking asks what happens next, and after that. The effects that arrive later are often the ones that matter most, and they can point in the opposite direction.

The big ideas

  • First-order is easy. Cut prices, sales go up. Skip sleep, get more done tonight. The first effect is obvious, which is why it's what everyone optimises for.
  • Keep asking "and then what?" Follow each consequence one more step, over time: in ten minutes, ten months, ten years. Look for who reacts and how.
  • It can win arguments. Pointing to the long-term benefits for everyone, not just the immediate gain for one side, can persuade people the first-order case never reaches.
  • Don't overdo it. You can't trace every ripple. Second-order thinking isn't a reason to freeze, and it isn't the same as a fearful slippery-slope argument. Follow the likely chains, then decide.
From the book

From the 1940s, farmers found that small doses of antibiotics in animal feed made livestock grow bigger and faster. The first-order effect was cheaper meat, and the practice spread around the world. The second-order effect took longer: bacteria living in those animals were bathed in low doses of antibiotics, and the ones that resisted survived and spread. Drug-resistant infections now reach people. The book contrasts this with Mary Wollstonecraft, who in 1792 argued for educating women. Rather than stopping at fairness to women, she laid out the knock-on benefits: better partners, better parents and a stronger society, a second-order case aimed at the people she needed to convince.

In plain words

Before you act, ask "and then what?" at least twice. Think about how other people will react, and what the result looks like a year from now, not just tomorrow.

Models 6 and 7 · weigh the odds

Weigh the Odds

The world rarely gives certainty, so these two models are about acting well without it. Think in chances instead of yes-or-no answers, and when the forward road is foggy, approach the problem from the far end.

The idea behind this part

Estimate the odds honestly, update as you learn, and ask what would make things go wrong.

Model 6 · Think in odds

Probabilistic Thinking

In short

The future isn't certain, so stop treating it as yes or no. Estimate how likely each outcome is, start from how often things like this usually happen, update as evidence comes in, and pay special attention to rare events with huge consequences.

The big ideas

  • Bayesian thinking. Start with the prior: how common is this in general? Then let new evidence move you, by as much as it deserves and no more. A dramatic headline about a rare event doesn't make it common.
  • Fat-tailed curves. Height clusters around the average; nobody is ten metres tall. Wealth, wars and pandemics don't: the extremes are far more likely than a bell curve suggests. Know which kind of world you're in.
  • Asymmetries. We tend to be overconfident in our estimates, and errors mostly run one way: projects run late far more often than early. Adjust for it.
  • Prepare, don't predict. In a fat-tailed world you can't forecast the next big shock, but you can be robust to it: keep slack, avoid ruin, and position yourself to benefit from surprises.
From the book

During the Second World War, Vera Atkins helped run the French section of Britain's Special Operations Executive, which sent agents into occupied France. Every decision was a bet made with scraps of information: who to send, where, and whether a network or a radio contact could still be trusted. There was no way to be certain, so she had to weigh the odds and act, knowing that a wrong estimate could cost lives. After the war, she spent years tracing what had happened to the agents who never came back.

Supporting idea · causation versus correlation

Two things moving together doesn't mean one causes the other. Often a third factor drives both, or it's chance. A special case is regression to the mean: after an unusually good or bad result, the next one is usually closer to average, whatever you did in between. Before you credit a cause, ask what would have happened anyway.

In plain words

When you hear news or get a result, first ask how common the thing is in general. Then ask how much this evidence should move you. Hold estimates loosely, update them, and make sure no single bad outcome can wipe you out.

Model 7 · Work it backwards

Inversion

In short

Instead of only asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure, and avoid it. Or start from the result you want and work backwards to what must be true. Turning a problem around often shows you what the forward view hides.

The big ideas

  • Invert, always invert. The mathematician Carl Jacobi's advice for hard problems. Assume the opposite of what you want to prove, or start from the end, and see what follows.
  • Avoid stupidity. Munger's version: it's often easier to stop doing the dumb things than to find the brilliant one. Ask "how could I ruin this?" and then don't.
  • Start at the end. Picture the result already achieved. What would have to be true for it to happen? Then make those things true.
  • Remove what holds you back. Kurt Lewin's force field analysis: a situation sits where the forces pushing for change balance the forces resisting it. Removing a resisting force often works better than pushing harder.
From the book

In the 1920s, piano makers asked the publicist Edward Bernays to sell more pianos. The forward approach was obvious: advertise pianos. Bernays inverted it and asked what would have to be true for people to want one. His answer was a home with a music room. So he set about making the music room fashionable, getting respected people to talk about it and architects to design houses with one. Once families had a music room, they needed something to put in it. People started calling the piano makers instead of the other way round.

In plain words

When you're stuck on how to succeed, flip it: list the surest ways to fail, and stop doing them. Ask what's holding the problem in place, not just what would push it forward.

Models 8 and 9 · cut through

Cut Through

The volume ends with two razors: quick rules for shaving away explanations that are probably wrong. One prefers the simpler story; the other prefers the honest mistake to the hidden plot.

The idea behind this part

When several explanations fit, start with the one that needs the fewest assumptions and the least villainy.

Model 8 · Fewer assumptions, better odds

Occam's Razor

In short

When several explanations fit the facts, start with the one that needs the fewest assumptions. Every extra assumption is another thing that has to be true, and the chances multiply down fast.

The big ideas

  • Simpler is likelier. Each assumption has its own chance of being wrong. An explanation that needs five things to all be true is usually far less likely than one that needs one.
  • Horses, not zebras. Doctors are taught: when you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras. Common causes are common. Check them first.
  • It saves time and effort. Starting with the simple explanation means you test the likeliest thing first, and you don't build elaborate plans on top of speculation.
  • Simple isn't always right. The razor is a starting point, not a law. When the evidence demands a more complicated answer, follow the evidence. Some things really are complex.
From the book

The razor is named after William of Ockham, a fourteenth-century English friar, though the idea is older. The book's modern example is the astronomer Vera Rubin. In the 1970s she and Kent Ford measured how fast stars orbit at the edges of galaxies, and found they moved far too fast for the visible matter to hold them. Either our understanding of gravity was badly wrong in many places at once, or there was a lot of matter we couldn't see. The explanation that needed fewest new assumptions was the unseen matter. It was surprising, but it was the simplest thing that fitted. Most astronomers now accept dark matter, though what it is remains open.

In plain words

When something goes wrong, list the possible explanations and count what each one needs to be true. Check the one with the fewest moving parts first, and only move on when the evidence says so.

Next: Hanlon's razor →
Model 9 · Mistakes before malice

Hanlon's Razor

In short

Don't put down to malice what can be explained by carelessness, ignorance or a mistake. Most people aren't plotting against you; they're busy, distracted or wrong. Assuming the worst makes you paranoid and leads to worse decisions.

The big ideas

  • The world isn't about you. When a driver cuts you off or an email goes unanswered, the likely reason is that they're preoccupied, not that they're out to get you.
  • We over-blame. We tend to explain other people's behaviour by their character, and our own by circumstances. Hanlon's razor corrects for that bias.
  • Malice is a heavy assumption. Intent requires motive, planning and secrecy. A mistake needs only a person having a bad day. It's Occam's razor applied to people.
  • A default, not blindness. Some people really do mean harm, and scams are designed to look like accidents. Start with the charitable reading, but keep your guard where the stakes are high and the pattern repeats.
From the book

In October 1962, during the Cuban Missile Crisis, the Soviet submarine B-59 was hiding deep under the Caribbean, out of contact with Moscow, when American ships began dropping small depth charges. The Americans meant them as signals to surface. Inside the hot, failing submarine, the captain assumed war had started and wanted to fire a nuclear torpedo. Launching needed the agreement of three senior officers. One of them, Vasili Arkhipov, refused: the charges could be something other than an attack. The submarine surfaced instead. By declining to assume the worst, Arkhipov may have prevented a nuclear war.

In plain words

When someone's behaviour annoys or hurts you, look for the ordinary explanation first: they forgot, they didn't know, they were rushed. Ask before you accuse, and save suspicion for patterns, not one-offs.
