Part one · chapters 1 to 9

Two Systems

Kahneman starts with two characters. System 1 runs automatically, quickly and without effort: it reads faces, finishes sentences and jumps to conclusions. System 2 is the slow, effortful thinker you believe you are: it does sums, checks arguments and makes choices. Part one shows how they divide the work, and why the lazy partnership goes wrong in predictable ways.

Kahneman's big claim

Most of what you think and do comes from System 1. System 2 believes it is in charge, but mostly it endorses what System 1 has already decided.

Chapter 1 · System 1 and System 2

The Characters of the Story

In short

Your mind runs two ways of thinking. System 1 is fast, automatic and always on. System 2 is slow, effortful and lazy. Almost everything interesting in the book comes from how they interact.

The big ideas

  • System 1 just happens. You see an angry face and know the person is angry. You read "2 + 2" and 4 appears. You can't stop it and you didn't ask for it. System 1 runs on instinct and on skills practised so long they have become automatic.
  • System 2 has to be switched on. Multiply 17 by 24, fill in a tax form, look for a woman with white hair in a crowd. These need attention, and they get disrupted when attention is pulled away. System 2 is the part of you that feels like "you": the one that chooses, reasons and controls.
  • They divide the work sensibly. System 1 suggests impressions, feelings and intentions all day long. Usually System 2 accepts them with little or no change. When System 1 hits something it can't handle, or notices a surprise, it calls in System 2.
  • System 1 can't be turned off. In the Müller-Lyer illusion, two equal lines with arrow fins look different lengths. Measuring them convinces System 2, but you still see them as different. Knowing about a bias doesn't make it go away. The best you can do is learn to recognise situations where mistakes are likely.
  • They are characters, not brain regions. Kahneman is clear that the two systems are a useful fiction, a way of talking about two kinds of mental process. There is no little System 2 sitting in a particular spot in your head.
In plain words

Think of System 1 as an autopilot that is brilliant at familiar flying and quick to react, and System 2 as a pilot who is capable but would rather read a magazine. Most of the time the autopilot is fine. The problems come when it confidently flies into a situation it doesn't understand and the pilot doesn't look up.

Chapter 2 · Mental effort is limited

Attention and Effort

In short

Thinking hard costs real effort, and you have a limited budget of it. Your pupils widen when you strain, you go blind to things you aren't attending to, and your mind prefers whatever path costs least.

The big ideas

  • Effort shows in your eyes. Kahneman measured pupils while people did a task called Add-1: hear a string of digits, then say each one plus one. Pupils widened steadily as the work piled up and shrank back the moment people finished or gave up. The pupil is a fairly faithful meter of mental effort.
  • Attention has a budget. You can talk while driving on an empty road, but you stop talking when you overtake a lorry on a narrow road. Ask a friend to calculate 17 × 24 while walking and they will usually stop walking. When effort is overloaded, System 2 protects the most important task and lets the rest go.
  • Intense focus makes you blind. In the famous "invisible gorilla" study, people counting basketball passes failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. About half missed it entirely, and they were sure they would have seen it.
  • The law of least effort. If there are several ways to reach a goal, people drift toward the least demanding one. Skill helps: as you get better at something, it takes less energy, and the brain shows less activity doing it.
  • Switching and holding are hard. Keeping several ideas in memory at once, following rules, and switching quickly between tasks are what System 2 does, and what makes it tire. Time pressure makes it worse.
In plain words

Mental effort is like a phone battery with a small capacity. Hard thinking drains it fast, and when it's running low your phone, and your mind, switch into a low-power mode that only does the essentials.

Next: The Lazy Controller →
Chapter 3 · System 2 would rather not

The Lazy Controller

In short

System 2 is supposed to check System 1's work, but it is lazy. If an answer feels right, it usually gets waved through, even among very clever people.

The big ideas

  • The bat and the ball. A bat and ball cost $1.10. The bat costs $1 more than the ball. How much is the ball? Ten cents pops into your head, and it's wrong (it's 5 cents). More than half of students at Harvard, MIT and Princeton gave the intuitive answer, and over 80% at less selective universities. Checking would have taken seconds. They didn't check.
  • Believable conclusions skip the logic. "All roses are flowers. Some flowers fade quickly. So some roses fade quickly." Most students call this valid. It isn't: the flowers that fade might not be roses. When a conclusion sounds true, we stop checking whether it follows.
  • Self-control and thinking draw on the same effort. People who are busy holding seven digits in memory are more likely to choose chocolate cake over fruit salad. When System 2 is busy, System 1 has more say over what we do.
  • Flow is effort without strain. Deep concentration on something you enjoy, like painting or racing a motorbike, can feel effortless. In flow, keeping your attention on the task needs no self-control, so more effort is left for the task itself.
  • Some people are more engaged. Keith Stanovich separates intelligence from rationality. Some smart people still go with the first answer; others habitually check. Walter Mischel's marshmallow studies found that four-year-olds who could wait for a second treat tended to do better on many measures years later.
In plain words

System 2 is like a supervisor who signs off reports without reading them because they look about right. It can do the checking, it just usually doesn't, and the cleaner and more confident the report looks, the less likely it is to be read.

Worth knowing

Since the book: "ego depletion", the idea that willpower runs out like a fuel tank, was a big part of this chapter. Large replication attempts since 2016 have found the effect much weaker than first reported, or absent. Kahneman acknowledged the problem. The bat-and-ball result and the general laziness of System 2 have held up well.

Next: The Associative Machine →
Chapter 4 · Ideas wake up other ideas

The Associative Machine

In short

Every idea in your head is connected to many others. When one is activated, it quietly lights up its neighbours, nudging what you think, feel and even do next, without you noticing.

The big ideas

  • Banana. Vomit. Read those two words and in a second or two your mind builds a little story, your face makes a small grimace, and you feel a slight aversion to bananas. None of it was chosen. This is associative activation: one idea sets off a cascade of others, in the body as well as the mind.
  • Priming. If you have just seen the word EAT, you are more likely to complete SO_P as SOUP. If you saw WASH, you are more likely to write SOAP. The earlier word made related ideas easier to reach.
  • Action can be primed too. In one study, students who unscrambled sentences with words linked to old age (Florida, forgetful, bald) were reported to walk down a hallway more slowly afterwards. In another, an image of watching eyes above an office "honesty box" for coffee money was linked to people paying nearly three times as much as when the image was flowers.
  • It works in reverse. Being made to smile can make cartoons seem a little funnier; nodding while listening can make a message seem more agreeable. The links between ideas and actions run both ways.
  • You are a stranger to yourself. Kahneman's point is that much of what shapes our choices happens out of view. When you explain a decision, you are often making up a plausible reason after the fact.
In plain words

Your memory is less like a filing cabinet and more like a spider's web. Touch one strand and the whole neighbourhood trembles. Whatever is trembling at the moment is what comes to mind most easily next.

Worth knowing

Since the book: behavioural priming is the part of this book that aged worst. The slow-walking "Florida effect" and money-priming studies failed to replicate in later attempts, and in 2012 Kahneman wrote an open letter warning of a "train wreck" in this field. Later he said he had placed too much faith in small studies. Word priming (EAT and SOUP) is solid; the claims about priming behaviour are doubtful.

Next: Cognitive Ease →
Chapter 5 · What feels easy feels true

Cognitive Ease

In short

Your mind constantly checks how easily things are going. When processing feels easy, things seem familiar, true, good and effortless. When it feels strained, you get vigilant and suspicious.

The big ideas

  • A dial from easy to strained. Clear print, a repeated message, a good mood and a primed idea all make the dial read "easy". Hard-to-read fonts, unfamiliar words and a bad mood push it toward "strain". You rarely notice the dial, but it changes your judgments.
  • Repetition breeds belief. Repeat a statement often enough and it starts to feel true, because familiarity is hard to tell apart from truth. Even hearing part of a statement before ("the body temperature of a chicken") makes the whole thing feel more familiar.
  • Make it easy to read. Statements are more likely to be believed when printed in high contrast, clear type, when they rhyme ("Woes unite foes" beat "Woes unite enemies"), and when the source has an easy-to-say name. Companies with easy-to-pronounce names did better just after their stock market listing.
  • Strain wakes up System 2. When the bat-and-ball type puzzles were printed in a small, faded font, far fewer students made mistakes: 35% instead of 90% got at least one wrong. The difficulty made them slow down and check.
  • Mere exposure. Robert Zajonc showed that just seeing something repeatedly, a word, a face, a shape, makes people like it more, even when they don't remember seeing it. Familiar means safe to an animal brain.
In plain words

A well-designed advert, a slogan you hear every day and a confident friend all feel more believable for the same reason: they are easy to process. Ease feels like truth, so anyone who wants to be believed tries to be easy to understand. And anyone who wants to think clearly should be a little suspicious of the warm feeling of "that sounds right".

Chapter 6 · How System 1 models the world

Norms, Surprises, and Causes

In short

System 1 keeps a model of what's normal, notices instantly when something breaks it, and looks for causes everywhere, even where there are none.

The big ideas

  • Normal is learned fast. See something odd once and the second time it is less surprising. Kahneman met an acquaintance unexpectedly at a remote spot; meeting him again elsewhere felt almost expected. System 1 builds norms from very little evidence.
  • Surprise is fast too. Brain responses show surprise within a fraction of a second when a sentence ends oddly ("Earth revolves around the trouble"). Yet familiar-feeling errors slip through: "How many animals of each kind did Moses take into the ark?" Most people answer two, without noticing it was Noah.
  • We see causes. In Albert Michotte's experiments, when one square moves into another and the second starts moving, people see the first "launch" the second. You don't infer the cause, you perceive it, just as you see a colour.
  • We see intentions. In Heider and Simmel's short animation of moving triangles and a circle, viewers see a bully, a victim and a rescue. Kahneman suggests this readiness to see agents behind events may make religious belief come naturally to people.
  • Statistics fight our nature. Because System 1 thinks in causes and stories, it is poor at statistical thinking, which deals with what is true of groups and chance. This sets up much of part two.
In plain words

Your mind is a story machine. Show it two events next to each other and it will tell you that one caused the other, with total confidence and without being asked. That instinct is usually useful, which is why it is so hard to switch off when the real explanation is chance.

Next: A Machine for Jumping to Conclusions →
Chapter 7 · WYSIATI and the halo effect

A Machine for Jumping to Conclusions

In short

System 1 builds the most coherent story it can from whatever information it has, and doesn't notice what's missing. Kahneman calls this WYSIATI: What You See Is All There Is.

The big ideas

  • Jumping is efficient, until it isn't. Jumping to conclusions saves time when you're likely to be right and mistakes are cheap. It's risky when the situation is unfamiliar, the stakes are high and there's no time to collect more information.
  • Believing comes first. Daniel Gilbert argued that to understand a statement you must first try to believe it. Unbelieving is System 2's job. When people are tired or distracted, they are more likely to believe nonsense.
  • The halo effect. If you like someone's politics, you probably like their voice and appearance too. First impressions colour everything after. Solomon Asch showed that "Alan: intelligent, industrious, impulsive, critical, stubborn, envious" is liked more than the very same list in reverse order.
  • Decorrelate errors. Ask people to guess the number of coins in a jar one by one, and the average is very good, because individual errors cancel out. Let them talk first and the errors line up. Before a meeting, have everyone write down their view privately.
  • WYSIATI. People given only one side of a legal case were more confident in their judgment than people who heard both sides. Less information made the story more coherent, and coherence is what feels like confidence.
In plain words

Someone tells you a new colleague is "brilliant and a bit difficult". Another person says "difficult, but brilliant". Same facts, different person in your head. The first word is the lens you see the rest through, and you rarely stop to ask what nobody has told you yet.

Next: How Judgments Happen →
Chapter 8 · Basic assessments and the mental shotgun

How Judgments Happen

In short

System 1 is constantly sizing things up: is this safe, is this normal, should I approach or avoid? It also translates easily between scales, and fires off more answers than you asked for.

The big ideas

  • Basic assessments never stop. Evolution built us to check constantly for threats and opportunities. Within a glance we judge whether a stranger is friend or foe, and whether a situation is normal.
  • Faces decide elections. Alexander Todorov showed people photos of candidates for a fraction of a second. Their snap rating of "competence" (a strong chin plus a slight, confident smile) predicted the winner in about 70% of US Senate, Congress and governor races. Voters who knew little about politics and watched a lot of TV were most swayed.
  • Averages yes, sums no. System 1 handles averages and prototypes easily but is bad at totals. Ask people how much they would pay to save 2,000, 20,000 or 200,000 birds from oil ponds and the answers barely differ: about $80, $78 and $88. They are pricing the image of one bird, not the number.
  • Intensity matching. System 1 can match how impressive one thing is to a different scale: "Julie read fluently at four" becomes "as tall as a man of six foot ten" or "a GPA of 3.7". This is useful, but it leads to over-extreme predictions (chapter 18).
  • The mental shotgun. When asked whether words rhyme, people slow down if the spellings differ (VOTE, GOAT), because System 1 compares the spelling too, though nobody asked. We compute more than we need, and the extra answers leak into our judgments.
In plain words

Your gut is a judge that never sleeps and always gives a verdict, whether or not it has the evidence. That verdict arrives before you have even decided to think about the question, and it has a way of becoming your answer.

Next: Answering an Easier Question →
Chapter 9 · Substitution

Answering an Easier Question

In short

When faced with a hard question, System 1 often swaps in an easier one and answers that instead, without noticing the switch. The answer feels right because it is the right answer, just to a different question.

The big ideas

  • Target and heuristic questions. The target question is what you meant to answer. The heuristic question is the simpler, related question you actually answered. "How happy are you with your life?" quietly becomes "What is my mood right now?"
  • Order changes everything. Students were asked "How happy are you these days?" and "How many dates did you have last month?" In that order, the answers were unrelated. Reversed, so dating came first, the correlation jumped to about 0.66. The dating question had become the happiness question.
  • The mood heuristic. People asked about their life satisfaction answer differently on a sunny day than a grey one, or after finding a coin on a photocopier. Mood stands in for the big question.
  • The affect heuristic. Paul Slovic found that people let their likes and dislikes decide what they believe. If you like a technology, you think its benefits are high and risks low. Feelings come first and the reasons follow.
  • It feels like answering. Substitution is so smooth that you rarely know it happened. You don't feel stumped by the hard question. You feel you answered it.
In plain words

It's like being asked "Is this a good investment?" and answering "Do I like this company?" Both questions are close enough that the swap feels natural, but only one of them is about whether you'll make money.

Part two · chapters 10 to 18

Heuristics and Biases

When a question is hard, System 1 quietly answers an easier one: how easily examples come to mind, how much someone fits a stereotype, which number was mentioned first. These shortcuts usually work. Part two maps where they fail, especially with anything statistical: small samples, base rates, chance and regression.

Kahneman's big claim

We are pattern seekers who think in stories, not statistics. Our intuitions about chance are badly wrong, and even experts fall for the same traps.

Chapter 10 · Small samples, big swings

The Law of Small Numbers

In short

Small samples produce extreme results far more often than big ones. Because System 1 wants causes, we explain these swings with stories instead of seeing them as noise.

The big ideas

  • The kidney cancer puzzle. US counties with the lowest kidney cancer rates are mostly rural, sparsely populated and in the Midwest, South and West. It is tempting to credit clean country living. But the counties with the highest rates are also mostly rural and sparse. Small populations swing to both extremes.
  • Pure arithmetic. Draw 4 marbles from a jar that's half red, half white, and all four will be the same colour 12.5% of the time. Draw 7 and it happens only about 1.6% of the time. Nothing causes it; small samples are just noisier.
  • Even researchers fall for it. Kahneman and Tversky found that experienced researchers, including statisticians, chose sample sizes so small that their studies often had little chance of finding real effects. They trusted their intuitions more than the maths.
  • Small schools. The most successful schools tend to be small, so the Gates Foundation and others invested in breaking big schools into small ones. But the worst schools also tend to be small. Small groups simply vary more.
  • We see patterns in randomness. In London during the Blitz, people believed the bombing targeted some areas and spared others. Analysis showed the hits were consistent with chance. Basketball's "hot hand" was long thought to be the same kind of illusion.
In plain words

If a tiny village has three cases of a rare illness in a year, it looks like a cluster with a cause. A big city with the same rate would have hundreds and nobody would notice. Before you look for a reason why a small group is extreme, ask whether it is just small.

Worth knowing

Since the book: in 2018 Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo found a subtle flaw in the original hot hand analysis. Corrected, the data show a small real hot hand in basketball shooting. The bigger point stands: people see far more streaks than chance can explain away.

Next: Anchors →
Chapter 11 · The first number sticks

Anchors

In short

When you estimate a number after seeing another one, your estimate stays close to it, even if the first number was obviously random.

The big ideas

  • A rigged wheel. Kahneman and Tversky spun a wheel of fortune, marked 0 to 100 but rigged to stop at 10 or 65. Students then guessed the percentage of African nations among UN members. Those who saw 10 guessed 25% on average; those who saw 65 guessed 45%.
  • Two engines. Anchoring works two ways. System 2 starts from the anchor and adjusts, but stops too soon, when it is no longer sure it should keep going. System 1 is primed by the anchor and finds evidence that fits it.
  • Experts too. Estate agents valued a house after seeing a listing price. Those shown a high price valued it higher, yet insisted the listing price hadn't influenced them. Judges given a random dice roll before sentencing gave longer sentences after a high roll.
  • Measuring it. Asked whether the tallest redwood is more or less than 1,200 feet, people guessed 844 feet on average; after an anchor of 180 feet, they guessed 282. The anchoring index was 55%: the gap in answers was more than half the gap in anchors.
  • Anchors are everywhere. "Limit of 12 per customer" signs make people buy more. An opening offer in a negotiation pulls the outcome toward it. Kahneman's advice: if the other side opens with an outrageous offer, make a scene and refuse to negotiate from that number.
In plain words

The first price you see for a car, a salary figure mentioned in passing, the "was £200" on a sale label: they all become the starting point your mind adjusts from, and it never adjusts far enough. The fix is to set your own anchor first, from your own research.

Next: The Science of Availability →
Chapter 12 · Easy to recall feels common

The Science of Availability

In short

We judge how common or likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. Things that are vivid, recent or personal feel more frequent than they are.

The big ideas

  • The availability heuristic. How many divorces among people over 60? How dangerous are plants? We answer by noticing how easily instances come to mind. A celebrity scandal, a dramatic plane crash or something that happened to you gets more weight than dry statistics.
  • More than 100%. Ask each partner in a couple what share of the housework they do, and the answers usually add up to more than 100%. Each remembers their own efforts far more vividly than their partner's. The same goes for team projects.
  • Ease beats amount. Norbert Schwarz asked people to list six times they had been assertive, or twelve. Twelve is hard. Those who listed twelve then rated themselves less assertive than those who listed six. The struggle to come up with examples outweighed the number they found.
  • Explaining the difficulty away. When people were told background music would make recall harder, the effect disappeared: they blamed the music, not their own assertiveness. The feeling of ease is used only when nothing else explains it.
  • Who relies on ease most? People lean more on ease of retrieval when they are in a good mood, when they feel powerful, when they are novices, and when they are busy with something else: whenever System 2 is less involved.
In plain words

If it took you ages to think of three times you were generous, you'll conclude you aren't very generous, even though you did find three. Your mind reads the effort, not the count. That is why a single vivid memory can outweigh a whole spreadsheet.

Chapter 13 · Fear, media and cascades

Availability, Emotion, and Risk

In short

Our sense of risk is shaped by what we see in the news and how it makes us feel. Dramatic, frightening deaths seem common; quiet killers seem rare.

The big ideas

  • Distorted death. Paul Slovic's team found that people judged accidents as causing as many deaths as disease, though disease kills about 18 times as many. Tornadoes seemed deadlier than asthma, which kills about 20 times more. The media cover what is novel and dramatic, and that is what we remember.
  • The affect heuristic. When people are asked about the risks and benefits of a technology, their answers line up with how they feel about it. Telling people a technology has big benefits makes them rate its risks lower too, though no new information about risk was given.
  • Experts and the public. Slovic argues that the public has a richer idea of risk than experts' death counts: whether a risk is chosen, fair, and how deaths happen matter to people. Cass Sunstein argues that public fear distorts policy and wastes money. Kahneman sees merit in both.
  • Availability cascades. A minor story gets media coverage, which causes public worry, which drives more coverage, which drives more worry. The Love Canal toxic waste scare and the Alar apple scare grew this way, until it seemed irresponsible to question them.
  • Probability neglect. With vivid threats, like terrorism, we react to the image of the bad outcome and barely to the probability. A tiny risk and a moderate one can produce the same fear.
In plain words

A shark attack makes the news around the world; heart disease doesn't. So sharks feel dangerous and cheeseburgers don't. Our fear tracks the stories we hear and the pictures we see, not the numbers that actually kill us.

Next: Tom W's Specialty →
Chapter 14 · Representativeness vs base rates

Tom W's Specialty

In short

When we judge how likely something is, we ask how much it resembles our stereotype and ignore how common it is in the first place. Kahneman calls these base rates.

The big ideas

  • Tom W. Tom W is described as intelligent, orderly, a bit mechanical, with little sympathy for others. Which field is he studying? People pick computer science or engineering, though those fields have very few students compared with humanities or education. Even psychology graduate students who knew the numbers ignored them.
  • Representativeness. We judge probability by similarity: does he look like a typical member? It works when the stereotype is accurate and the groups are similar in size. It fails badly when one group is much smaller than another.
  • Sin one: predicting unlikely events. Someone reading the New York Times on the subway is more likely to have a PhD than to have no degree? No: there are vastly more people without a degree. A good fit to a stereotype can't overcome a tiny base rate.
  • Sin two: ignoring the quality of evidence. The description of Tom was written by a psychologist from personality tests taken years earlier. Is it reliable? People treat it as fully trustworthy. WYSIATI: the evidence is in front of you, so it's taken as true.
  • The discipline of Bayes. Anchor your estimate on the base rate. Then ask how much the evidence really shifts it. Thomas Bayes's rule says exactly how: the odds after the evidence equal the odds before, times how much more likely the evidence is under one answer than another.
In plain words

If someone is quiet and loves books, are they more likely to be a librarian or a salesperson? It feels like librarian. But salespeople outnumber librarians by so much that even if only a few of them are quiet bookworms, there are more quiet bookworm salespeople than librarians.

Next: Linda: Less Is More →
Chapter 15 · The conjunction fallacy

Linda: Less Is More

In short

Adding details to a story makes it more convincing and less likely. When a detailed story feels more plausible than a simple one, logic has lost to representativeness.

The big ideas

  • Linda. Linda is 31, single, outspoken and very bright. She studied philosophy and was deeply concerned with discrimination and social justice. Which is more probable: Linda is a bank teller, or Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement? Most people choose the second.
  • It can't be more likely. Every feminist bank teller is a bank teller. Adding a condition can only shrink the group. Between 85% and 90% of undergraduates at several major universities still ranked the feminist bank teller as more probable. This is the conjunction fallacy.
  • Plausibility is not probability. The extra detail makes the story fit Linda better, and System 1 confuses "fits better" with "more likely". Forecasters do it too: a scenario with a detailed chain of causes feels more plausible than the bare outcome.
  • Less is more. Christopher Hsee priced two dinnerware sets. Set A had 40 pieces, some broken. Set B had 24 pieces, all intact (and all also included in A). Seen alone, B was valued more ($33 vs $23). The broken pieces lowered the average quality of A. Seen side by side, people saw that A has more and paid more for it.
  • Frequencies help. "Of 100 people like Linda, how many are bank tellers, and how many are feminist bank tellers?" puts the groups in plain view, and errors drop sharply.
In plain words

"He'll lose his job and then move abroad" sounds like a more believable story than "he'll move abroad", because it comes with a reason. But it must be less likely: it requires both things to happen. Every extra detail is one more thing that has to come true.

Next: Causes Trump Statistics →
Chapter 16 · We learn from stories, not numbers

Causes Trump Statistics

In short

We ignore base rates that are just statistics, but use them readily when they come with a cause. And learning a surprising statistic rarely changes what we believe about individuals.

The big ideas

  • The cab problem. A cab hit someone at night. 85% of the city's cabs are Green and 15% are Blue. A witness says the cab was Blue, and witnesses in those conditions are right 80% of the time. The most common answer is 80% likely to be Blue. The correct answer is 41%.
  • Add a cause. Change one detail: the two companies have the same number of cabs, but Green cabs are involved in 85% of accidents. Now people take the base rate into account, because it suggests a cause: Green drivers are reckless. Statistical base rates are ignored; causal base rates are used.
  • Stereotypes are causal base rates. Treating "Green drivers are reckless" as a reason is exactly how stereotypes work. Kahneman notes the uncomfortable trade-off: refusing to use stereotypes is socially right, but can cost accuracy in a narrow statistical sense.
  • The helping experiment. In a classic study, only 4 of 15 people rushed to help someone apparently having a seizure, because others were around. Students taught this were no better at predicting whether two nice-seeming individuals had helped. They learned the statistic but not the lesson.
  • Surprise teaches. Students did change their predictions when shown a video of two ordinary people who did not help. A surprising individual case moved them; a surprising statistic didn't. People reason from the particular to the general far more readily than from the general to the particular.
In plain words

Telling people that "most people in this situation behaved badly" doesn't make them believe they would. Showing them one ordinary person who behaved badly does. A good story about one person does more than an accurate number about a million.

Next: Regression to the Mean →
Chapter 17 · Luck evens out

Regression to the Mean

In short

Extreme results tend to be followed by more ordinary ones, simply because luck that helped or hurt the first time is unlikely to repeat. We invent causes for what is really statistics.

The big ideas

  • The flight instructors. Israeli Air Force instructors told Kahneman that praise didn't work: cadets praised for a great landing did worse next time, while those shouted at after a bad one improved. Kahneman realised that great and terrible landings partly reflect luck, and the next one would be closer to average regardless of what the instructor said.
  • Talent and luck. Success = talent + luck. Great success = a little more talent + a lot of luck. On the first day of a golf tournament, the leaders were probably lucky too. On day two, their luck is unlikely to hold, so they tend to slip back.
  • The cover jinx. Athletes on the cover of Sports Illustrated often had a worse season next. No curse: they made the cover after an unusually good season.
  • Fake treatments. Depressed children given an energy drink will improve over three months, but so will depressed children who stand on their heads. Extreme scores drift back toward average. That's why treatments need a control group.
  • Wherever correlation is imperfect. Regression shows up whenever two measures are not perfectly correlated. Tall parents tend to have tall children, but slightly less tall. Brilliant women tend to marry men less brilliant than they are. Not because of any cause, but because of arithmetic.
In plain words

When a teacher shouts at a student after their worst test and the next test is better, the teacher learns that shouting works. It probably didn't: after an unusually bad day, almost anything is followed by a better one. Regression rewards punishment and punishes praise, which is a cruel lesson to learn wrongly.

Next: Taming Intuitive Predictions →
Chapter 18 · How to correct a gut forecast

Taming Intuitive Predictions

In short

Intuitive predictions are as extreme as the evidence feels. Good predictions are pulled back toward the average, by an amount that depends on how reliable the evidence is.

The big ideas

  • Julie. "Julie is a university senior. She read fluently when she was four. What is her GPA?" People answer around 3.7 or 3.8. That's intensity matching: early reading is impressive, so they pick an equally impressive GPA. But reading at four has only a weak link to grades at university.
  • The four steps. Start with the baseline: the average GPA. Next, the GPA that matches your impression. Then estimate the correlation between the evidence and the outcome. Finally move from the baseline toward your impression by that fraction. A correlation of 0.3 means moving 30% of the way.
  • Unbiased, but less exciting. Corrected predictions are never as extreme as the evidence feels. You will never predict a rare event, like an unknown start-up becoming the next Google. That's the price of being right on average.
  • When to bend the rule. Sometimes missing a rare success is worse than a wrong call: a venture capitalist needs to spot the next big win. Then extreme guesses may make sense, as long as you know they are extreme.
  • Two confidences. Kahneman warns that the corrected prediction will feel wrong. System 1 finds an extreme prediction from vivid evidence natural. Moderation is a System 2 achievement, and it needs effort every time.
In plain words

If a new restaurant gets one glowing review, your gut predicts it's one of the best in town. A careful forecaster says: most restaurants are average, a single review says a bit, so it's probably a bit better than average. Less exciting, much more often right.

Next: The Illusion of Understanding →
Part three · chapters 19 to 24

Overconfidence

The mind builds a tidy story from whatever it sees, and the tidier the story, the more confident we feel, regardless of how little evidence went into it. Part three looks at the illusion of understanding the past, the illusion of skill in predicting the future, when simple formulas beat experts, and when expert intuition can actually be trusted.

Kahneman's big claim

Confidence is a feeling about how coherent a story is, not a measure of how likely it is to be true.

Chapter 19 · Stories, hindsight and halos

The Illusion of Understanding

In short

We tell neat stories about the past that make it seem inevitable, and those stories make us think the future is more predictable than it is.

The big ideas

  • The narrative fallacy. Nassim Taleb's term for the flawed stories we build to explain the past. Good stories are simple, concrete, give a big role to talent and intentions, and leave out the countless events that could have gone differently. The story of Google's rise reads like skill; it leaves out the luck.
  • "I knew it all along." Before President Nixon's trips to China and Russia in 1972, Baruch Fischhoff asked people how likely various outcomes were. Afterwards, people remembered having given higher odds to what actually happened. Once we know the outcome, we can't recall what we believed before.
  • Outcome bias. We judge decisions by how they turned out, not by whether they were sensible at the time. A surgeon whose routine operation went wrong is blamed; a CEO who made a reckless bet that worked is called visionary. This makes decision-makers cautious and bureaucratic, and rewards lucky gamblers.
  • Business books. Books like Built to Last compare successful companies with less successful ones and find the "secrets". But in the years after such studies, the gap in profits and returns between the excellent firms and the others shrank to almost nothing. Much of the original gap was luck, and luck regresses.
  • How much do leaders matter? If the correlation between a CEO's quality and their firm's success is a generous 0.30, then the firm with the stronger CEO will be the more successful one in only about 60% of pairs. That's only 10 points better than a coin toss.
In plain words

When you read how a successful founder "always knew" their idea would work, remember that you only hear from the ones it worked for. The story makes success look planned. If you could see all the equally confident founders who failed, the lesson would be "be lucky".

Next: The Illusion of Validity →
Chapter 20 · Confidence without skill

The Illusion of Validity

In short

People can feel completely confident in judgments that have been shown again and again to be no better than chance. Confidence comes from the coherence of the story, not the quality of the evidence.

The big ideas

  • The leaderless group. In the Israeli army, Kahneman watched candidates for officer training try to get over a wall as a team. The observers felt sure they could see who would lead. Months later, feedback showed their predictions were barely better than blind guesses. The next batch, they felt just as sure.
  • Stock pickers. Kahneman studied eight years of results for 25 wealth advisers at one firm. The correlation between their rankings from one year to the next was 0.01, essentially zero. The firm was paying bonuses for luck. When he told the executives, they didn't argue. They didn't change anything either.
  • Most investors lose by trading. Terry Odean found that individual investors who traded most did worst. The stocks they sold tended to do better than the stocks they bought. Professional fund managers, as a group, mostly fail to beat the market index year after year.
  • Expert forecasters. Philip Tetlock collected over 80,000 predictions from 284 experts in politics and economics. They did worse than simple rules, like "assume no change". The more famous the forecaster, the more overconfident they tended to be.
  • The world is hard to predict. The problem isn't that pundits are fools. Long-term political and economic events are mostly unpredictable. The error is in feeling, and acting, as if they are not.
In plain words

If you flipped coins every year and called the people with the most heads "star investors", you'd still get a ranking, and the stars would still feel brilliant. The only way to tell skill from luck is whether the ranking holds next year.

Next: Intuitions vs. Formulas →
Chapter 21 · Simple rules beat experts

Intuitions vs. Formulas

In short

In many fields, simple formulas that add up a few factors predict better than trained experts. Humans are inconsistent; formulas aren't.

The big ideas

  • Meehl's little book. In 1954 Paul Meehl compared experts' predictions with simple scoring rules, for things like students' grades and parole violations. The rules won in most cases. Later reviews of around 200 studies found algorithms beat or matched experts in nearly all of them.
  • The wine formula. Orley Ashenfelter predicted the future price of Bordeaux vintages from just three weather facts: summer temperature, harvest rain and the previous winter's rain. His formula did better than the famous tasters.
  • Why experts lose. Experts try to be clever and consider complex combinations of features. Worse, they are inconsistent: shown the same case twice, they often give different answers. A formula always gives the same answer to the same input.
  • Equal weights work. Robyn Dawes showed that you don't even need well-tuned weights. Pick a few relevant factors, score them, add them up with equal weight. One example: how often a couple makes love minus how often they argue does a decent job of predicting marital stability.
  • The Apgar score. In 1953 anaesthetist Virginia Apgar wrote down five quick checks for a newborn, each scored 0, 1 or 2. It gave delivery rooms a standard and is credited with helping to cut infant deaths. Kahneman used the same idea to fix the Israeli army's interviews: score six traits one at a time, then make the overall call.
In plain words

When hiring, most interviewers chat for an hour and go with their gut. Kahneman's advice: decide in advance which few traits matter, score each one separately with facts, and add them up. Then, if you must, close your eyes and make a gut call. His army data showed both together predicted far better than gut alone.

Next: Expert Intuition: When Can We Trust It? →
Chapter 22 · Skill needs regularity and feedback

Expert Intuition: When Can We Trust It?

In short

Expert intuition is real when the world is regular enough to learn and the expert has had lots of practice with quick feedback. Without those, confident intuition is often an illusion.

The big ideas

  • An adversarial collaboration. Gary Klein studies the remarkable intuitions of firefighters and nurses; Kahneman studies the failures of intuition. Instead of arguing, they worked together to find where their views met.
  • The firefighter. A fire commander ordered his team out of a burning house without knowing why. Moments later the floor collapsed. He had picked up subtle cues: the fire was quiet and his ears were unusually hot. The fire was in the basement under them.
  • Intuition is recognition. Herbert Simon put it simply: the situation gives a cue; the cue gives the expert access to memory; the memory gives the answer. Nothing more, nothing less. Chess masters see a board the way you read a word.
  • Two conditions. An environment regular enough to be predictable, and long practice with timely feedback. Chess, firefighting, nursing and anaesthesiology qualify. Stock picking and long-term political forecasting don't: the outcome is too noisy or comes too late.
  • Confidence isn't evidence. Subjective confidence comes from how coherent the story feels. You can't tell a real expert from a lucky or deluded one by how sure they sound. Ask instead whether they had a chance to learn the regularities.
In plain words

A taxi driver who has driven a city for twenty years can feel a traffic jam coming; trust them. A friend who has bought shares for twenty years and feels the market is about to rise has learned much less, because the market isn't regular and the feedback is mostly noise. Same confidence, very different value.

Next: The Outside View →
Chapter 23 · The planning fallacy

The Outside View

In short

Planning from the inside, by imagining how your project will unfold, is reliably too optimistic. Look at how similar projects actually turned out, and start from there.

The big ideas

  • The textbook. Kahneman's team was writing a decision-making curriculum for Israeli schools. Everyone guessed about two years to finish. Then he asked Seymour Fox, the curriculum expert, how long similar teams took. About 40% never finished; the rest took 7 to 10 years. The team carried on anyway. It took eight years, and the book was never used.
  • The planning fallacy. Plans and forecasts that are close to best-case scenarios, and could be improved by looking at the statistics of similar cases. In 2002 a survey of American homeowners remodelling their kitchens found they expected to spend about $18,658 on average and actually spent $38,769.
  • Big public projects. The Scottish Parliament building was estimated at up to £40 million in 1997; it ended up costing about £431 million. Studies of rail projects worldwide found passenger numbers were overestimated in more than 90% of cases.
  • Reference class forecasting. Bent Flyvbjerg's method: identify the right class of similar projects, find their typical outcomes, and use that as a baseline. Then adjust for specific features of your case, but only a little.
  • Why we ignore it. The inside view is detailed and feels like knowledge. The outside view feels irrelevant ("our project is different"). And there's pressure to promise less time and money to get approved.
In plain words

Ask yourself not "how long will this take me?" but "how long did it take other people who tried something like this?" Your plan always looks quick because you can't imagine the problems you haven't had yet. Other people's results include those problems.

Next: The Engine of Capitalism →
Chapter 24 · Optimism and the premortem

The Engine of Capitalism

In short

Optimism drives people to start businesses, invent and take risks, but it makes them badly overconfident. One simple ritual, the premortem, helps.

The big ideas

  • Optimistic founders. In the US, about 35% of small businesses survive for five years. Yet in one survey, 81% of entrepreneurs put their own odds of success at 7 out of 10 or higher, and 33% said their chance of failure was zero.
  • Competition neglect. Founders focus on what they will do and forget that rivals are doing the same. Hollywood studios release big films on the same weekend, each sure of its own plan. Colin Camerer and Dan Lovallo found people enter markets too often because they compare themselves to the average, not to the other entrants.
  • Confident CFOs. In a study of over 11,000 forecasts of the stock market by finance chiefs, the correlation between their forecasts and the real results was slightly below zero. Their 80% confidence ranges were so narrow that the result fell outside them about two-thirds of the time.
  • Optimism has value. Optimists are more resilient, happier and healthier, and they keep the economy moving. Persistence despite the odds sometimes pays off. Kahneman doesn't want to cure optimism, just to tame it where it costs us.
  • The premortem. Gary Klein's idea: before a big decision, say "Imagine it's a year from now. We went ahead with the plan and it was a disaster. Take ten minutes to write the history of that disaster." It frees doubters to speak and gets the team to look for threats.
In plain words

Before you quit your job to open a café, imagine it's two years later and the café has closed. Write down why. Most people find three or four very plausible reasons within minutes, reasons that were invisible while they were imagining success.

Next: Bernoulli's Errors →
Part four · chapters 25 to 34

Choices

Economists assumed people are "Econs": rational, consistent and selfish. Kahneman and Amos Tversky studied "Humans" instead, and built prospect theory, the work that won Kahneman the Nobel prize. Part four shows that we judge outcomes as gains and losses from a reference point, that losses hurt about twice as much as gains please, and that the way a choice is framed can flip the answer.

Kahneman's big claim

We don't value wealth, we value changes in it. Losses loom larger than gains, and the same choice described two ways is not the same choice to us.

Chapter 25 · Econs, Humans and reference points

Bernoulli's Errors

In short

For 250 years economists judged choices by the final wealth they lead to. But people feel changes, gains and losses from where they are now, not levels of wealth.

The big ideas

  • Econs and Humans. Economic theory assumed an "Econ": a rational, selfish agent with stable tastes. Psychologists know people are "Humans". Kahneman and Tversky set out to describe how Humans actually choose between risky options.
  • Bernoulli's idea. In 1738 Daniel Bernoulli suggested people don't value money itself but its utility, and that each extra dollar adds less as you get richer. A gift of 10 ducats means more to a poor man than a rich one. This explains why people buy insurance and avoid gambles.
  • Jack and Jill. Today Jack and Jill each have $5 million. Yesterday Jack had $1 million and Jill had $9 million. Are they equally happy? Bernoulli's theory says yes, same wealth. Obviously not: Jack is thrilled, Jill is devastated. Bernoulli left out the reference point.
  • Anthony and Betty. Anthony has $1 million and Betty $4 million. Both are offered a gamble or a sure $2 million. For Anthony the sure thing is a gain; for Betty it's a loss. Same final wealth on offer, opposite feelings, and they will choose differently.
  • Theory-induced blindness. Why did scholars miss this for centuries? Once you accept a theory, it is very hard to notice its flaws. You assume there must be some explanation you're missing.
In plain words

A pay rise to £50,000 feels wonderful if you were on £40,000 and like a punishment if you were on £60,000. Your bank balance is the same either way. What you feel is the direction of travel, measured from where you started.

Next: Prospect Theory →
Chapter 26 · Gains, losses and loss aversion

Prospect Theory

In short

We judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, we feel less and less as the amounts grow, and losses hurt about twice as much as equal gains feel good.

The big ideas

  • Three principles. One: outcomes are judged against a reference point, usually the status quo. Two: diminishing sensitivity, the difference between $100 and $200 feels bigger than between $900 and $1,000. Three: loss aversion, losses loom larger than gains.
  • Two problems. Would you take a sure $900 or a 90% chance of $1,000? Most take the sure thing. Would you take a sure loss of $900 or a 90% chance to lose $1,000? Most gamble. We avoid risk with gains and seek it with losses.
  • How big is loss aversion? Offered a coin toss where you lose $100 on tails, most people need to win around $200 on heads before they will accept. The loss aversion ratio has been estimated in many experiments, usually between 1.5 and 2.5.
  • The S-shaped curve. The value function is concave for gains, convex for losses and steeper on the loss side. That one picture is the heart of the theory that won Kahneman the 2002 Nobel prize in economics (Tversky had died in 1996).
  • What it misses. Prospect theory can't handle disappointment and regret: a 90% chance of a big prize that comes to nothing hurts more than losing a lottery ticket. Kahneman notes the theory traded some of that richness for simplicity.
In plain words

Finding £20 on the street is nice. Losing £20 from your pocket ruins your afternoon. That asymmetry, loss aversion, is behind why people hold on to losing shares, refuse fair bets and fight harder to keep what they have than to get something new.

Next: The Endowment Effect →
Chapter 27 · What's mine is worth more

The Endowment Effect

In short

Once we own something, giving it up feels like a loss, so we demand much more to sell it than we would pay to buy it.

The big ideas

  • Professor R. Richard Thaler noticed that an economics professor who loved wine would not sell a bottle from his cellar for $100, but would never pay more than $35 for one. The same bottle had two prices depending on whether he owned it.
  • The mugs. In an experiment by Kahneman, Thaler and Jack Knetsch, students were given coffee mugs and asked their selling price. Others were asked what they would pay. Sellers asked about $7.12; buyers offered about $2.87. Choosers, who could pick a mug or cash without owning either, valued it about $3.12, close to buyers.
  • Use vs exchange. The effect applies to things we hold for use, like a mug or concert tickets. It doesn't apply to things held for trade: a shopkeeper doesn't mourn selling a pair of shoes, and you don't mind giving up a £5 note for five £1 coins.
  • Experience reduces it. John List found that experienced traders at baseball card conventions showed little endowment effect. Those who trade a lot learn to see goods as exchangeable.
  • Poverty changes the frame. For the poor, Kahneman notes, every purchase is a loss: buying one thing means giving up another. Small trade-offs that richer people barely notice hurt.
In plain words

Try selling your old car and you'll think the dealer's offer is insulting. Try buying the same car and you'll think the asking price is crazy. You're not being dishonest either time; owning something genuinely changes how much it's worth to you.

Next: Bad Events →
Chapter 28 · Negativity and loss aversion everywhere

Bad Events

In short

The brain gives priority to bad news. Threats are noticed faster, losses weigh more, and we work harder to avoid falling short of a goal than to exceed it.

The big ideas

  • Threats get through first. Pictures of frightened eyes that people can't consciously see still trigger the brain's threat system. An angry face pops out of a crowd of happy faces, but not the other way around.
  • Bad is stronger than good. John Gottman's research on couples found that a stable relationship needs good interactions to outnumber bad ones by at least five to one. A single act of betrayal can wreck years of trust.
  • Goals are reference points. New York cab drivers aimed for a daily earnings target. On busy rainy days they hit it early and went home, though those were the best days to keep working. Falling short of a goal is felt as a loss.
  • Golfers and par. Devin Pope and Maurice Schweitzer studied 2.5 million putts. Professionals sank putts for par more often than putts of the same length for birdie. Par is the reference point; missing it is a loss, so they try harder.
  • Fairness. A hardware store raises the price of snow shovels from $15 to $20 the morning after a snowstorm. 82% of people called it unfair. Firms can pass on their own losses, but exploiting their power to cut what customers are used to paying feels like a loss imposed on them, and people will punish it.
In plain words

One rude comment from a colleague can stay with you all week, while five compliments fade by lunchtime. That's not you being sensitive, it's how the brain is built. The practical upshot: avoid the bad moments, because the good ones have to do a lot of work to make up for them.

Next: The Fourfold Pattern →
Chapter 29 · Decision weights

The Fourfold Pattern

In short

We don't weigh outcomes by their real probability. Small chances get too much weight and near-certainties too little, and that produces four different attitudes to risk.

The big ideas

  • The possibility effect. Going from 0% to 5% feels huge: suddenly it could happen. That's why people buy lottery tickets, and why a tiny risk of disaster weighs on us.
  • The certainty effect. Going from 95% to 100% also feels huge: now it's guaranteed. People pay a lot to remove the last bit of doubt, and settle lawsuits they are very likely to win.
  • Decision weights. Kahneman and Tversky estimated how much weight people give each probability. A 1% chance gets the weight of about 5.5%; a 50% chance about 42%; a 99% chance about 91%. Only 0% and 100% are weighted accurately.
  • Four combinations. High chance of a gain: risk averse (take the settlement). High chance of a loss: risk seeking (gamble on a long shot to avoid a sure loss). Low chance of a gain: risk seeking (lotteries). Low chance of a loss: risk averse (insurance).
  • The costly corner. Desperate gambles to avoid a sure loss are where much damage is done: businesses losing to new technology spend their last money trying to turn things around, and losing armies fight on too long.
In plain words

A 1-in-a-million lottery ticket feels like it could change your life, so you pay more than it's worth. A 99% safe operation feels scary, so you'd pay a lot to make it 100%. Our feelings stretch the ends of the probability scale and squash the middle.

Next: Rare Events →
Chapter 30 · Vividness and the denominator

Rare Events

In short

We either ignore rare events entirely or give them far too much weight. Vivid images, and numbers framed as counts of people, push us toward overweighting.

The big ideas

  • Bus bombs. During a period of suicide bombings on buses in Israel, Kahneman knew the risk was tiny but still felt uneasy waiting beside a bus. The vivid image of the event, not its probability, drove the feeling.
  • Vivid outcomes, vivid odds. A vivid description of the outcome makes us weigh it more. So does a vivid description of the probability: "1 in 1,000" calls up an image of the one person; "0.1%" doesn't.
  • Denominator neglect. Urn A has 10 marbles, 1 red. Urn B has 100 marbles, 8 red. Draw red to win. Between 30% and 40% of students choose B, because 8 winning marbles look like more chances, though B's odds are worse.
  • Frames of counts. A disease that kills "1,286 out of 10,000 people" was judged more dangerous than one that kills "24.14% of the population", though the second is twice as deadly. Psychiatrists were more likely to refuse release of a patient when told "10 of every 100 patients like Mr Jones" commit violence than when told "a 10% chance".
  • Experience vs description. When people learn about probabilities from experience instead of a description, they tend to underweight rare events, because they usually haven't met them yet. That's why people living in flood or earthquake zones often don't insure.
In plain words

"One in a thousand people get this side effect" feels scarier than "0.1% risk", because you can picture the one person. Same number. Whenever a risk is stated, translate it into both forms and see which one is grabbing you.

Next: Risk Policies →
Chapter 31 · Think broad, not narrow

Risk Policies

In short

Taking each risky decision on its own makes us too cautious. Seen as one of many similar bets, most small favourable gambles are clearly worth taking.

The big ideas

  • Narrow framing. Faced with two decisions at once, people decide each separately. In a classic pair of problems, most people make two choices that together are worse in every outcome than an alternative pair. A broad frame would have spotted it.
  • Samuelson's colleague. Paul Samuelson offered a colleague a coin toss: win $200 or lose $100. He refused one toss, but said he would happily take 100 of them. Samuelson pointed out that he was being inconsistent; Kahneman thinks the colleague's instinct about many tosses was right and his rejection of one was the error.
  • A risk policy. Adopt a rule for a whole class of decisions. "Always take the highest deductible on insurance." "Never buy extended warranties." You will lose sometimes, but over many decisions you come out ahead.
  • "You win a few, you lose a few." A mantra for broad framing. Investors who check their portfolios less often see fewer small losses, feel less pain, and take sensible long-term risks.
  • Companies too. Richard Thaler asked the heads of 25 divisions of a large company about a project with equal chances of losing a large chunk of the capital they managed or earning double that. None would take it. The CEO wanted all 25 to say yes, because across 25 such projects the company was almost sure to come out ahead.
In plain words

Turning down a small bet with good odds feels prudent. Turning down every such bet across a lifetime, from warranties to job opportunities, is expensive. Ask "what if I made this kind of choice a hundred times?" and the right answer often becomes obvious.

Next: Keeping Score →
Chapter 32 · Mental accounts, sunk costs and regret

Keeping Score

In short

We keep mental accounts that make us hold on to losers, honour sunk costs and fear regret, especially regret from acting instead of doing nothing.

The big ideas

  • Mental accounts. We put money into separate mental boxes: savings, holiday money, "winnings". Money is money, but people spend a tax refund freely while saving a salary rise.
  • The disposition effect. Investors are much more likely to sell shares that have gone up than shares that have gone down, because selling a loser closes the account at a loss. Terry Odean found the winners they sold went on to beat the losers they kept by about 3.4% in the following year.
  • Sunk costs. Two fans plan to drive to a game through a blizzard. One paid for his ticket; the other got his free. The one who paid is more likely to drive. The money is gone either way, yet the paid ticket feels like an account that must not be closed at a loss.
  • Regret and the default. People feel more regret about bad outcomes that come from acting than from not acting. Mr Brown almost never picks up hitchhikers; Mr Smith often does. Each gives a ride one day and is robbed. Almost everyone expects Mr Brown to feel more regret: he broke his own routine. Doing what's normal protects us from blame, including our own.
  • Taboo trade-offs. Asked how much they'd pay to remove a small risk, people give modest sums. Asked how much they'd need to accept the same added risk, many refuse at any price. Kahneman suggests this fear of regret can make us waste resources protecting against tiny risks.
In plain words

If you keep going to a gym membership you hate because you paid for the year, you're feeding a sunk cost. The money is gone whatever you do. The only question that matters is whether the next visit is worth your time.

Next: Reversals →
Chapter 33 · Single vs joint evaluation

Reversals

In short

Judge two options one at a time and you may prefer one; see them side by side and you prefer the other. Context changes what we notice.

The big ideas

  • Dictionaries. Christopher Hsee showed people two music dictionaries. A: 10,000 entries, like new. B: 20,000 entries, cover torn. Seen alone, A was valued higher ($24 vs $20): condition is easy to judge, entry counts aren't. Side by side, B won easily ($27 vs $19), because you can now see it has twice as many entries.
  • Categories set the norm. Asked separately how much to give to save dolphins or to screen farmworkers for skin cancer, people gave more to dolphins: they are judged against other animals. Side by side, the farmworkers won: they are human.
  • Poignancy. A man is shot during a robbery in a shop he rarely visits; another is shot in his usual shop. Seen separately, people award more compensation to the first, because it feels more tragic. Seen together, they agree the place shouldn't matter.
  • The legal system. Juries see one case at a time. Punitive damages awarded in isolation are wildly inconsistent, but when cases from different categories are compared, judgments become more coherent.
  • Joint evaluation isn't always better. Comparison can make us focus on what's easy to compare, like a salary figure, rather than what matters, like how the job feels. But usually, broader frames make for more rational judgments.
In plain words

A flat with a big garden can seem wonderful on its own. Viewed next to an equally priced flat with a shorter commute, the garden may lose. What you notice depends on what it's next to, which is why shops place a deliberately overpriced item beside the one they want you to buy.

Next: Frames and Reality →
Chapter 34 · Same facts, different choices

Frames and Reality

In short

Logically equal descriptions of the same choice produce different decisions. We don't choose between things, we choose between descriptions of things.

The big ideas

  • The Asian disease problem. A disease is expected to kill 600 people. Program A will save 200; program B has a one-third chance of saving all 600. Most choose A. Reframe: under C, 400 will die; under D, a one-third chance nobody dies. Most choose D. A and C are identical. So are B and D.
  • Emotional words. A 90% one-month survival rate after surgery sounds better than a 10% mortality rate, to doctors as well as patients. "Cash discount" sounds better than "credit card surcharge", which is why card companies lobbied for the first wording.
  • Miles per gallon. Adam switches from a 12 to a 14 mpg car; Beth from 30 to 40 mpg. Who saves more fuel? It feels like Beth. Over 10,000 miles, Adam saves 119 gallons and Beth 83. Richard Larrick and Jack Soll argued for gallons per mile, and US fuel stickers later added a gallons-per-100-miles figure.
  • Organ donation. Countries where you are a donor unless you tick a box to opt out have far higher donation rates than those where you must tick to opt in: nearly 100% in Austria against 12% in Germany; 86% in Sweden against 4% in Denmark. The frame is just which box is the default.
  • No "true" preference. Kahneman concludes that our preferences are about framed problems. There is often no underlying, frame-free preference to discover. That gives a lot of power to whoever writes the description.
In plain words

"This yoghurt is 95% fat free" and "this yoghurt is 5% fat" are the same yoghurt. Only one will be printed on the lid. When a choice feels obvious, try restating it the other way around; if your answer flips, the frame was choosing for you.

Next: Two Selves →
Part five · chapters 35 to 38

Two Selves

The final part adds two more characters. The experiencing self lives each moment. The remembering self keeps score and makes the decisions. They want different things: memory ignores how long something lasted and judges it by its peak and its ending. Part five asks what that means for how we measure happiness and plan our lives.

Kahneman's big claim

We don't choose between experiences, we choose between memories of experiences. And the remembering self is the one in charge.

Chapter 35 · Peak-end rule and duration neglect

Two Selves

In short

The self that lives each moment and the self that remembers often disagree. Memory judges an episode by its worst moment and its end, and ignores how long it lasted.

The big ideas

  • The colonoscopy study. Patients reported their pain every 60 seconds. Patient A's procedure lasted 8 minutes and ended at a high point; patient B's lasted 24 minutes but ended gently. B suffered far more in total, yet remembered it as less bad.
  • Peak-end rule. Remembered pain was well predicted by the average of the worst moment and the final moment. Duration barely mattered. Kahneman calls this duration neglect.
  • The cold-hand experiment. People held a hand in painfully cold water for 60 seconds. With the other hand, they held it for 90 seconds: the same 60 seconds, plus 30 seconds with the water warming slightly. Asked which to repeat, about 80% of those who felt the pain ease chose the longer one, adding 30 seconds of suffering.
  • The remembering self decides. We choose between memories of experiences, not experiences. So we can choose more pain because the memory of it is better. The experiencing self has no voice in the decision.
  • Which self counts? A doctor who adds a gentler ending to a painful procedure makes it remembered as better, but makes the patient suffer longer. Kahneman doesn't resolve which self should count; he insists both do.
In plain words

A wonderful holiday with a disastrous last day is remembered as a bad holiday. A concert ruined by a screeching sound at the very end feels "ruined", though you enjoyed 40 minutes of it. Memories are stories, and stories are judged by their best or worst moments and how they end.

Chapter 36 · Endings and duration neglect

Life as a Story

In short

We judge whole lives the way we judge stories: by significant moments and the ending. Adding more good years can make a life seem worse if they're only mildly good.

The big ideas

  • La Traviata. The heroine's lover rushes to her side as she dies. Does it matter so much whether he arrives in time? Kahneman notes that we care deeply about the ending, though she has only a few minutes left either way.
  • Jen. Ed Diener's students judged the life of Jen, who was very happy all her life and died suddenly at 30. Another version added five more years that were pleasant but less happy. People rated the longer life as less desirable. Adding good years lowered the average.
  • Duration doesn't count. Doubling the length of Jen's very happy life barely changed how desirable people thought it was. A life, like a colonoscopy, is judged by a typical slice, not a total.
  • The amnesic vacation. Imagine at the end of your holiday every photo and memory will be wiped. Would you still choose the same trip? Many people find that a holiday they'll forget seems far less worth taking, which shows how much of the value lies in the memory.
  • Tourism is memory-making. Photographing a view instead of looking at it is a choice made for the remembering self. The experiencing self might have enjoyed the moment more.
In plain words

Our memories turn our lives into stories, and we judge stories by their high points and how they end, not by how long they ran. That's why a long good marriage that ends in a bitter divorce can feel like "a mistake", even though most of it was happy.

Chapter 37 · Measuring the moments

Experienced Well-Being

In short

Asking people how satisfied they are with life measures the remembering self. To measure the experiencing self, ask how they felt during the actual moments of a day.

The big ideas

  • The Day Reconstruction Method. Kahneman and colleagues asked people to divide the previous day into episodes, like scenes in a film, and rate how they felt in each. This avoids the biases of overall life questions while staying practical.
  • The U-index. The share of time a person spends in an unpleasant state, where the strongest feeling is negative. It's simple, it measures time, not memory, and it varies a lot: some people spend most of their day content; a minority spend much of it in distress.
  • What the day reveals. Commuting and work are among the least pleasant parts of the day; socialising and intimate time are among the most. Being with a boss is worse than being alone. The way you use your time is one of the few things you can change.
  • Money matters, up to a point. Severe poverty makes every misfortune worse. In Kahneman and Angus Deaton's 2010 study of US data, life evaluation kept rising with income, but daily emotional well-being levelled off beyond about $75,000 a year.
  • Attention is the key. Our mood depends mainly on what we are doing and paying attention to at the moment. Giving more time to things we enjoy, and doing them with people we like, is the most direct way to improve the experiencing self's life.
In plain words

Instead of asking "Are you happy with your life?", ask "How much of yesterday did you spend feeling bad?" The first gets an answer shaped by memory and mood. The second gets a number you can actually change, by rearranging your hours.

Worth knowing

Since the book: in 2023 Matthew Killingsworth, Kahneman and Barbara Mellers published an "adversarial collaboration" on the income question. With better data, they found that for most people experienced happiness keeps rising with income well beyond $75,000; the plateau shows up only in the least happy fifth of people.

Chapter 38 · The focusing illusion

Thinking About Life

In short

Whatever you are thinking about seems to matter more than it does. We overrate how much a single change, a new car, a move, a raise, will affect our happiness.

The big ideas

  • The focusing illusion. Kahneman sums it up in one line: "Nothing in life is as important as you think it is when you are thinking about it." Ask people about their car and the car suddenly seems central to their happiness.
  • California. Students in California and the Midwest were asked how satisfied they were with life, and how satisfied students in the other region were. Both groups thought Californians would be happier because of the climate. In fact life satisfaction was about the same. Climate is something you notice when you compare, not when you live.
  • Adaptation. Studies suggest that people who become paraplegic are in a fairly good mood more than half the time within about a month of the injury. Thinking about their situation is still painful, but most of the day they are thinking about other things. The condition is still there; attention has moved on.
  • Miswanting. Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson's term for bad choices that come from errors in predicting how we'll feel. We buy things whose pleasure fades because we stop paying attention to them, and neglect things, like time with friends, that keep demanding attention and keep giving pleasure.
  • Marriage. Life satisfaction rises around the time of marriage and falls back over the next few years. The wedding is new and gets attention; later, married life is just life. The marriage didn't fail; the focusing illusion faded.
In plain words

Before you buy the bigger house or take the job in the sunnier city, ask: how much of an ordinary Tuesday will I spend thinking about this a year from now? For most things the honest answer is "hardly any", and that is roughly how much they will change how you feel.

Conclusions

Three Pairs of Characters

The book ends by returning to its three distinctions: the two selves, Econs and Humans, and the two systems. None of them is a verdict that people are irrational. Humans are not stupid; they are just not the coherent, perfectly logical agents of economic theory, and they need help to make better decisions.

You can't switch System 1 off. What you can do is learn the situations where it is likely to fail, slow down when the stakes are high, and build habits and organisations that catch the errors you can't see in yourself.

Two systemsFast and slowSystem 1 does most of the work; System 2 thinks it's in charge. Recognise the minefield, and ask System 2 for help.
Econs and HumansReal choicesWe feel gains and losses, not wealth. That justifies gentle "nudges" like good defaults, as Thaler and Sunstein argued.
Two selvesLiving and rememberingThe remembering self makes the decisions. Wise choices consider the self that has to live through them.
OrganisationsBetter than individualsChecklists, premortems, outside views and a shared vocabulary of biases help groups catch errors that individuals miss.
