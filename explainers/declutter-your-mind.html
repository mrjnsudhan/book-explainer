Part one · chapters 1 to 2

Why Your Mind Gets Cluttered

Before any fixes, the book explains the problem. Most of what runs through your head is automatic, much of it is about the past or the future, and four everyday forces keep piling more on top.

The idea behind this part

You are not your thoughts. You are the one who can notice them, and anything you can notice you can start to change.

Introduction · Why thoughts pile up

Your Thoughts Are Habits

In short

Most thoughts arrive on their own and repeat out of habit. Left alone they become constant background noise, much of it about the past or the future. The good news is that habits can be changed, and the first step is noticing.

The big ideas

  • Thoughts shape what you see. The stories in your head colour how you read every situation. Two people can walk into the same meeting and leave with completely different days, because of what they were telling themselves.
  • They feel automatic, but they are habits. Thinking patterns get worn in by repetition, like a path across a lawn. That makes them feel like part of you, but it also means a new path can be worn in the same way.
  • The trouble lives in the past and the future. Replaying what went wrong and rehearsing what might go wrong are where most of the clutter comes from. The present moment is usually far more manageable than either.
  • Who the book is for. Anyone who feels stuck, anxious, distracted or worn out by their own mind, and who wants a calmer, simpler head without needing therapy or a retreat.
  • The answer, in a word: mindfulness. Paying attention to what is happening now, on purpose and without judging it. Every habit in the book is a different way of training that one skill.
Worth knowing

This is not from the book, but it backs it up: a 2010 Harvard study that pinged thousands of people at random found their minds were wandering away from what they were doing almost half the time, and that they felt less happy when they were.

Try it today

Set three reminders on your phone for tomorrow. When each one goes off, write a few words on what you were just thinking about, and whether it was about the past, the present or the future.

In plain words

Your head is not a place you are stuck with. It is a set of habits, and most of them drag you out of the moment you are actually in. Learn to notice where your mind has gone, and you have already started to declutter it.

Next: The four causes →
Four causes · What fills your head

The Four Causes of Mental Clutter

In short

Four forces fill the mind: daily stress, too many choices, too much stuff, and a built-in habit of noticing the bad more than the good. Stress is the biggest, and the other three feed it.

The big ideas

  • 1. Daily stress. A constant stream of demands, information and noise keeps the body's alarm system switched on. Over time that shows up as worry, poor sleep, headaches, tight muscles, even chest pain.
  • 2. The paradox of choice. More options feel like freedom, but past a point they make it harder to choose and less satisfying once you have. Every small decision uses up a little of your attention.
  • 3. Too much stuff. Possessions, unread messages and endless feeds all ask to be dealt with. When everything looks urgent, you end up feeling helpless about all of it.
  • 4. The negativity bias. Your brain gives bad news far more weight than good news, a leftover from when missing a threat could kill you. One criticism can outweigh ten compliments and keep you up at night.
  • What helps. The rest of the book attacks each cause: calming habits for stress, clear values for choices, decluttering for stuff, and mindfulness for the negativity bias.
Worth knowing

The book draws on psychologist Barry Schwartz's idea of the paradox of choice. Its most famous example, a jam stall where shoppers offered 24 flavours bought far less often than those offered 6, has not held up well in later studies: the effect seems real in some settings and absent in others.

Try it today

Write down the four causes and give each a score out of ten for how much it is affecting you this week. Keep the list; you will use it to decide which part of this guide to read first.

In plain words

Your mind is not cluttered because something is wrong with you. It is cluttered because modern life pours stress, choices, stuff and bad news into it faster than you can clear them out. Once you can name which of the four is loudest, you know where to start.

Declutter your thoughts · chapters 3 to 6

Declutter Your Thoughts

Four habits for the inside of your head: breathe on purpose, sit with your mind every day, interrupt negative thoughts before they snowball, and teach your mind more useful ways to think.

The idea behind this part

Calm is a skill, not a mood. It gets stronger with short, daily practice, the same way a muscle does.

Habit 1 · The fastest way to calm down

Focused Deep Breathing

In short

Most of us breathe shallowly into the chest, especially when stressed. Slow, deep breaths into the belly switch on the body's calming system, what doctors call the relaxation response. A few minutes a day is enough to feel it.

The big ideas

  • Shallow breathing feeds stress. Quick breaths high in the chest are part of the body's alarm response. When you breathe that way out of habit, your body stays slightly on alert all day.
  • Deep breathing flips the switch. Breathing slowly and fully, so that the belly rises before the chest, signals that you are safe. Heart rate slows, muscles soften and the mind quietens.
  • Make the exhale longer. Breathing out takes you towards calm, so letting the out-breath run a little longer than the in-breath helps most.
  • It is a habit, not a one-off. The book suggests a short daily session, five to ten minutes, ideally right before you meditate. Attach it to something you already do, like your morning coffee.
  • Use it in the moment too. Once the habit is built, a handful of deep breaths becomes a tool you can reach for before a hard conversation or when worry starts to climb.
Try it today

Lie down and put one hand on your chest and one on your belly. Breathe so that only the lower hand moves. Do ten slow breaths like that, then notice how your body feels.

In plain words

Breathing is the one part of your stress response you can take over on purpose. Breathe slowly into your belly, make the out-breath longer, and your body follows. Do it for a few minutes every day so it is there when you need it.

Next: Meditation →
Habit 2 · Sit with your mind

Meditation

In short

Meditation is sitting quietly, resting your attention on your breath, and gently coming back each time your mind wanders. The wandering is not failure. Each return is one repetition, and the repetitions are what train focus and calm.

The big ideas

  • What it is. Not emptying your mind, which nobody can do. It is watching thoughts come and go without chasing them, and returning to the breath when you notice you have drifted.
  • What it does. Regular practice is linked to lower stress, better focus and a calmer response to difficult emotions. Most of all, it makes the gap between a thought and your reaction to it a little wider.
  • Start small and fixed. Same time and same place each day, a quiet spot, phone away, a ten-minute timer. Sit on a cushion or a chair with a straight back and your hands resting in your lap.
  • A simple technique. Close your eyes and breathe through your nose. Silently say “in” as you breathe in and “out” as you breathe out. When you notice you are thinking, let the thought go and say “in” again.
  • Notice, don't judge. Sounds, itches and feelings will come up. Notice them, name them if you like, and come back. Guided apps can help when you are starting out.
Try it today

Tomorrow, after your breathing practice, sit for five minutes using the in and out method. Keep a tally of how many times you noticed your mind had wandered. A high number is a good sign: it means you are noticing.

In plain words

Meditation is not about a blank mind. It is about catching yourself drifting and coming back, over and over. Ten minutes a day of that is like a gym session for your attention.

Habit 3 · Catch the spiral early

Reframe Your Negative Thoughts

In short

Negative thoughts snowball: one leads to the next until you are sure everything is going wrong. The trick is to catch them early. The book gives six ways to interrupt a negative thought before it builds.

The big ideas

  • Be a watcher. Step back and observe your mind as if it were someone else's. The moment you are watching a thought, you are no longer completely inside it.
  • Name that thought. Label it: “That's a worry” or “That's a judgement.” A thought is an event in your head, not a fact about the world.
  • Just say no. Say “stop” out loud or in your head, or picture yourself throwing the thought away. It sounds too simple, but a sharp interruption breaks the momentum.
  • The rubber band trick. Wear a band on your wrist. When you catch a negative loop, move it to the other wrist, or give it a light snap. Over time you become far more aware of how often the loop runs.
  • Know your triggers. Certain people, places, times of day and states, like being tired or hungry, set off the same spirals. Spot them and you can prepare, or avoid them.
  • Distract yourself. Give your mind something absorbing to do: a task, a walk, a conversation. A busy mind has less room for a spiral.
Try it today

Pick the one negative thought you have most often. Write down when it usually shows up and what tends to set it off. Then choose one of the six interrupts to try next time it appears.

In plain words

You cannot stop negative thoughts from showing up, but you can stop them gathering speed. Notice them early, see them as thoughts rather than facts, and use whichever interrupt works for you.

Next: Teach your mind new tricks →
Habit 4 · Replace, accept, act, or schedule

Teach Your Old Mind New Tricks

In short

Interrupting a negative thought makes space. This habit fills that space with something better. Challenge the thought and replace it, accept what you cannot change, take action on what you can, and give worry a fixed slot in your day.

The big ideas

  • Challenge it. Ask: is this really true? What is the evidence? What would I say to a friend who thought this? Then write a version that is fairer and more accurate.
  • Practise acceptance. Some things you simply cannot change. Fighting them just adds a second layer of suffering on top of the first. Accepting them is not giving up; it is choosing where to spend your energy.
  • Take mindful action. When a worry is about something you can affect, the best cure is a step forward: a decision, a phone call, a plan. Getting clear on your goals and values, the subject of the next part, helps here.
  • Use a worry timer. Set aside ten to fifteen minutes a day as worry time. When a worry comes up at other times, note it and park it for later. Often by the time your slot arrives, it no longer matters much.
Try it today

Pick a time for a fifteen-minute worry slot tomorrow, not right before bed. Keep a notepad handy all day and park every worry on it. When the slot comes, go through the list and see how many still need your attention.

In plain words

Your mind will keep producing worries, so give it something to do with them. Rewrite the unfair ones, let go of the ones you cannot change, act on the ones you can, and save the rest for a short slot in your day.

Declutter your obligations · chapters 7 to 10

Declutter Your Life Obligations

Much of the noise in your head comes from not being sure what matters. This part helps you pick your values, rank the areas of your life, set a few goals for the next three months, and tie them to what you care about.

The idea behind this part

When you know what matters most, a lot of decisions make themselves, and the worry that came with them goes away.

Strategy 1 · Know what you stand for

Identify Your Core Values

In short

Values are the few things that matter most to you, like honesty, family or adventure. When you know them, decisions get easier and guilt about the rest fades. Pick your top five or six, then check how closely your life matches them today.

The big ideas

  • Start with a long list. Read through a list of value words and pick every one that resonates. The book offers hundreds of words to choose from. Do it once for your life and once for your work.
  • Narrow it down. From each list, keep the five or six that feel most essential. If you had to drop all but these, which would you fight to keep?
  • Check the gaps. For each value, write down how well your daily life matches it right now. Most people find at least one value they say matters but give almost no time.
  • Plan small corrections. For each gap, write one small, specific action that would bring you closer. Small actions you actually take beat big plans you do not.
  • Why it clears the mind. Without clear values, every choice is a fresh debate. With them, many options are ruled out at once, and you stop second-guessing.
Try it today

Write your top five values on a card and keep it where you will see it. Next time you are torn about saying yes to something, check the card.

In plain words

Decide what you stand for, and write it down. Then look honestly at where your time actually goes. The gap between the two is where most of the quiet guilt in your head comes from, and where a few small changes help the most.

Next: Clarify your life priorities →
Strategy 2 · Where your time, energy and money go

Clarify Your Life Priorities

In short

Your life has several areas competing for your time: career, family, partner, health and more. Rate how each is going, then start with the one that would improve everything else the most.

The big ideas

  • Seven areas. The book splits life into career, family, marriage or partnership, spirituality and self-improvement, leisure and social life, life management (money, home, admin), and health and fitness.
  • Priorities are a choice. You cannot give every area full attention at once. Deciding which ones come first this season is what stops you feeling guilty about all of them.
  • Start with the biggest lever. Some areas lift the others. Better sleep and exercise can improve your work and your patience at home. Pick the area where a change would ripple furthest.
  • Let your values guide you. Priorities flow from the values you picked in the last chapter. If family is a core value but gets your last hour of energy, that is a mismatch to fix.
Try it today

Write the seven areas down and give each a score out of ten. Circle the one where a small improvement would help the most other areas, and pick one thing to do about it this week.

In plain words

You cannot do everything at once, and trying to is a big source of mental clutter. Look at the main areas of your life, pick the one that would make the biggest difference, and give it attention first.

Next: Quarterly SMART goals →
Strategy 3 · Plan three months at a time

Mindful Goal Setting and Quarterly SMART Goals

In short

Set a few goals for the next three months, and make each one specific, measurable, attainable, relevant and time-bound. Three months is long enough to make real progress and short enough to stay focused, while still leaving room to enjoy life now.

The big ideas

  • Mindful goals. Goals should improve your life, not take it over. Keep enough room to enjoy the present while you work towards the future.
  • SMART, letter by letter. Specific (who, what, where, when, which, why), Measurable (how much or how many), Attainable (you believe you can do it), Relevant (it matters to you), and Time-bound (it has a deadline). The acronym comes from management writer George Doran in 1981.
  • Think in quarters. Pick three or four areas from your priorities and set a goal for each that you can finish in about thirteen weeks.
  • Review every week. Once a week, check your commitments, your goal projects and how much time you really have, then schedule the next steps.
  • Keep them in view. Read your goals two or three times a day, and at the end of the quarter look again at the why behind each one before setting the next round.
Try it today

Take your fuzziest goal, like “get fit”, and rewrite it so that someone else could check whether you did it by a date three months from now.

In plain words

Vague goals clutter your mind because you never know if you are on track. A few clear, three-month goals that you check every week turn a cloud of “shoulds” into a short list of next steps.

Next: Connect goals to your passions →
Strategy 4 · Goals that pull you forward

Connect Your Goals to Your Passions

In short

Goals you are excited about are far easier to stick to than goals you merely think you should do. Link your goals to what you love, and if you do not yet know what that is, the book lays out a step-by-step way to find it.

The big ideas

  • Why passion matters. Doing something you care about brings energy, confidence and meaning. Work stops feeling like a drain, and the rest of life tends to improve with it.
  • Start with a vision. Write a clear picture of the life you want, then compare it with the life you have. The differences point to what you care about.
  • Explore before you leap. Learn about your personality, read widely about your interests, narrow the options, find a mentor and brainstorm on paper.
  • Test it small. Take one small first step and try the option out before you commit, for example by volunteering or doing a side project. Ask people you trust what they see.
  • Make it safe to change. Save some money, plan how you will earn while you switch, and keep doing your current job well. And when in doubt, do something: action builds motivation, not the other way round.
Try it today

List three things you have lost track of time doing in the past year. For each, write one small way you could do more of it in the next month.

In plain words

Willpower runs out; excitement does not. Tie your goals to things you genuinely care about, and if you are not sure what those are, explore in small, safe steps until you find out.

Declutter your relationships · chapters 11 to 15

Declutter Your Relationships

The people around you can calm your mind or fill it with noise. This part covers how to show up more fully for the people you care about, stop replaying old hurts, bring mindfulness into a partnership, and step back from relationships that only drain you.

The idea behind this part

Start with what you can change, which is how you show up. Other people may follow; if they never do, you will at least know where you stand.

Relationships · The people in your head

How Relationships Clutter Your Mind

In short

Draining relationships take up huge space in your head long after the conversation ends. Good ones calm you. What counts is quality, not the number of people, and the place to start changing things is yourself.

The big ideas

  • Bad relationships echo. An argument or a cutting remark can replay for days. Tension with someone close is one of the biggest sources of mental noise there is.
  • Quality over quantity. A few close, healthy relationships do far more for your wellbeing than a wide circle of shallow ones.
  • What healthy looks like. Honest communication, handling conflict fairly, mutual respect, some shared interests, closeness, whether emotional or intellectual, and accepting each other as you are.
  • Start with yourself. You cannot make someone else change. You can change how you listen, speak and react, and that often shifts the whole relationship.
Try it today

Think of the relationship that takes up the most space in your head. Write down which of the six signs of a healthy relationship are strong in it and which are weak.

In plain words

People take up room in your mind, for better or worse. Notice which relationships calm you and which drain you, judge them by their quality, and start any change with your own behaviour.

Next: Be more present →
Strategy 1 · Give people your full attention

Be More Present

In short

Being fully present is the best gift you can give someone. Listen to understand rather than to reply, speak with care, put the phone away, stop comparing, and say thank you often.

The big ideas

  • Empathic listening. Listen to understand how the other person feels, not to fix it or tell your own story. Reflect back what you heard before you add anything.
  • Mindful speaking. Pause before you speak. Ask yourself whether it is true, kind and needed, especially when you are upset.
  • Loving-kindness. A short meditation where you silently wish well to yourself, then to people you love, then to people you find hard. It softens how you see them.
  • Stop comparing. Measuring yourself or your partner against others keeps your mind somewhere else. Accept yourself as you are, change what you can, and let the rest be.
  • Practise gratitude. Tell people what you appreciate about them, often and specifically. It strengthens the bond and trains your mind to notice the good.
Try it today

In your next real conversation, put your phone face down out of reach and do not offer advice unless you are asked. Afterwards, notice how the other person responded.

In plain words

Presence is mostly about what you stop doing: checking your phone, planning your reply, fixing, comparing. When you stop, people feel it, and your own mind gets quieter too.

Strategy 2 · Stop replaying old hurts

Get Unstuck from the Past

In short

Replaying an old hurt keeps it alive and keeps you tied to it. There are three ways out: resolve it if you can, challenge the story you tell about it, or forgive and let it go.

The big ideas

  • The replay loop. Each time you go over what happened, the memory gets stronger and the feelings come back as if it were new. The past is not happening now, but your mind makes it feel that way.
  • Resolve it. If something can still be done, such as an apology, a conversation or a repair, do it. Unfinished business loops the loudest.
  • Challenge your story. Separate what happened from what you decided it meant. “She didn't call” is a fact. “She doesn't care about me” is a story, and maybe not a true one.
  • Forgive. Forgiveness is not saying it was fine. It is deciding to stop carrying it, mostly for your own sake.
Try it today

Write down an old hurt in two columns: on the left, only the facts a camera would have recorded; on the right, what you concluded from them. Look at the right-hand column and ask whether each part is certain.

In plain words

You cannot change what happened, but you can change what you keep doing with it. Fix what can be fixed, question the meaning you gave it, and let the rest go.

Next: Mindfulness with your partner →
Strategy 3 · Bring calm into a partnership

Mindfulness with Your Partner

In short

Close relationships trigger our strongest reactions. The book lists eight practices for bringing mindfulness into a partnership, from committing to it daily to listening without getting defensive and finding the lesson in each argument.

The big ideas

  • Commit, and say so. Decide to practise mindfulness every day, and tell your partner, so they understand what you are trying to do.
  • Be there emotionally. Notice what you are feeling, and stay with your partner's feelings instead of drifting off or shutting down.
  • Listen without defending. Hear the whole point before you respond, and reflect back what you heard so they know you got it.
  • Speak honestly. Say what you actually feel and need, calmly, rather than hinting or attacking.
  • Learn from conflict. Every argument shows you something about what each of you needs. Look for that lesson instead of a winner.
  • Protect time together. Spend regular time with no screens and no distractions.
Try it today

Next time a disagreement starts, try just one practice: before you reply, repeat back what you heard your partner say and ask if you got it right.

In plain words

Your partner is the person most likely to push your buttons, which makes the relationship the best place to practise. Listen fully, speak honestly, and treat each argument as information about what you both need.

Next: Let go of certain people →
Strategy 4 · When a relationship only drains you

Let Go of Certain People

In short

Some relationships cause far more pain than they give. When you have tried to improve one and it still drains you, it may be time to let it go. The book gives six steps for doing that with care.

The big ideas

  • Picture life without them. Think honestly about what would be better, and what you would lose.
  • Weigh the consequences. Ending a relationship can affect family, friends, work or money. Think it through before you act.
  • Say what you intend. Where it is safe, tell the person clearly and calmly. Slowly vanishing usually prolongs the pain on both sides.
  • Expect a reaction. They may be angry or try to pull you back. Decide in advance how you will respond.
  • Give it time, and grieve. Even a bad relationship leaves a gap. It is normal to feel sad, and it takes time to settle.
Try it today

List the people you spend the most time with. Next to each, write how you usually feel after seeing them: lighter, the same, or heavier.

In plain words

You are allowed to step back from people who consistently hurt you. Try to improve things first, then, if it is still draining, let go with care: be clear, expect a reaction, and give yourself time to grieve.

Declutter your surroundings · chapters 16 to 20

Declutter Your Surroundings

Your space and your schedule talk to your mind all day. A cluttered room, a buzzing phone and a calendar with no gaps all keep it busy. This part clears each of them, then shows how to bring full attention to ordinary things.

The idea behind this part

Every object, alert and commitment asks for a little of your attention. Fewer of them means more attention left for what matters.

Surroundings · Clear the space, clear the head

Simplify Your Home

In short

A cluttered home keeps tugging at your attention. Declutter it in short, timed sessions, starting with the room you spend the most time in, and decide quickly about each item.

The big ideas

  • Prepare first. Set up a staging area and some boxes for keep, donate, relocate and throw away. Have a timer, a notebook and a pen.
  • Work in short sessions. Ten minutes at a time, on a schedule, keeps it from becoming overwhelming. Start where you spend the most time, so you feel the benefit soonest.
  • Use a system. Go top to bottom or left to right, so you never wonder where you were.
  • Decide fast. Indecision is the enemy. If you have not used something in a year and would not buy it again, let it go.
  • Bring people along. Tell your household what you are doing, and try to enjoy it. A clear room is a reward you get every time you walk in.
Try it today

Set a timer for ten minutes and clear one surface, like a desk, a kitchen counter or a bedside table. Stop when the timer goes off.

In plain words

Your mind keeps track of everything around you. Fewer things means less to track. Clear your space in short, timed bursts and make quick decisions, and the calm spreads from the room to your head.

Next: Simplify your digital life →
Surroundings · Tame the screens

Simplify Your Digital Life

In short

Phones and feeds are built to grab attention, and every ping breaks your focus. Cut the alerts, check messages at set times, and swap some screen time for things that actually refresh you.

The big ideas

  • Notice the habit. Much screen use is automatic: you pick up the phone without deciding to. Noticing that moment is the first step.
  • Cut the noise. Turn off notifications you do not need, unsubscribe from lists you never read, and remove apps that mostly waste your time.
  • Batch it. Check email and messages at a few set times a day instead of all the time.
  • Replace, don't just remove. Fill the time you win back with reading, exercise, family, a hobby, learning a skill, meditating or music.
  • Make some spaces screen-free. Keep the phone out of the bedroom and off the dinner table.
Try it today

Go through your phone's notification settings tonight and switch off every alert that is not from a real person who needs you.

In plain words

Your phone is not neutral; it is built to pull you back in. Take control of it: silence what does not matter, check messages on your schedule, and use the time you win back for something that fills you up.

Next: Simplify your activities →
Surroundings · Leave room in the week

Simplify Your Activities

In short

A packed calendar leaves no room to think. Cut commitments that do not match your priorities, protect some free time, and aim for activities that absorb you completely, a state psychologists call flow.

The big ideas

  • Purge commitments. Look at everything you have signed up for and drop what does not fit your values or priorities. This includes children's schedules too.
  • Three a day. Pick three important things to get done each day. Anything else is a bonus.
  • Sacred time. Block out regular time that belongs to nobody but you, and protect it like a meeting. Leave work on time, and take regular breaks from screens.
  • Find flow. Flow happens when a task is challenging enough to stretch your skills but not so hard it overwhelms you, with a clear goal and no distractions. It takes at least fifteen minutes to get into, and leaves you refreshed rather than drained.
Try it today

Look at your week ahead and find one commitment you could cancel, delegate or decline. Put a two-hour block of free time in its place.

In plain words

Busyness feels productive but clutters your head. Keep fewer commitments, give each day three clear priorities, protect some empty time, and spend more of it on things that absorb you completely.

Next: Simplify your distractions →
Surroundings · Beat procrastination

Simplify Your Distractions

In short

Unfinished tasks hang around in your head. The book's twelve-step routine for procrastination boils down to: plan ahead, break the task into small pieces, remove distractions, work against a timer, take breaks, and reward yourself.

The big ideas

  • Plan and know why. Decide the night before what you will work on, and remind yourself why it matters.
  • Shrink the task. Break it into small sub-tasks, so the first step is easy enough to start right away. Prepare everything you need before you begin.
  • Clear the decks. Close tabs, silence the phone, shut the door. Start with a minute of mindful breathing to settle in.
  • Use a timer. Work in focused blocks with short breaks in between, and longer breaks after a few blocks. Reward yourself when you finish.
  • Save the mindless stuff. Schedule easy tasks like email and filing for the times when your energy is low.
Try it today

Pick the task you have been putting off longest. Write down its very first step, the one that would take five minutes or less, and do only that.

In plain words

Procrastination is often a task that feels too big or too vague. Make it smaller, make the space quieter, put it on a timer, and reward yourself when it is done.

Next: Simplify your actions →
Surroundings · Full attention on ordinary things

Simplify Your Actions

In short

You do not need a cushion to be mindful. Do ordinary things, like eating, cleaning, walking, being outdoors and exercising, one at a time and with full attention, and each becomes a short break for your mind.

The big ideas

  • One thing at a time. Multitasking splits your attention and leaves everything half done. Doing one thing fully is calmer and usually faster.
  • Eat mindfully. No screens. Notice colours, smells, textures and tastes, and put the fork down between bites.
  • Clean and walk mindfully. Feel the warm water on your hands, or each foot touching the ground. Chores and commutes become practice.
  • Notice nature. Time outdoors, really looking and listening, is one of the quickest ways to quieten a busy mind.
  • Move mindfully. In exercise, pay attention to your breath and how your body feels instead of tuning out with a screen.
Try it today

In plain words

Mindfulness is not something extra you have to find time for. It is a way of doing the things you already do. Pick one daily activity and give it your full attention; that is where a calm mind is built.
