What if you could go after a goal as though success were only a matter of time? Success Is Inevitable is Thibaut Meurisse's practical blueprint for making it so: take full responsibility, build real self-belief with his self-empowerment triangle, get clear and committed, then follow seventeen laws from humility and mastery to perseverance, patience and long-term thinking, with a bullet-proof timeframe to keep you going and a toolkit for the emotional storms on the way. This guide covers all five parts and the bonus chapter in 33 sections, each with an illustration and a lab where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

Do the right things, keep doing them, and refuse to quit: then success stops being a gamble and becomes a matter of time.

Part I · mastering the four powers

The Four Powers That Enable Success

Meurisse opens with the foundations. Before any law can work, he says, you need four things in place: you accept full responsibility for your life, you believe you can change it, you know clearly what you want, and you care enough about it to keep going. These five sections start with the book's question of what success even means, then take the four powers one at a time.

The idea behind this part

Success becomes far more likely once you own your results, believe you can improve, know exactly what you want and have strong reasons to want it.

Chapter 1 · What success means to you

Success Is a Mindset

In short

The book starts with a question most people skip: what would success actually look like for you? Meurisse argues that success is personal. It's reaching goals you chose for yourself, and if you borrow the definition from your parents, your feed or your peers, you can win and still feel empty.

The big ideas

  • Write your own definition. Before you set any goal, put into words what success means to you. A vague or borrowed definition makes every goal feel arbitrary.
  • Success is a mindset before it is a result. The title's claim is that when you expect success, and act like someone who expects it, you behave in the ways that make it likely.
  • Expect it, then earn it. Expecting success isn't wishful thinking. It's a stance that keeps you trying, learning and adjusting until the result arrives.
  • Results follow conditions. Take responsibility, get clear, commit, keep learning and never give up: when those conditions are in place, the outcome is less a gamble and more a matter of time.
The idea in action

Picture two friends who both land the same well-paid job at thirty. One had always wanted to build things and chose the job for the craft; she goes home tired and pleased. The other took it because it was what his family respected; he hits every target and feels nothing. Same salary, same title, opposite verdicts. Meurisse's point is that the scoreboard you keep must be one you designed. When the definition is yours, effort feels like progress. When it's borrowed, even winning feels like someone else's victory.

Try it this week

Write one paragraph that starts "Success, for me, is..." without mentioning anyone else's opinion. Read it again in a week and edit anything that sounds borrowed.

In plain words

Success means reaching goals that matter to you, not to everyone else. Decide what it means first, then chase it as if you expect to get there.

Chapter 2 · Owning every part of your life

The Power of Absolute Responsibility

In short

The first power is taking full responsibility for where you are and where you go next. Not blame, which looks backwards, but ownership, which looks forwards. Meurisse argues that the moment you stop pointing at the economy, your boss or your past, you get back the only lever that ever worked: your own response.

The big ideas

  • Responsibility isn't fault. Plenty of what happens to you isn't your fault. Responsibility means owning what you do next, whatever caused the situation.
  • Blame gives your power away. Every time you explain a result by something outside you, you also say you can't change it. Ownership keeps the steering wheel in your hands.
  • Accept what is, then act. Fighting reality wastes energy. Accept the situation as it is today, then ask what you can do with it.
  • Own your thoughts and feelings too. You can't stop every thought, but you are responsible for which ones you feed and which ones you act on.
From the research

In 1966 psychologist Julian Rotter described what he called locus of control: whether people see their outcomes as mostly driven by their own actions (an internal locus) or by luck, fate and powerful others (an external one). Over the following decades, a large body of research linked a more internal locus with things like better study habits, more job-hunting effort, healthier behaviour and higher earnings. The direction runs both ways, since success also makes people feel more in control, but the pattern fits Meurisse's argument: people who believe their actions matter tend to take more of them.

Worth knowing

Locus of control is a tendency, not a fixed trait, and a sense of control can tip into self-blame for things that really were outside your hands. The book's version is about owning your response, not blaming yourself for events.

Try it this week

Pick one result you're unhappy with. Write the sentence "I am responsible for what I do about this, and the next step is..." and finish it.

In plain words

You can't control everything that happens, but you always control what you do next. Stop blaming and you get your power back.

Chapter 3 · The self-empowerment triangle

The Power of Belief

In short

You won't work hard for something you don't believe you can get. Meurisse's tool for building that belief is the self-empowerment triangle: self-discipline (keeping promises to yourself) builds self-esteem (respecting yourself for it), which builds self-confidence (trusting yourself to act), which makes the next promise easier.

The big ideas

  • Belief drives effort. What you think is possible decides how hard you try and how soon you quit, so belief comes before results, not after.
  • Self-discipline: keep small promises. Every promise you keep to yourself, even a tiny daily habit, is a piece of evidence that you can be trusted.
  • Self-esteem: acknowledge yourself. Notice and record what you get done, including small wins. Esteem grows from evidence you actually look at.
  • Self-confidence: act anyway. Confidence grows by acting before you feel ready and seeing that you survive. Each round of the triangle makes the next easier.
From the research

In 1977 psychologist Albert Bandura introduced self-efficacy: your belief that you can carry out the actions a goal needs. He found that it predicts how much effort people put in, how long they persist and how they handle setbacks. Of the sources of self-efficacy he identified, the strongest was mastery experience, actually succeeding at something step by step, more than encouragement from others or watching someone else do it. That's the logic of the triangle: you don't talk yourself into confidence, you collect proof of it, one kept promise at a time.

Try it this week

Choose one daily promise so small you can't fail (two minutes of it counts), keep it for thirty days, and tick it off where you can see the row grow.

In plain words

Belief comes from proof. Keep small promises to yourself, notice that you kept them, and your confidence will grow enough to take on bigger ones.

Next: Clarity →
Chapter 4 · Knowing exactly what you want

The Power of Clarity

In short

Most people don't get what they want because they never decided exactly what it is. Meurisse asks you to rate seven areas of your life, imagine what a ten would look like in each, write a vision, know yourself well, and then turn one major goal into a specific plan broken down by month, week and day.

The big ideas

  • Rate your life. Score career, family, finance, health, personal growth, relationship and spirituality from 1 to 10. The low scores show where clarity is most needed.
  • Picture a ten. For each area, describe what a ten would look like. Vague dissatisfaction becomes a concrete picture you can move toward.
  • Know yourself. Your strengths, values and what energises you shape which goals are right. Clarity about yourself comes before clarity about goals.
  • Make one goal SMART, then break it down. A specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound goal, split into monthly, weekly and daily targets, tells you what to do today.
From the research

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham spent decades studying goals at work, in hundreds of studies across many industries. Their most consistent finding, summed up in a 2002 review, was that specific, challenging goals led to better performance than easy goals or the instruction to "do your best". A specific goal focuses attention, sets the pace of effort and tells you when you've fallen behind. "Do your best" sounds encouraging but gives you nothing to aim at. Clarity, in other words, isn't just a nice feeling; it changes what you do.

Try it this week

Rate the seven areas tonight. For the lowest one, write two sentences describing a ten, then one SMART goal for the next ninety days.

In plain words

You can't hit a target you haven't set. Score your life, picture what great looks like, and turn one area into a clear goal with a weekly and daily step.

Chapter 5 · Finding and strengthening your whys

The Power of Passion

In short

Clarity tells you where to go; passion gets you through the boring and painful middle. Meurisse suggests finding what genuinely excites you with a few reflective questions, then building a long list of reasons why you must reach your goal. The more and stronger your whys, the more hard days they can carry.

The big ideas

  • Look for clues to your passion. What did you love as a child? Whom do you envy? What would you do with total freedom or unlimited money? The answers point at what moves you.
  • Whys are fuel. When motivation dips, reasons are what make you keep going. Write many of them, not one.
  • Four kinds of reasons. The book tags reasons as pain (what you want to escape), pleasure (what you want to enjoy), ego (how you want to be seen) and love (who you do it for). Mixing kinds makes the fire harder to put out.
  • Passion is built, not only found. Interest grows as you get better at something. Effort and skill feed passion as much as passion feeds effort.
The idea in action

Two people start training for a marathon in January. The first has one reason: a friend dared him. By March, on a cold morning with sore legs, the dare isn't enough and he stays in bed. The second wrote down eleven reasons on the first day: she wants to feel strong again (pleasure), she's scared of the health problems that ran in her family (pain), she wants to see her name on the results list (ego), and she's raising money for her nephew's school (love). On the same cold morning one of those reasons is always awake. She runs. The race was equally hard for both; only the fuel was different.

Try it this week

Set a timer for ten minutes and write every reason you have for reaching your main goal. Tag each one pain, pleasure, ego or love, and add more to the kinds you're missing.

In plain words

Motivation fades on hard days; reasons don't. Find what excites you and write down lots of reasons to keep going, of several kinds.

Part II · activating commitment

The Power of Commitment

The second part is short and sharp. Most people are interested in their goals; far fewer are committed to them. The difference is whether you have already decided to keep going when it gets hard, to learn whatever you need to learn, and to take feedback without flinching.

The idea behind this part

Interest asks whether it's convenient. Commitment has already decided to do what it takes.

Part II · Interested or committed?

The Power of Commitment

In short

Most people are interested in their goals; they'll work on them when it's convenient. Committed people have decided to do whatever it takes. Meurisse describes commitment as four willingnesses: to learn what you need to learn, to persist until you get the result, to raise your standards, and to welcome feedback even when it stings.

The big ideas

  • Interest versus commitment. Interest says "I'd like to." Commitment says "I will, and I've already decided what I'll do when it gets hard."
  • Willing to learn. If you don't yet know how, commitment means going to find out: books, courses, mentors, practice.
  • Willing to persist and raise standards. You keep going until you get results, and you stop accepting from yourself what you used to accept.
  • Willing to hear feedback. Criticism is information about how to get there faster. The committed person asks for it.
An old story

A story told for centuries says that when the Spanish conquistador Hernán Cortés landed in Mexico in 1519, he had his ships destroyed so his men couldn't sail home. With no way back, the only direction left was forward. Self-help books love it as a picture of commitment: remove the easy exit and you stop wasting energy wondering whether to quit. Historians note that the ships were scuttled rather than burned and that the expedition's goals were brutal conquest, so it's best taken as a metaphor about removing escape routes, not as a model to admire.

Worth knowing

A backup plan isn't wrong in itself, and reviewers of the book point this out too. The book's warning is against using one as a reason not to give the main plan everything you've got.

Try it this week

Write down the one thing you'd do this week if you were fully committed to your main goal rather than just interested in it. Do it before Friday.

In plain words

Wanting something isn't the same as committing to it. Commitment means you've already decided to learn, persist and take feedback until you get there.

Part III · the laws of success

The Seventeen Laws

This is the heart of the book. Meurisse lays out seventeen laws that, followed together, make reaching a goal close to inevitable. They run from the inner game (humility, identity, conditioning, focus of attention) through how you work (mastery, strategic action, deliberate practice, consistency) to how you hold on (perseverance, courage, flexibility, patience and long-term thinking). Each section here takes one law.

The idea behind this part

Success is less a lucky event than the predictable result of the right actions, repeated long enough, by someone who refuses to quit.

Law 1 · Stay teachable

The Law of Humility

In short

The first law is humility: the willingness to admit you don't know, to learn from anyone, and to drop an idea that isn't working. Meurisse warns that pride, the need to be right or to look clever, quietly blocks more progress than lack of talent.

The big ideas

  • Pride is expensive. Defending a wrong idea because it's yours costs you months. Admitting a mistake early costs you a moment of discomfort.
  • Anyone can teach you something. People further along, people starting out, even critics: each has a piece of information you lack.
  • Humility makes feedback usable. If you can't hear that you're wrong, feedback bounces off. A humble mind turns it into improvement.
  • Humble isn't small. Humility isn't thinking less of yourself. It's thinking about yourself less, and about the work more.
An old story

A well-known teaching story tells of a learned professor who visited a Zen master to ask about Zen. The master served tea and kept pouring after the cup was full, until it spilled over the table. When the professor protested, the master said that the professor was like the cup: so full of his own opinions that nothing new could go in. To learn, he'd first have to empty it. Meurisse's law of humility asks the same of anyone with a goal: leave room in the cup.

Try it this week

In your next conversation about your goal, ask one person "What would you do differently in my position?" and only listen. Write down one thing you hadn't considered.

In plain words

If you think you already know it all, you can't learn anything. Stay curious, admit mistakes quickly, and let anyone teach you.

Next: Mastery →
Law 2 · Go deep before you go wide

The Law of Mastery

In short

Results come from mastery, not from knowing a little about many things. Meurisse's recipe has three steps: focus on one thing at a time, practise what you already know intellectually instead of collecting more theory, and repeat until you reach a high level.

The big ideas

  • One thing at a time. Split your effort across five projects and none gets deep enough to pay off. Choose the most exciting one and give it most of your attention.
  • Knowing isn't doing. Most people already know enough to improve their lives a lot. The gap is in applying it, so practise what you know before you read the next book.
  • Repeat until it's natural. Mastery comes from repetitions, refined each time, until the skill no longer takes conscious effort.
  • Find the one skill. Ask which single skill, mastered, would most change your life. That's where your hours go first.
The idea in action

A line usually credited to Bruce Lee goes: he didn't fear the man who had practised ten thousand kicks once, but the man who had practised one kick ten thousand times. Picture two people learning to draw. One tries watercolour, then charcoal, then digital art, then calligraphy, a month each. After a year she can do a little of everything and nothing well. The other spends the year on pencil portraits, drawing a face every evening and comparing it to the photo. By December he can draw a recognisable likeness in twenty minutes, and people start asking him for portraits. Same hours, very different results.

Try it this week

List everything you're currently trying to get better at. Circle one, and for the next month spend at least two thirds of your practice time on it.

In plain words

Do one thing until you're good at it. Practise what you already know instead of always learning more, and repeat it until it's second nature.

Law 3 · Concentrate your energy

The Law of Focus

In short

Focus is mastery applied to your whole life. Meurisse asks you to pick the one area (health, finance, relationships, career or personal growth) where improvement would make the biggest difference, and put your best energy there for a season, instead of trying to transform everything at once.

The big ideas

  • Energy is limited. You have only so much willpower and attention each day. Spread thin, it barely moves anything.
  • Concentrated energy breaks through. Like sunlight through a lens, the same effort focused on one point reaches the heat it takes to change things.
  • Choose the area with leverage. Some improvements make others easier: better health gives you energy for work; better finances lower stress at home. Start there.
  • Switching has a cost. Every time you jump between goals you pay to restart: remembering where you were, rebuilding momentum.
From the research

In 2001 Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer and Jeffrey Evans had people switch between tasks such as solving maths problems and classifying shapes. Every switch cost time, and the cost grew with how complicated the tasks were, as the brain shifted goals and reloaded the rules for the new task. Meyer later suggested that frequent switching could eat a large share of someone's productive time. The same logic scales up from tasks to life goals: chasing five big changes at once means paying the restart cost over and over.

Try it this week

Name the one area of your life that would lift the others if it improved. For the next ninety days, make it the first thing you work on each day.

In plain words

You can't fix everything at once. Pick the one area that would make the biggest difference and put your best energy there until it moves.

Next: Effective action →
Law 4 · Strategic moves and the 80/20 rule

The Law of Effective Action

In short

Being busy isn't the same as making progress. Meurisse asks you to brainstorm at least twenty actions that could move you toward your goal, then pick the three to five strategic moves that would make the biggest difference, using the 80/20 rule: a small share of actions usually drives most of the results.

The big ideas

  • List many options first. Write twenty or more possible actions. The best ideas often come after the obvious ones run out.
  • Pick three to five strategic moves. Ask which few actions, done well, would make the others easier or unnecessary. These get your prime time.
  • Use the 80/20 rule everywhere. A minority of clients, tasks, relationships or worries usually account for most of the gain or the pain. Find that minority.
  • Learn from people who've done it. Role models show you which moves actually worked, so you don't have to guess.
From the research

In 1896 the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto noticed that about 80 percent of the land in Italy was owned by about 20 percent of the people. Decades later the quality pioneer Joseph Juran generalised the pattern as "the vital few and the trivial many" and named it after Pareto. The exact split varies, and nothing makes it always 80/20, but lopsided distributions show up again and again: a few products make most of the sales, a few bugs cause most of the crashes. Meurisse's point is to look for the lopsidedness in your own actions.

Try it this week

Write twenty actions that could move your main goal forward. Circle the three that would make the most difference and schedule the first one for tomorrow morning.

In plain words

A few actions do most of the work. List all your options, then put your time into the handful that matter most.

Next: Deliberate practice →
Law 5 · Practising the way experts do

The Law of Deliberate Practice

In short

Years of doing something don't make you an expert by themselves. What does, according to the research Meurisse draws on, is deliberate practice: working just past your current level, on specific weaknesses, with full focus and fast feedback, guided by a clear picture of what good looks like.

The big ideas

  • Stretch, don't coast. Practice that feels easy is mostly repetition. Growth happens just outside your comfort zone, not deep in panic.
  • Specific goals, full focus. "Get better at piano" is a wish. "Play bars 9 to 16 at full tempo without mistakes" is something you can practise.
  • Feedback closes the loop. A coach, a recording, a score: without feedback you can practise the same mistake for years.
  • Build mental representations. Experts carry detailed pictures of what good looks like. Studying great examples sharpens yours.
From the research

In 1993 Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer studied violin students at a music academy in Berlin. The students their teachers rated as the best had, by age eighteen, spent far more hours on solitary, deliberate practice than those rated good, who in turn had spent more than the students training to become music teachers. Ericsson spent the rest of his career describing what made such practice different from simply playing: clear goals, focus, feedback and constant stretching. His later book Peak, with Robert Pool, sets out those elements, and Meurisse's checklist follows them closely.

Worth knowing

A 2014 meta-analysis by Brooke Macnamara and colleagues found that deliberate practice explains a meaningful but smaller share of differences in performance than the early work suggested, with talent, starting age and other factors also mattering. Practice is necessary, not the whole story.

Try it this week

For your next practice session, write one specific target, decide how you'll get feedback (record it, time it, ask someone), and stop to adjust halfway through.

In plain words

Doing something a lot isn't enough. Practise on purpose: just past your level, with a clear target, full focus and quick feedback.

Next: Consistency →
Law 6 · Turn strategic moves into daily habits

The Law of Consistency

In short

Once you know your strategic moves, the job is to make them automatic. Meurisse argues that consistency beats intensity: a modest action done every day compounds, while bursts of effort followed by long gaps keep resetting you to zero. Turn your key moves into daily habits, starting with one morning habit kept for thirty days.

The big ideas

  • Small and daily beats big and rare. Thirty minutes a day keeps a skill warm. Three and a half hours on Sunday lets most of the week's forgetting win.
  • Habits remove the decision. When an action is a habit, you don't have to find the motivation each day. It's simply what you do.
  • Start with one morning habit. Mornings are the most predictable part of most days. Anchor one strategic move there for thirty days.
  • Don't break the chain twice. Missing one day is normal. Missing two starts a new habit: not doing it.
From the research

In 2010 Phillippa Lally and colleagues at University College London asked 96 volunteers to pick a new daily habit, such as eating fruit at lunch or running before dinner, and report every day for twelve weeks how automatic it felt. On average it took about 66 days for the behaviour to reach its peak automaticity, but the range was huge, from 18 days to an estimated 254. Missing an occasional day didn't derail the process. The lesson matches the law: there's no magic 21 days, but steady repetition does eventually turn effort into habit.

Try it this week

Choose one strategic move and attach it to your morning: "After I make coffee, I will..." Mark each day you do it, and if you miss one, make the next day non-negotiable.

In plain words

Little and often wins. Turn your most important actions into daily habits and let repetition do the heavy lifting.

Next: Identification →
Law 7 · Become the person who has it

The Law of Identification

In short

You tend to act in line with who you believe you are. So Meurisse asks you to identify with the person who has already reached your goal: what they believe, how they think and feel, what they do each day, which habits they've adopted and which they've dropped. Then start behaving like them now.

The big ideas

  • Identity drives behaviour. Someone who thinks "I'm trying to quit smoking" is still a smoker. Someone who thinks "I don't smoke" turns down the cigarette without a fight.
  • Describe your future self. Write how the person who's already succeeded thinks, feels and acts. Make it concrete enough to copy.
  • Borrow their habits today. You don't need the result to adopt the habits that produce it. Act as that person in small ways now.
  • Let old habits go. Part of the new identity is what that person no longer does. Name those habits too.
From the research

In a 2011 study, Christopher Bryan and colleagues asked eligible voters in California and New Jersey a few questions before an election. Some were asked how important it was "to vote"; others how important it was "to be a voter". The noun version, which framed voting as part of who you are, led to noticeably higher turnout in official records. The researchers argued that inviting people to claim an identity made the behaviour feel more like an expression of self. That's the law of identification in miniature.

Worth knowing

Later, larger studies by other researchers (for example Gerber and colleagues in 2016) did not find the same turnout boost from the noun wording, so treat the voter study as an illustration of the idea, not a proven trick.

Try it this week

Finish this sentence and put it where you'll see it each morning: "I am the kind of person who..." Then do one small thing today that proves it.

In plain words

Act like the person who already has what you want. Decide who that is, copy their habits now, and the results start to follow.

Next: Conditioning →
Law 8 · Train your mind every day

The Law of Conditioning

In short

Your mind is conditioned by whatever you repeat: the thoughts you rehearse, the media you consume, the stories you tell yourself. Meurisse asks you to condition it on purpose with a daily practice, ideally in a morning ritual, that trains you toward positive, empowering emotions instead of leaving it to chance.

The big ideas

  • You're already conditioned. Years of thoughts and inputs have shaped your default mood and beliefs. The question is whether you choose the conditioning from now on.
  • Repetition shapes the default. A thought rehearsed every day becomes a habit of mind, whether it's "I can figure this out" or "nothing ever works".
  • Morning sets the tone. A short ritual before the day's demands (gratitude, visualisation, reading your goals) steers the rest of the day.
  • Guard the inputs. Hours of alarming news or comparison scrolling condition you too. Cut what drags your baseline down.
The idea in action

Picture someone who starts each day by reaching for their phone: news alerts, a work email marked urgent, a friend's holiday photos. By 7.15 she's anxious, behind and a little envious, and the day hasn't started. Now picture her for a month with a different first twenty minutes: water, a few lines about what she's grateful for, a reread of her goals, a short walk. Nothing about her job or bills has changed, but her default setting has. That's conditioning: the mind settles into whatever it rehearses most often.

Try it this week

Add one five-minute conditioning practice to your morning for the next two weeks, before you check your phone: three things you're grateful for and one line about your goal.

In plain words

Your mind becomes what you feed it every day. Choose a short daily practice that trains it toward good feelings, and cut the inputs that drag you down.

Next: Attraction →
Law 9 · What you focus on expands

The Law of Attraction

In short

Meurisse's law of attraction is about attention. What you focus on, you notice more, think about more and act on more. So he asks you to list what you'll stop focusing on (and cross it out) and what you'll focus on instead (and underline it), then guard your attention accordingly.

The big ideas

  • Attention is a filter. Your brain can't take in everything, so it keeps what matches your current focus. Look for problems and you'll find plenty; look for opportunities and you'll find those.
  • Focus shapes action. What you notice is what you respond to, so a change of focus quietly changes what you do each day.
  • Stop-list and focus-list. Write both. Crossing out the old focus and underlining the new one makes the switch concrete.
  • No magic needed. You don't have to believe thoughts reach out into the universe. Attention plus action is enough to explain why focus matters.
From the research

In 1999 Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris showed people a short video of two teams passing basketballs and asked them to count the passes made by the team in white. Midway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked into the middle of the scene, thumped their chest and walked off. About half the viewers never saw it. They weren't careless; their attention was so firmly on the passes that something obvious didn't register. Focus decides what exists for you, which is why choosing your focus deliberately matters so much.

Worth knowing

The popular "law of attraction", the idea that thoughts alone draw events to you, has no scientific support. The book's useful version is about attention and action, and that's how this guide presents it.

Try it this week

Write two short lists: three things you'll stop dwelling on (cross each out) and three you'll focus on instead (underline each). Read the underlined list every morning this week.

In plain words

You see what you're looking for. Decide what deserves your attention and you'll start noticing, and acting on, more of it.

Next: Reset →
Law 10 · Start again any day

The Law of Reset

In short

You'll slip. The law of reset says a slip only becomes a failure if you treat it as one. You can restart at any moment: wipe yesterday's slate, re-commit, and act as if today were day one. Meurisse offers three ways in: brainstorm what you'd change, take a seven-day "born again" challenge, or visualise your future self.

The big ideas

  • A slip isn't a verdict. Missing a workout or overspending one week says nothing about who you are. Abandoning the plan because of it is what does the damage.
  • Restart fast. The shorter the gap between slipping and resetting, the less ground you lose. Ideally, the very next day.
  • Make a ritual of it. A clear reset, a list of changes, a seven-day challenge, a fresh page, helps you mentally close the old chapter.
  • Every day is a fresh start. You don't need January or a Monday. Any morning can be the beginning.
From the research

In 2014 Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis looked at gym visits at a university, Google searches for the word "diet" and sign-ups for goal-setting websites. All three rose at the start of a new week, a new month, after a public holiday and after birthdays. They called it the fresh start effect: dates that feel like new beginnings help people mentally separate from past failures and make a new attempt. Meurisse's law of reset takes the idea further: you don't have to wait for the calendar to hand you a fresh start. You can declare one.

Try it this week

Next time you slip, reset within 24 hours: write one line about what you'll do differently and do the habit that same day, even in its smallest form.

In plain words

Everyone slips. What matters is how fast you start again. Any day can be day one.

Next: Intent →
Law 11 · Decide how you'll show up

The Law of Intent

In short

Much of life runs on autopilot: the commute, the meeting, the evening at home. The law of intent says to choose, before each part of the day, how you want to feel and act during it. Meurisse suggests picking one part of your day and setting an intention for it every day for a week.

The big ideas

  • Autopilot isn't neutral. Without an intention, old habits and other people's moods decide how a meeting or an evening goes.
  • Set it before, not during. A few seconds before you walk in, decide: "I'll be calm and listen first," or "I'll be fully present with the kids."
  • Intent shapes small choices. An intention to be patient changes the tone of your reply, whether you check your phone, how you handle a delay.
  • Start with one part of the day. Pick the part that goes worst. Set intent there for seven days and notice what changes.
The idea in action

A manager notices that her Monday team meeting always leaves her irritated. For a week she tries something small: in the thirty seconds before she walks in, she decides she'll be curious rather than impatient and will ask two questions before making any point. The agenda is the same and so are the people. But she interrupts less, learns about a problem she'd been missing, and leaves calmer. The meeting didn't change on its own; she arrived with an aim.

Try it this week

Choose the part of your day that most often goes badly. For the next seven days, before it starts, say one sentence to yourself about how you want to feel and act in it.

In plain words

Decide how you want to be before each part of your day begins. A few seconds of intention beats an hour of autopilot.

Next: Continuous learning →
Law 12 · A little every day

The Law of Continuous Learning

In short

The world and your goals keep changing, so the people who keep learning keep winning. Meurisse's ask is modest: at least ten minutes a day of educational reading or listening, related to your goals. Small daily learning compounds into expertise most people never build.

The big ideas

  • Ten minutes is enough to start. Ten minutes a day is about sixty hours a year, more than many people spend learning anything after school.
  • Learn toward your goal. Choose material that helps your current strategic moves, so learning turns into action quickly.
  • Books are compressed experience. Someone spent years learning what you can read in a week. That's the best deal in the world.
  • Apply as you learn. The law of mastery still holds: learning only pays when you practise what you learn.
The idea in action

A young accountant decides to read twelve pages a day about the field he wants to move into, data analysis. Twelve pages takes him about fifteen minutes on the train. It doesn't feel like much, and for the first months nothing visible changes. But twelve pages a day is roughly fifteen books a year. Two years later he has read about thirty books on the subject, tried the techniques on his own work and become the person colleagues ask. Nobody saw him study; they only saw the result.

Try it this week

Pick one book that helps your main goal and put it where you'll see it first thing each morning. Read ten minutes before you open email for the next two weeks.

In plain words

Learn a little every day. Ten minutes of reading toward your goal adds up to dozens of books and hours you'd never find otherwise.

Next: Perseverance →
Law 13 · The bullet-proof timeframe

The Law of Perseverance

In short

Most goals aren't failed; they're abandoned. Meurisse's tool here is the bullet-proof timeframe: a period you set in advance, often measured in years, during which you won't quit, whatever happens. It takes "should I give up?" off the table, so your energy goes into getting better rather than doubting.

The big ideas

  • Most people quit too soon. Results usually come later than expected, and the dip before them is where most people stop.
  • Set the timeframe in advance. Choose a length that's realistic for your goal (often longer than you'd like) and commit to it while you're calm.
  • Doubt costs energy. Asking every week whether to quit drains the effort you need. A fixed timeframe ends the debate.
  • Others will give up. Persistence is an advantage partly because it's rare: many competitors drop out along the way.
An old story

The journalist Jacob Riis, writing about his own work, described watching a stonecutter hammering at a rock perhaps a hundred times without a crack showing. Then, on the hundred-and-first blow, it split in two. He knew it wasn't that last blow that did it, but all the ones that came before. The image appears in countless books on persistence because it captures what's so hard about it: from the outside, and often from the inside, the first hundred blows look like failure.

Try it this week

Write your bullet-proof timeframe on a card: "I will work on [goal] until [date], no matter what." Choose a date that honestly gives it enough time, and keep the card where you work.

In plain words

Decide in advance how long you'll keep going and don't reopen the question until then. Most rocks split later than you'd think.

Next: Courage →
Law 14 · Do the thing you're afraid of

The Law of Courage

In short

Every goal has a task you keep postponing because it scares you: the call, the pitch, the publish button. Meurisse's law of courage asks you to name that task and do it this week. Fear shrinks with action and grows with avoidance, and confidence is mostly the memory of having acted despite fear.

The big ideas

  • Fear inflates in the dark. The longer you avoid something, the bigger it looks. Doing it usually reveals it's smaller than you imagined.
  • Courage is acting while afraid. You don't need to stop feeling fear first. Feel it, and do the task anyway.
  • Each act lowers the next fear. The second cold call is easier than the first; by the twentieth it's routine.
  • Postponed tasks drain energy. A task you dread sits in your head all week. Doing it frees attention for everything else.
From the research

Exposure is one of the best-supported tools in psychology for treating fears and phobias. People face the thing they fear in gradual, repeated steps, from looking at a picture of a dog to standing near one, without escaping. Across many studies, fear typically drops both within a session and from one session to the next, because the brain learns that the feared outcome doesn't arrive. Meurisse's law of courage is an everyday version: do the dreaded thing, survive it, and the next time is easier.

Try it this week

Write the task you've postponed the longest because of fear. Put it on your calendar for this week, with a time, and do it then, nervous or not.

In plain words

The thing you're avoiding is smaller than it looks. Do it while you're still scared; fear shrinks each time you act.

Next: Flexibility →
Law 15 · Firm goal, flexible route

The Law of Flexibility

In short

Perseverance doesn't mean repeating the same failing approach. The law of flexibility says to hold your goal firmly and your methods loosely: when a route is blocked, look for another way, reframe the goal if you need to, and occasionally ask the honest question of whether it still excites you at all.

The big ideas

  • Persist on the goal, not the tactic. If something hasn't worked after real effort, changing your approach isn't giving up. It's learning.
  • List alternative routes. For any goal there are several paths: another market, another skill, another partner. Write them down before you need them.
  • Reframe when needed. Sometimes the goal itself needs a new shape: a smaller first version, a different deadline, a different angle.
  • Knowing when to stop is part of it. If a goal no longer excites you after honest reflection, letting it go frees energy for one that does.
The idea in action

In the late 2000s Stewart Butterfield's small company, Tiny Speck, was building an online game called Glitch. To coordinate their work, the team had built an internal messaging tool. The game never found a large enough audience and closed in 2012. But the team looked at what they had and noticed the tool was the most useful thing they'd made. They reshaped it into a product for other companies and launched it in 2013 as Slack. They kept the goal, building something people would love to use, and let go of the route.

Try it this week

Write your main goal at the top of a page and list three completely different routes to it. Circle the one you'll switch to if your current route still isn't working by a date you set.

In plain words

Stick to your goal, not to your first plan. When the road is blocked, take another one.

Next: Patience →
Law 16 · Results take longer than you think

The Law of Patience

In short

Almost everything worthwhile takes longer than planned. The law of patience asks you to expect that, and to stop reading slow early results as proof it isn't working. Meurisse asks you to recall a time you gave up too early and imagine what patience might have changed.

The big ideas

  • Early growth is invisible. Skills, audiences and savings grow quietly at first. The visible jump comes after a long, flat-looking stretch.
  • Impatience causes quitting. Most people don't fail because the goal was impossible; they stop just before the curve turns up.
  • Expect it to take longer. Doubling your time estimate isn't pessimism, it's realism. It protects your motivation.
  • Patient isn't passive. Patience means keeping up the daily work while you wait, not waiting instead of working.
An old story

A popular parable describes a kind of bamboo that shows almost nothing above ground for its first years, however carefully it's watered. Then, in a single season, it shoots up many metres. The growth wasn't sudden: for years the plant was building a vast root system underground, the part no one could see. Gardeners who dug it up early, assuming it was dead, would have killed it just before the visible growth began.

Worth knowing

The parable exaggerates real biology. Many bamboos do grow very fast once established after a slow start of a few years, but the story is a metaphor rather than botany.

Try it this week

Look at the goal you're most impatient about. Double the time you gave it, write the new date down, and decide what you'll keep doing daily until then.

In plain words

Results take longer than you want, and early growth is hard to see. Keep doing the work and give it the time it needs.

Next: Long-term thinking →
Law 17 · Today's choices, ten years out

The Law of Long-Term Thinking

In short

The last law ties the others together. Ask whether what you're doing today, and the thoughts you keep thinking, serve your long-term vision. Small daily choices look trivial in isolation, but over years they compound into completely different lives.

The big ideas

  • Think in years, act in days. Hold a picture of where you want to be in ten years, then let it decide what you do this afternoon.
  • Small choices compound. One percent better or worse each day looks like nothing in a week and like everything in a few years.
  • Check your recurring thoughts. The thoughts you repeat every day are a long-term choice too. Do they fit the life you're building?
  • Short-term discomfort, long-term gain. Most good long-term choices cost something now. Seeing the long arc makes the cost easier to pay.
From the research

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, Walter Mischel's team at Stanford offered preschool children a choice: one treat now, or two if they could wait alone for about fifteen minutes. Follow-ups years later found that children who waited longer tended, on average, to have better outcomes such as higher test scores. The study became famous as proof that self-control shapes life. A larger 2018 replication by Tyler Watts and colleagues found a much smaller link once family background was taken into account, suggesting the ability to wait partly reflects circumstances. The underlying idea still holds for adults choosing on purpose: weighing the future against the moment is a skill you can practise.

Worth knowing

The marshmallow test is often oversold. The 2018 replication suggests the link with later success is weaker than first reported and partly explained by children's home environments.

Try it this week

Tonight, write where you want to be in ten years in three sentences. Tomorrow, before each bigger decision, ask: does this move me toward that or away from it?

In plain words

Little choices add up over years. Keep your long-term picture in mind and let it decide what you do today.

Part IV · building emotional resilience

Emotional Resilience

Every long goal runs into setbacks, and setbacks bring bad days. This part is about not letting those days decide the outcome. Meurisse covers planning for the worst while you're calm, acting on decisions rather than moods, reframing failure, getting through hard stretches, treating yourself kindly, practising gratitude and shaping the environment that shapes you.

The idea behind this part

Your feelings will go up and down. Resilience is building a life in which the down days can't cancel the plan.

Part IV · 1 · Plan for the storm while it's calm

The Importance of Emotional Stability

In short

Long goals bring setbacks, and setbacks bring emotional storms that can sink a plan. Meurisse's answer is to prepare while you're calm: list the worst things that could happen, imagine them, decide in advance how you'll respond, and even decide what, if anything, would make you give up.

The big ideas

  • Setbacks are certain; panic isn't. You can't prevent every setback, but you can decide now how you'll react to the likely ones.
  • Picture the worst, then plan. List worst-case scenarios, visualise them, and write a response for each. When one happens, you already know your next step.
  • Define your give-up line. Deciding in advance what would truly make you stop keeps you from quitting on a bad day for a lesser reason.
  • Support yourself, not just the plan. Self-compassion, gratitude and a good environment, the rest of this part, keep your emotions steady enough to follow the plan.
From the research

Psychologist Gary Klein proposed the premortem in a 2007 Harvard Business Review article. Before a project starts, the team imagines it's a year later and the project has failed badly, then each person writes down why. Research by Deborah Mitchell, Jay Russo and Nancy Pennington in 1989 had found that imagining an event has already happened makes people better at finding reasons for it. The Stoics practised something similar two thousand years earlier, picturing losses in advance so they wouldn't be thrown when they came. Meurisse's exercise is a personal premortem.

Try it this week

Write the three worst things that could realistically happen on the way to your goal. Next to each, write the first action you'd take if it happened. Keep the page with your goals.

In plain words

Storms will come. Plan your response while the weather's calm, and you'll steer through them instead of being thrown off course.

Next: Overrule feelings →
Part IV · 2 · Decisions, then actions, then feelings

Overruling Your Feelings

In short

If you only act when you feel like it, your goals are at the mercy of your mood. Meurisse's reminder, to stick where you'll see it, is Decisions > Actions > Feelings: make the decision, take the action, and let the feelings catch up, which they often do once you've started.

The big ideas

  • Feelings are unreliable guides. Your mood swings with sleep, weather and a hundred other things that have nothing to do with your goal.
  • Decide once, act daily. A decision made in a clear moment should carry you through the days you don't feel like it.
  • Action often creates motivation. Many people find that starting, five minutes of the task, is what brings the energy, not the other way round.
  • Feelings still count. Overruling isn't ignoring. Notice the feeling, learn from it if there's something to learn, and act anyway.
The idea in action

A writer decides to write 500 words every morning for a year. On day 1 she feels inspired. On day 12 she feels tired and unconvinced, and she writes anyway. On day 40 she's had a bad night and the words are clumsy, and she writes anyway. Over the year her mood is good on maybe half the days, but she writes on nearly all of them, and many of her favourite pages come from mornings she didn't want to start. If she'd waited to feel like it, she'd have half the book, and missed some of the best of it.

Try it this week

Write "Decisions > Actions > Feelings" on a sticky note and put it where you do your key habit. On a low day, commit to just the first five minutes.

In plain words

Don't wait to feel like it. Decide, act, and let your feelings follow. They usually do.

Next: Reframing →
Part IV · 3 · Choose the meaning that helps

Reframing

In short

Events don't arrive with meanings attached; you supply them. Reframing means deliberately choosing an interpretation that helps you move forward. Meurisse asks you to look at a major past failure and find the empowering meanings in it: what it taught you and what it made possible.

The big ideas

  • Meaning is a choice. A rejection can mean "I'm not good enough" or "this wasn't the right fit; next". The event is the same; the meaning changes what you do.
  • Failure is feedback. Every failed attempt tells you something specific about what to change. That's information you couldn't have got any other way.
  • Ask better questions. "Why does this always happen to me?" leads nowhere. "What can I learn from this?" leads to a next step.
  • Honest, not fake. Reframing isn't pretending something bad was good. It's finding the true meaning that also helps.
From the research

In a 2010 study, Jeremy Jamieson and colleagues told some students preparing for the GRE graduate exam that feeling anxious before a test can actually improve performance, because arousal helps the body rise to a challenge. Other students got no such message. The students taught to reframe their nerves as helpful scored higher on the maths section of a practice test and, according to later reports, on the real exam too. Their nerves didn't disappear; what changed was the meaning they gave them.

Try it this week

Write down one failure that still bothers you. Under it, list three things it taught you and one thing it made possible that wouldn't have happened otherwise.

In plain words

What happens to you is fixed; what it means is up to you. Choose the meaning that teaches you something and gets you moving.

Part IV · 4 · Getting through the dark stretches

Overcoming Challenging Times

In short

Some stretches are just hard. For those, Meurisse offers three habits: picture yourself handling the setback with kindness, delay major decisions while your emotions are low, and catch the negative thought patterns that run on repeat, replacing them with empowering counter-beliefs you reinforce each day.

The big ideas

  • Be kind to yourself in the dip. Imagining how you'd comfort a friend in your situation, and then saying that to yourself, shortens the low.
  • Don't decide in the storm. Big decisions made in a low mood tend to be bad ones. If it can wait until you're steadier, let it wait.
  • Spot the loop. Low moods run on a few repeated thoughts: "I always fail", "nothing works". Write them down so you can see them.
  • Install a counter-belief. For each negative pattern, write a believable opposite ("I've got through hard things before") and repeat it daily.
The idea in action

A founder's biggest client leaves on a Tuesday. By Tuesday night he's drafting an email to shut the company down. Instead, following a rule he set months before, he gives himself one week before any big decision. He writes down the thought looping in his head, "I'm not cut out for this", and next to it the counter-belief "I've replaced a client before, in 2022". By the following Tuesday he feels steadier, has two new leads, and the shut-down email is still in drafts. The situation was serious; the decision would have been worse.

Try it this week

Make a rule today, while you're calm: "I won't make a major decision within 72 hours of a big setback." Write it where you'll find it.

In plain words

Hard times pass. Be kind to yourself, wait before you make big decisions, and replace the gloomy thoughts on repeat with truer, stronger ones.

Next: Self-compassion →
Part IV · 5 · Accept, act or ask

Cultivating Self-Compassion

In short

Harsh self-criticism feels like it keeps you sharp, but it mostly makes you afraid to try. Meurisse recommends self-compassion instead, and offers a practical exercise: list the things you're poor at and label each one Accept (it's fine to be bad at it), Act (work on it) or Ask (get help).

The big ideas

  • Criticism isn't fuel. Beating yourself up after a mistake drains energy and makes the next attempt scarier.
  • Talk to yourself like a coach. Kind and honest: "That didn't work. What will you try next?" gets you back in the game faster.
  • Accept, act or ask. Not every weakness needs fixing. Some you accept, some you work on, and some you hand to someone better at them.
  • Good enough for now. The book's reminder: you're good enough as you are right now, and you're making progress.
From the research

In 2012 Juliana Breines and Serena Chen at the University of California, Berkeley, gave students a hard vocabulary test they were bound to do poorly on. Afterwards, one group got a self-compassionate message (it's common to find these tests hard, don't be hard on yourself), another a self-esteem boost (you must be smart if you got into Berkeley), and a third nothing. Given a chance to study for a second test, the self-compassion group studied longer than the others. Kindness toward their failure made them more willing to work on it, not less. Researcher Kristin Neff's work points the same way.

Try it this week

List five things you're not good at. Label each Accept, Act or Ask. For one marked Ask, contact someone this week who could help.

In plain words

Being hard on yourself doesn't help. Be kind and honest instead, and decide for each weakness whether to accept it, work on it or ask for help.

Next: Gratitude →
Part IV · 6 · Notice what's going right

Practising Gratitude

In short

When you're chasing big goals, it's easy to see only what's missing. Gratitude balances that. Meurisse asks you to choose one gratitude practice, such as writing down three things you're grateful for each day, and keep it for two weeks. It steadies your mood and keeps the hard days in proportion.

The big ideas

  • Goals focus you on the gap. Ambition looks at what isn't there yet. Without a counterweight, that turns into constant dissatisfaction.
  • Gratitude is a trained skill. Noticing good things gets easier with practice, as your attention learns to look for them.
  • It builds resilience. A record of good things is evidence you can return to on bad days.
  • Keep it specific. "My family" every day goes stale. "My brother called to check how the interview went" stays alive.
From the research

In a 2003 study, Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough asked volunteers to keep a short weekly record for ten weeks. One group wrote five things they were grateful for, another five hassles, and a third simply five events. The gratitude group felt better about their lives as a whole, were more optimistic about the coming week, reported fewer physical complaints and spent more time exercising than the hassles group. Later studies have found gratitude effects that are real but usually modest, which fits the book's advice: it's a small daily habit, not a cure-all.

Try it this week

For the next fourteen days, write three specific good things before bed. Make at least one about something you did yourself.

In plain words

Chasing goals keeps your eyes on what's missing. Writing down a few good things each day keeps you steady on the way.

Next: Proximity →
Part IV · 7 · Shape the environment that shapes you

Leveraging the Power of Proximity

In short

You gradually become like what you're close to. Meurisse divides your environment into three: mental (what you read, watch and listen to), social (the people you spend time with) and physical (your home, desk and the cues around you). Change each so it pulls you toward your goals instead of away.

The big ideas

  • Environment beats willpower. Fighting your surroundings every day is exhausting. Changing them once makes the right choice the easy one.
  • Mental: curate your inputs. Swap some of the scrolling for books, talks and podcasts from people doing what you want to do.
  • Social: choose your circle. Spend more time with people who are working toward something, and less with those who mock effort.
  • Physical: design your space. Put what helps in sight and within reach; put what distracts out of sight and out of reach.
From the research

In 2007 Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler analysed 32 years of records from the Framingham Heart Study, which tracked the health of thousands of people and their social ties. They reported that a person's chance of becoming obese rose by 57 percent if a friend became obese, and found similar clustering for smoking and happiness. Other researchers questioned how much of this was true influence rather than similar people choosing each other, or shared surroundings. Either way, the pattern that habits travel through friendships is now widely studied, and it's the core of the law of proximity.

Worth knowing

Christakis and Fowler's contagion claims are debated because, with observational data, it's hard to separate influence from people choosing similar friends. Treat the numbers as suggestive.

Try it this week

Make one change in each environment this week: unfollow one account that drags you down, arrange one meeting with someone ahead of you, and move one distraction out of sight.

In plain words

Your surroundings shape you whether you notice or not. Change what you read, who you're with and what's around you so they pull you toward your goal.

Part V and bonus · working with others, and five core beliefs

Working With Others

The last part turns outward. No big goal is reached alone, so Meurisse ends with two habits for working with people, adding value to their lives and daring to ask for what you need, and a bonus chapter of five beliefs that keep you going when you doubt yourself.

The idea behind this part

Give value generously, ask for help boldly, and hold the beliefs that make giving up unthinkable.

Part V · 1 · Give first

Adding Value to People's Lives

In short

The fastest way to get help with your goals is to help other people with theirs. Meurisse asks you to understand the people around you, their goals, problems, visions, values and even hobbies, look for ways to help, and see the potential in them that they may not yet see themselves.

The big ideas

  • Give before you need. Relationships built by helping are there when you need them. Ones built only when you need something feel transactional.
  • Understand what people want. You can only add real value if you know someone's goals, problems and values. Ask, and listen.
  • See their potential. Telling someone what you see they're capable of can be one of the most valuable things you give.
  • Value compounds through networks. People you help talk to others. Over years, a habit of giving builds a web of goodwill.
From the research

In Give and Take (2013), Wharton professor Adam Grant sorted people by how they handle the give-and-take of work. Givers help others without keeping score; takers try to get more than they give; matchers aim for an even trade. Looking across studies of engineers, salespeople and medical students, he found that givers were over-represented at the bottom of success measures, because some got exploited and burned out, but also at the top. The givers who rose helped generously while protecting their own time and goals. Meurisse's advice to add value works best in that form.

Try it this week

Pick two people in your circle. Ask each about their current goal or biggest problem, and offer one concrete piece of help this week, expecting nothing back.

In plain words

Help other people get what they want. Listen for their goals and problems, offer what you can, and the help tends to find its way back.

Next: Asking →
Part V · 2 · If you don't ask, the answer is no

Developing an Asking Mentality

In short

Many goals stall for want of a simple question: for advice, an introduction, a discount, a chance. Meurisse urges you to build an asking mentality and to name one thing you've wanted to ask for but haven't dared to. Most people are more willing to help than you think, and a "no" costs far less than never asking.

The big ideas

  • Not asking is a guaranteed no. Every request you don't make has the same result as a refusal, without the chance of a yes.
  • People say yes more than you expect. We overestimate how awkward a request is and underestimate how much people like to help.
  • Ask specifically. "Could you introduce me to your editor?" is easy to say yes to. "Can you help me with my career?" isn't.
  • A no is information. Rejection tells you something about timing, the fit or the request. Adjust and ask again, or ask someone else.
From the research

In 2008 Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake at Stanford asked participants to approach strangers with requests such as filling in a short questionnaire, and first to predict how many people they'd need to ask to get five yeses. People guessed they'd need to ask about twice as many as they actually did; strangers said yes far more often than expected. The askers focused on how easy it would be to say no, while the people asked felt the social pressure of saying no to someone's face. The lesson for goals is simple: ask more often than feels comfortable.

Try it this week

Write down the one thing you've wanted to ask for but haven't. Make it specific, choose whom to ask, and send the message before the end of the week.

In plain words

If you never ask, the answer is always no. Ask clearly and more often; people say yes more than you think.

Bonus · The bonus chapter

Five Core Beliefs to Achieve Success

In short

The book closes with five beliefs to carry when doubt creeps in: if one person can do it, a million can; if others can, so can I; I can always get better; others will give up, so I will succeed; and success is inevitable. Together they reframe almost any doubt about a goal into a reason to keep going.

The big ideas

  • If one, then one million. Once anyone has done a thing, it's proven possible. The question becomes how, not whether.
  • If others can, I can. People who reached your goal had no magic. They learned and practised, which you can do too.
  • I can get better. Whatever your starting level, skill responds to practice. Today's level is a starting point.
  • Others will give up; success is inevitable. Most people quit along the way, so persistence is an edge. Keep at it long enough and success becomes a matter of time.
From the research

For years many people believed running a mile in under four minutes might be beyond the human body. On 6 May 1954, the British medical student Roger Bannister ran it in 3 minutes 59.4 seconds in Oxford. Just 46 days later, the Australian John Landy broke Bannister's record, and within a few years many more runners had gone under four minutes. Training, tracks and rivalry all played a part, but the barrier in people's heads clearly mattered too. It's the first two beliefs in action: if one can, others can.

Try it this week

Write the five beliefs on a card. The next time you doubt your goal, read them and pick the one that answers that specific doubt.

In plain words

Five beliefs keep you going: it's been done, so it can be done; you can learn it; you can improve; most people quit; and if you don't, success is only a matter of time.
