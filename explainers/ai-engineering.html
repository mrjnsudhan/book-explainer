Training a frontier model costs a fortune. Building something useful on top of one does not, and that is the job Chip Huyen calls AI engineering: picking a model, measuring it, steering it with prompts and context, finetuning it when you must, and serving it fast and cheap. Each of the ten chapters here has the short answer, the big ideas, a worked example with real numbers, a lab that computes things live, questions to sit with, and a quiz.

Chapter 1 · Introduction

What is AI engineering, and why now?

The short answer

Models became a service. Once a few labs could train huge general-purpose foundation models and rent them out by the token, the hard part for everyone else moved from training a model to adapting one: choosing it, evaluating it, feeding it the right context and wrapping it in a product. That adaptation work is AI engineering.

The big ideas

  • Self-supervision unlocked scale. Old models needed humans to label every training example. A language model labels itself: hide the next word, ask the model to guess it. Any text on the internet becomes training data, which is why models could grow from millions to hundreds of billions of parameters.
  • From language model to foundation model. Scale made the models general. The same network can summarise, translate, classify, write code and, once trained on images and audio too, describe a photo. "Foundation" means you build many different products on one base.
  • AI engineering is not ML engineering with a new name. ML engineers mostly trained their own models on their own data. AI engineers start from someone else's model and focus on adapting and evaluating it. Inputs and outputs are open-ended text, so evaluation is harder and matters more.
  • Two families of adaptation. Prompt-based techniques (prompting, adding context, RAG, agents) change what you send in and leave the weights alone. Finetuning changes the weights. Start with the first; reach for the second when it stops being enough.
  • The stack has three layers. Application development (prompts, context, evaluation, interface), model development (training, finetuning, dataset work, inference optimisation) and infrastructure (serving, compute, monitoring). Most new work happens at the top, but the lower layers decide your cost and speed.
  • Ask why before how. Before building, decide whether AI is critical or complementary to the product, whether it acts reactively (you ask, it answers) or proactively (it offers things unasked), and how much a human stays in the loop. Those answers set your latency budget, your quality bar and your risk.
  • The last mile is the long mile. Demos are easy because foundation models are already good. Getting from "impressive" to "reliable enough to ship" is where the time goes. Teams often hit a decent quality bar within weeks, then spend months on the last few points.

The book's planning advice can be summarised as a short checklist. Use case: is this a real need, or are you only afraid of being left behind? Role of AI: critical, complementary, reactive or proactive? Role of humans: start with a human approving every output ("crawl"), move to AI handling routine cases ("walk"), and only then let it act alone ("run"). Defensibility: if a better foundation model could do your whole product tomorrow, what is your moat? Usually it is data, distribution, or the workflow you sit inside. Maintenance: models, prices and APIs change every few months, so design for swapping them.

In plain words

A foundation model is a brilliant new hire who has read the whole internet but knows nothing about your company. AI engineering is the onboarding: what to tell them, what files to hand them, how to check their work, when to send them on a specialist course, and how to keep the bill down.

Contemplate
  1. Think of one AI feature you use every day. Is the AI critical or complementary to that product? What would break if it were wrong one time in ten?
  2. If next year's model could do your whole idea in one prompt, what would still be yours? Data, users, workflow, trust?
  3. Where in your own work would "crawl" (AI drafts, a human approves) already save real time?

Chapter 2 · Understanding foundation models

How is a foundation model made, and how does it choose its words?

The short answer

Three choices shape a model: its training data, its architecture and size, and its post-training, which turns a raw text predictor into a helpful assistant. Then, at every step of writing, the model produces a probability for every possible next token, and a sampling strategy decides which one to actually emit. Much of a model's "personality", and many of its failures, come from that last step.

The big ideas

  • A model is what it ate. Most pretraining text is scraped web data, heavy on English and on popular topics. Languages and domains that are thin on the web are thin in the model, which is why there are specialised models for medicine, law, code, and for languages like Thai or Swahili.
  • The transformer dominates, but not forever. Attention lets every token look at every other token, and it parallelises well on GPUs. Its weakness is cost that grows with the square of the context length. Newer families such as state space models (Mamba and its relatives) try to keep the quality while scaling more gently.
  • Three numbers describe scale. Parameters (the model's capacity), training tokens (how much it learned from) and training compute in FLOPs. Training compute is roughly 6 × parameters × tokens.
  • Compute-optimal scaling. The Chinchilla study found that for a fixed compute budget you get the best model with roughly 20 training tokens per parameter. Many modern models deliberately train on far more than that, because a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to serve.
  • Post-training has two stages. Supervised finetuning (SFT) shows the model thousands of good demonstrations of how to respond. Preference finetuning (RLHF with a reward model, or simpler methods like DPO) then teaches it which of two answers people prefer. Pretraining gives knowledge; post-training gives manners.
  • Sampling makes AI probabilistic. Temperature reshapes the probabilities; top-k keeps only the k likeliest tokens; top-p keeps the smallest set whose probabilities add up to p. The same prompt can give different answers, which is useful for creativity and painful for consistency.
  • Hallucination has roots in how models work. Two hypotheses the book discusses: a model can't tell the facts it was given from the text it generated itself, so one wrong token snowballs; and SFT can teach a model to imitate answers that rely on knowledge the labeller had but the model doesn't.
  • Structured outputs can be forced. If you need valid JSON, you can ask nicely in the prompt, repair the output afterwards, finetune for the format, or use constrained sampling, which blocks any token that would break the grammar.
Going deeper

Worked example: from logits to a chosen token

The model's last layer outputs one raw score, a logit, per vocabulary token. Softmax turns logits into probabilities. Temperature T divides the logits first, so a low T exaggerates the gaps and a high T flattens them.

// softmax with temperature pi = exp(zi / T) / Σj exp(zj / T) // three candidate tokens with logits z = [2.0, 1.0, 0.0] T = 1.0 → e2, e1, e0 = 7.39, 2.72, 1.00 → p = 0.67, 0.24, 0.09 T = 0.5 → e4, e2, e0 = 54.6, 7.39, 1.00 → p = 0.87, 0.12, 0.02 T = 2.0 → e1, e.5, e0 = 2.72, 1.65, 1.00 → p = 0.51, 0.31, 0.19 // the logprob of the chosen token is what APIs return as "logprobs" log(0.67) = −0.40 // closer to 0 means the model was more sure

Scaling arithmetic. A 70-billion-parameter model trained compute-optimally wants about 70B × 20 = 1.4 trillion tokens, and training costs about 6 × 70×109 × 1.4×1012 ≈ 5.9 × 1023 FLOPs. On a cluster sustaining 1018 FLOP/s (roughly 2,500 modern GPUs at decent utilisation) that is about a week. Try your own numbers in the second tab.

Test-time compute. You can trade inference cost for quality by sampling several answers and picking the best one, judged by a verifier, a reward model, or a simple majority vote. Doing this well can lift a small model's accuracy close to a much bigger one on tasks with checkable answers, like maths.

In plain words

Pretraining is reading the library. SFT is a job shadowing programme. Preference tuning is a manager saying "this answer, not that one" thousands of times. And sampling is the model rolling loaded dice at every word: temperature decides how loaded they are.

Contemplate
  1. For which of your tasks do you want temperature near zero, and for which would you want it high? What does that say about the difference between "correct" and "good"?
  2. If a model can't tell its own earlier words from facts you gave it, how should that change the way you write prompts and check long outputs?
  3. Why might a lab train a small model on 100× more tokens than Chinchilla suggests? Who pays for training, and who pays for inference?

Chapter 3 · Evaluation methodology

How do you measure something that writes essays?

The short answer

With a toolbox, not a single number. Language-model metrics like perplexity tell you how well a model predicts text. Exact evaluation works when there's a checkable answer: run the code, compare to a reference. For open-ended output you use AI as a judge, and to rank models against each other you use comparative evaluation, the way chess ranks players.

The big ideas

  • Evaluation is the bottleneck. The smarter a model gets, the harder it is to tell whether it's right; you can check a child's maths but not a PhD proof. Open-ended answers have no single correct output, models are black boxes, and public benchmarks saturate fast.
  • Entropy and cross-entropy. Entropy measures how unpredictable text is. Cross-entropy measures how surprised a model is by real text. Lower cross-entropy means the model predicts the data better.
  • Perplexity is cross-entropy made readable. A perplexity of 8 means the model is, on average, as unsure as if it were choosing among 8 equally likely tokens. Useful for comparing base models and spotting odd or contaminated data; less useful after post-training, which deliberately reshapes the probabilities.
  • Functional correctness beats similarity. For code, run the unit tests. pass@k asks whether at least one of k samples passes. When you can't execute, you compare to reference answers: exact match, lexical overlap (BLEU, ROUGE, edit distance) or semantic similarity via embeddings.
  • Embeddings turn meaning into geometry. An embedding model maps text to a vector; similar meanings land close together, measured by cosine similarity. This powers semantic evaluation and, later, retrieval.
  • AI judges are fast, cheap-ish and flawed. A strong model can score a response alone, compare it to a reference, or pick the better of two. But judges are inconsistent, their scores depend on the prompt and model version, and they show self-bias (liking their own outputs), position bias (liking the first option) and verbosity bias (liking longer answers).
  • Compare, then rank. People find "which is better, A or B?" easier than "score this from 1 to 10". Platforms like Chatbot Arena collect millions of such votes and fit ratings with Elo or Bradley-Terry. The catch: pairwise votes say A beats B, not whether A is good enough for your job.
Going deeper

Worked example: perplexity, pass@k and Elo by hand

// a model reads "the cat sat" and gave the true next tokens these probabilities p = [0.50, 0.25, 0.125] cross-entropy H = −(1/n) Σ log2 p = −(−1 −2 −3)/3 = 2 bits per token perplexity PPL = 2H = 4 // as unsure as picking 1 of 4 // pass@k (unbiased): n samples per problem, c of them pass the tests pass@k = 1 − C(n−c, k) / C(n, k) n = 10, c = 3, k = 1 → 1 − C(7,1)/C(10,1) = 1 − 7/10 = 0.30 n = 10, c = 3, k = 5 → 1 − C(7,5)/C(10,5) = 1 − 21/252 = 0.92 // Elo: expected score of A against B, then update after a match EA = 1 / (1 + 10(RB − RA)/400) RA=1200, RB=1000 → EA = 1/(1+10−0.5) = 0.76 A loses (S=0), K=32 → RA ← 1200 + 32·(0 − 0.76) = 1175.7

How to use an AI judge well. Write the criterion precisely ("Does the answer contain any claim not supported by the context? yes/no") rather than vaguely ("Is it good?"). Prefer small classification-style outputs over 1–10 scores. Give examples of each grade. Swap the order of A and B and run twice to cancel position bias. Fix the judge model and prompt version, log them, and treat a judge change like a metric change: your old scores are no longer comparable.

Kinds of judges. A general model prompted as a judge; a reward model trained to score a (prompt, response) pair; a reference-based judge that compares to a gold answer; and a preference model that predicts which of two responses people will prefer. Smaller specialised judges can be cheaper than the model being judged.

In plain words

Perplexity is how often a reader is surprised by a book. pass@k is how many tries you need to get a working answer. An AI judge is a quick but opinionated critic. And an arena is a tasting contest: people can't always say how good a wine is, but they can say which of two they prefer.

Contemplate
  1. Pick one task you'd like AI to do for you. Could you check the answer automatically? If not, what exactly would a human (or judge) look for?
  2. An AI judge gives model A an 8 and model B a 7. What else would you want to know before believing A is better?
  3. A model is top of a public arena. Why might it still be the wrong choice for your product?

Chapter 4 · Evaluate AI systems

How do you choose a model and prove your system works?

The short answer

Decide what "good" means for your application before you look at any leaderboard. Write criteria and rubrics, tie them to business outcomes, build an evaluation set with slices, and use it to filter models down from hundreds to one. Then keep running the same pipeline every time anything changes, because evaluation is not a phase, it's a habit.

The big ideas

  • Four buckets of criteria. Domain-specific capability (can it write SQL, read contracts?), generation capability (fluency, factual consistency, safety), instruction-following (does it respect the format, length and persona you asked for?), and cost and latency.
  • Factual consistency comes in two flavours. Local: is the output supported by the context you gave it? Global: is it true about the world? Checks include asking a judge, sampling several answers and seeing whether they agree (self-consistency, as in SelfCheckGPT), searching the web for evidence, or using a textual-entailment model.
  • A four-step selection workflow. Filter out models whose hard attributes rule them out (licence, privacy, context length, price); use public benchmarks to shortlist; run your own evaluation on the shortlist; then keep monitoring in production.
  • Build or buy is a set of trade-offs. APIs win on capability and convenience. Open-weight models win on data privacy, control, on-device use and sometimes cost at scale. Watch for licence terms, data lineage, and the engineering cost of hosting it yourself.
  • Public benchmarks are a starting point at best. They saturate, they may not match your use, and they may be contaminated: the test questions leaked into the training data. Contamination is often detected with n-gram overlap or unusually low perplexity on the test set.
  • Evaluate every component, every turn. In a multi-step system, score the retrieval separately from the answer and each turn separately from the whole conversation, so you can tell where it fails.
  • Slice your data. An overall number can hide a disaster in one group of users, or even point the wrong way (Simpson's paradox, in the lab below). Look at results per segment: query type, language, user tier, input length.
  • Evaluate your evaluation. Is your signal reliable when you rerun it? Do your metrics agree with each other and with human judgement? Does a better score actually move the business metric you care about?
Going deeper

How big must an evaluation set be?

An accuracy measured on n examples has a standard error of about √(p(1−p)/n). To tell two models apart, the difference has to be bigger than the noise. A rough rule of thumb follows from that square root: every time the gap you want to detect shrinks by about 3×, you need about 10× more examples.

// standard error of an accuracy p measured on n examples SE = √( p(1 − p) / n ) p = 0.80, n = 100 → SE = √(0.16/100) = 0.040 // ±8 points at ~95% p = 0.80, n = 1000 → SE = √(0.16/1000) = 0.013 // ±2.5 points // rough sizes to spot a real difference between two systems gap 30 points → ~10 examples gap 10 points → ~100 gap 3 points → ~1,000 gap 1 point → ~10,000

A good rubric names one criterion per score, defines each level with an example, and maps to something the business feels: "factual consistency above 90% lets us automate 40% of support tickets" is a target a whole team can reason about. Bootstrapping is a quick sanity check: resample your evaluation set with replacement many times and see how much the score wobbles. If it wobbles more than the gap between your candidate models, get more data.

Latency, measured properly. Track time to first token (how long before anything appears), time per output token (how fast it streams) and total latency. Users forgive a slow stream far more than a long blank wait. Look at percentiles (p90, p99), not averages.

In plain words

Choosing a model from a leaderboard is like hiring from a list of exam scores. It narrows the field, but you still need your own interview, with your own questions, scored the same way for every candidate, and enough questions that one lucky answer doesn't decide it.

Contemplate
  1. Write three criteria for an AI feature you care about. For each, what would a score of 1 and a score of 5 look like?
  2. Which slices of your users would you check first, and why might the overall number lie about them?
  3. What business number would you watch to know the AI is actually helping, not just scoring well?

Chapter 5 · Prompt engineering

How do you instruct a model, and keep others from hijacking it?

The short answer

A prompt is a program written in plain language: a task, instructions, examples and context. Good prompts are clear, specific, well-structured and tested like code. And because anything the model reads can contain instructions, every prompt is also an attack surface, so you design defences at the model, prompt and system levels.

The big ideas

  • In-context learning. Models can pick up a task from the prompt alone. Zero-shot gives only instructions; few-shot adds examples. Stronger models need fewer examples, but examples are still the fastest way to show format and tone.
  • System prompt, user prompt, chat template. The system prompt sets the role and rules; the user prompt carries the request. Under the hood both get stitched into one string with special tokens defined by the model's chat template. Use the wrong template and quality silently drops.
  • Context length is not context use. Models handle the start and end of a long prompt better than the middle. "Needle in a haystack" tests hide a fact at different depths to measure this. Put the most important instructions at the beginning or the end.
  • Best practices that keep working. Write explicit instructions; give the model a persona; show examples; specify the output format exactly; supply the context it needs instead of hoping it knows; break complex tasks into smaller prompts; give it room to think (chain-of-thought, or asking it to check its own answer); and iterate with evaluation, not vibes.
  • Decomposition pays twice. Splitting one giant prompt into a chain of small ones makes each step easier, lets you monitor and debug each step, run steps in parallel, and use a cheaper model for the easy ones. The cost is more calls and more latency.
  • Treat prompts as code. Keep them in version control, separate from application logic, with metadata (model, date, owner). Tools that auto-optimise prompts can help, but read what they generate: they can spend many hidden API calls and produce prompts no one understands.
  • Three kinds of attack. Prompt extraction (getting the model to reveal its system prompt), jailbreaking and prompt injection (getting it to do what it shouldn't) and information extraction (leaking private or training data). Injection can be direct, typed by the user, or indirect, hidden in a web page, email or document the model reads.
  • Defence in depth. At the model level, train an instruction hierarchy: system prompt beats user, user beats model output, and tool outputs rank lowest. At the prompt level, state explicitly what must never happen and repeat key rules. At the system level, isolate code execution, require human approval for risky actions, filter inputs and outputs, and watch for anomalies.
Going deeper

Two numbers for every defence, and what prompts cost

A safety measure is judged on two axes. The violation rate is the share of attacks that succeed. The false refusal rate is the share of harmless requests it wrongly blocks. You can always get violations to zero by refusing everything, so a defence that only reports one number is hiding the other.

// rough token arithmetic (English ≈ 4 characters or ¾ of a word per token) system prompt 1,200 tokens + few-shot examples 1,800 + user turn 150 = 3,150 input tokens output ≈ 300 tokens cost per call = 3,150 × $3/M + 300 × $15/M = $0.0095 + $0.0045 = $0.014 × 50,000 calls a day = $700/day // cache the fixed 3,000-token prefix at a 90% discount and the input cost drops ~85%

Why chain-of-thought helps. A model spends the same compute on every token it writes. Asking it to reason step by step gives it more tokens, and so more computation, before it commits to an answer, and the intermediate steps become context for the final one. The price is more output tokens and therefore more latency and cost.

In plain words

Prompting is writing a brief for a very fast contractor who takes everything literally and never asks follow-up questions. Prompt security is remembering that anyone who can slip a note into the contractor's inbox, including a web page, can try to give them orders too.

Contemplate
  1. Take a prompt you've written recently. Which instruction was implicit in your head but never actually written down?
  2. If your assistant can read email and send email, what is the worst thing a malicious incoming email could ask it to do? Which layer of defence would stop it?
  3. When would you accept a higher false refusal rate, and when would that be a bigger failure than a violation?

Chapter 6 · RAG and agents

How do you give a model the right context, and the ability to act?

The short answer

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) fetches the most relevant pieces of your data and puts them in the prompt, so answers rest on facts the model never saw in training. Agents go further: a model plans, calls tools, reads the results and decides what to do next. RAG is really the simplest agent, one with a single read-only tool.

The big ideas

  • Retriever plus generator. Documents are split into chunks and indexed. At query time the retriever finds the best chunks and the generator answers using them. The quality of the whole system is capped by the retriever: the model can't use what it never sees.
  • Term-based retrieval. Match the words. TF-IDF and its refinement BM25 score a document higher when it contains the query's rare words, often, without being padded. Fast, cheap, no training, and very good at exact terms like error codes and product names.
  • Embedding-based retrieval. Match the meaning. Embed every chunk, embed the query, return the nearest vectors. Finds "refund" when the user typed "money back". Needs an embedding model and a vector index; nearest-neighbour search at scale uses approximate methods like HNSW, IVF or product quantisation.
  • Hybrid usually wins. Combine both, for instance by merging their rankings with reciprocal rank fusion, or by retrieving broadly with BM25 and reranking the top results with a stronger model.
  • Retrieval is tuned, not installed. Choose a chunking strategy (size and overlap), rewrite queries (turn "what about the second one?" into a standalone question), and enrich chunks with contextual retrieval, prepending a short note about where the chunk came from. Measure with context precision and recall, and ranking metrics like NDCG or MRR.
  • Tools give agents reach. Tools add knowledge (search, databases), extend capability (calculator, code interpreter, translator) and allow write actions (send an email, issue a refund). Write actions are where the value and the danger both live.
  • Plan, validate, execute, reflect. Good agents separate planning from execution so a plan can be checked before anything happens. Patterns like ReAct interleave reasoning and acting; Reflexion-style loops have the agent evaluate its own result and retry.
  • Errors compound. If each step is 95% reliable, a 10-step task succeeds about 60% of the time. Agent failures include invalid tools, valid tools with wrong parameters, wrong parameter values, giving up or declaring false success, and wasting steps.
  • Memory. An agent has three kinds: its internal knowledge (the weights), short-term memory (the context window) and long-term memory (an external store it retrieves from). When context fills up, it must decide what to drop, summarise or move to long-term storage.
Going deeper

BM25 and reciprocal rank fusion, written out

// BM25 score of document D for query Q score(D,Q) = Σq∈Q IDF(q) · f(q,D)·(k1+1) / ( f(q,D) + k1·(1 − b + b·|D|/avgdl) ) IDF(q) = ln( (N − nq + 0.5) / (nq + 0.5) + 1 ) // f = term count in D, |D| = doc length, typical k1 ≈ 1.2–2, b ≈ 0.75 // reciprocal rank fusion: merge any number of rankings RRF(d) = Σrankers 1 / (k + rank(d)), k ≈ 60 doc ranked 1st by BM25 and 4th by embeddings → 1/61 + 1/64 = 0.0320 doc ranked 3rd by both → 1/63 + 1/63 = 0.0317 // compounding: probability an n-step task succeeds P(success) = pn 0.9510 = 0.60 0.9910 = 0.90 0.9550 = 0.08

RAG beyond text. The same idea works for images (retrieve with multimodal embeddings such as CLIP) and for tables, where "retrieval" often means having the model write a SQL query (text-to-SQL), run it, and answer from the result.

Function calling. Modern model APIs let you declare tools with a name, description and parameter schema. The model replies with which tool to call and with what arguments; your code runs it and sends back the result. The description is effectively a prompt, so wording matters, and fewer, sharper tools are easier for a model to use than dozens of overlapping ones.

In plain words

RAG is an open-book exam: the model answers better when you hand it the right pages, so most of the work is building a good librarian. An agent is an intern with a phone and a credit card: hugely useful, but every extra step is another chance to slip, so you check the plan and keep a hand on the card.

Contemplate
  1. Think of the documents your team relies on. Where would BM25 do better than meaning-based search, and where worse?
  2. For an agent you'd trust, which actions would it take alone, which need approval, and which should it never be able to take?
  3. If an agent fails one time in ten on a long task, is the fix a better model, fewer steps, or better checks between steps?

Chapter 7 · Finetuning

When should you change the weights, and how do you afford it?

The short answer

Finetune when prompting and retrieval have stopped improving things and the gap is about form (format, style, a narrow skill, a smaller cheaper model) rather than facts. The obstacle is memory: training needs room for weights, gradients, optimiser state and activations. Parameter-efficient finetuning, above all LoRA and QLoRA, trains a tiny fraction of the parameters and makes it fit on one GPU.

The big ideas

  • Finetuning is transfer learning. Start from a model that already knows a lot and nudge it toward your task. Variants include continued pretraining on raw domain text, supervised finetuning on (instruction, response) pairs, and preference finetuning on comparisons.
  • Good reasons to finetune. A specific output format or style the model keeps missing; a narrow task where a small finetuned model can beat a big general one; bias you want to correct; and distillation, teaching a small model to imitate a big one so serving is cheaper and faster.
  • Good reasons not to. It can make the model worse at things you didn't train for; it needs data, expertise and compute up front; and you then own a model that must be re-done whenever a better base model appears.
  • Finetuning is for form, RAG is for facts. If the model is wrong because it lacks information, retrieve it. If it has the information but behaves wrongly, finetune. Many systems do both. A sensible order: prompt well, add examples, add retrieval, and only then finetune.
  • Memory is the bottleneck. Inference needs the weights (plus some overhead). Training a parameter also needs its gradient and, with the Adam optimiser, two more numbers per parameter, plus activations saved for backpropagation. Full finetuning of a mid-sized model needs a lot more memory than serving it.
  • Fewer bits per number. FP32 uses 4 bytes, FP16 and BF16 use 2, INT8 uses 1, INT4 half a byte. BF16 keeps FP32's range with less precision, which is why training likes it. Quantisation converts to fewer bits after training (post-training quantisation) or simulates it during training (quantisation-aware training).
  • LoRA: learn a small correction. Freeze the original weight matrix W and learn two thin matrices A and B whose product is a low-rank update. The model uses W + BA. Ranks of 4 to 64 usually suffice, and adapters are small files you can swap per customer or per task.
  • QLoRA: LoRA on a 4-bit base. Store the frozen base in a 4-bit format (NF4) and train LoRA adapters on top. It made finetuning a 65-billion-parameter model on a single 48 GB GPU practical.
  • Model merging. Combine separately finetuned models without more training: average their weights, add task vectors (the difference between a finetuned model and its base), interpolate smoothly (SLERP), prune conflicting changes first (TIES, DARE), or even stack layers from different models.
Going deeper

Worked example: memory for a 13B model, and the size of a LoRA

// inference: weights × bytes, plus ~20% overhead (activations, KV cache, buffers) 13×109 params × 2 bytes (FP16) = 26 GB → × 1.2 ≈ 31 GB // full finetuning with Adam, everything kept in 16-bit (a simplified count) weights 13B × 2 B = 26 GB gradients 13B × 2 B = 26 GB Adam states (m,v) 13B × 2 × 2 B = 52 GB = 104 GB + activations // more than one 80 GB GPU // LoRA on one 4096 × 4096 attention matrix, rank r = 8 full update 4096 × 4096 = 16,777,216 params LoRA update r × (4096 + 4096) = 65,536 params (0.39%) W' = W + (α / r) · B·A // B: 4096×r, A: r×4096, α scales the update

Choosing hyperparameters. Learning rates for finetuning are small, typically somewhere between 10−7 and 10−3; too high and the loss jumps around, too low and nothing moves. Larger batches give steadier updates; gradient accumulation fakes a large batch on a small GPU. A few epochs are usually enough for big datasets and more help small ones; watch the validation loss for overfitting. A prompt loss weight controls how much the model learns from the prompt tokens versus the response tokens; usually you want it to learn mostly from responses.

Serving adapters. You can merge a LoRA into the base weights (no extra latency, one model per adapter) or keep adapters separate and attach them per request (one base model serves hundreds of customers, at a small speed cost).

In plain words

Full finetuning is retraining a seasoned chef from scratch. LoRA is handing them a small card of house adjustments ("less salt, plate it this way") clipped to the original recipe book. QLoRA does the same with a pocket edition of the book, so it fits on a smaller kitchen shelf.

Contemplate
  1. For a problem you'd like to solve, is the model missing information or missing behaviour? What does your answer say about RAG versus finetuning?
  2. If a new base model arrives in six months that beats your finetuned one out of the box, what did the finetuning buy you?
  3. Why might a company prefer one base model with a hundred LoRA adapters over a hundred finetuned models?

Chapter 8 · Dataset engineering

What makes training data good, and can AI make more of it?

The short answer

As models become commodities, data becomes the differentiator. Good finetuning data is high-quality, covers the range of inputs you'll face, and is just big enough. You get it by curating, annotating, synthesising with AI (and verifying what you synthesise) and then processing: inspect, deduplicate, clean, filter and format.

The big ideas

  • Quality, coverage, quantity. In that order. A few hundred excellent, varied examples often beat tens of thousands of sloppy ones. Quality means relevant, aligned with what the task needs, consistent between annotators, correctly formatted, sufficiently unique and legally usable.
  • Coverage means the real distribution. Include the short and the long, the polite and the rude, the typos, the languages, the edge cases. If 30% of real users write in Spanish and none of your data does, the model will meet that 30% unprepared.
  • How much data? It depends on the method (LoRA needs less than full finetuning), task complexity and how good the base model already is. Start small with well-crafted examples, train, and plot performance against dataset size. If the curve is still climbing steeply, more data will pay; if it has flattened, it won't.
  • The data flywheel. Usage generates data, data improves the model, a better model attracts usage. Design the product so it collects useful signals from day one, with permission.
  • Synthetic data has real uses. Fill coverage gaps, multiply rare cases, protect privacy, or distil a big model into a small one. Techniques run from rule-based templates and simulations to having a strong model write instructions, answers, or both. A neat trick is reverse instruction: take real high-quality text and ask a model to write the question it answers.
  • Verify everything you synthesise. Run generated code against tests, use AI judges, apply heuristics, and spot-check by hand. Pipelines for code have generated problems, solutions, unit tests and fixes in a loop, keeping only the solutions that pass.
  • Synthetic data has limits. Quality control is hard; a model trained on imitations can learn the style without the substance; training repeatedly on model-generated data can cause model collapse, where rare patterns disappear; and the lineage of the data gets murky.
  • Processing is unglamorous and decisive. Look at your data (really look). Remove exact and near-duplicates, strip junk and personal data, filter by quality, and format to the model's chat template with prompt and response tokens clearly separated.
Going deeper

Near-duplicate detection with shingles and Jaccard

Exact deduplication (hash every example) misses copies that differ by a word. Near-duplicate detection breaks each text into overlapping word shingles and measures overlap with Jaccard similarity. At scale, MinHash with locality-sensitive hashing estimates Jaccard without comparing every pair.

// 2-word shingles A = "reset your password from the login page" {reset your, your password, password from, from the, the login, login page} B = "reset your password from the sign-in page" {reset your, your password, password from, from the, the sign-in, sign-in page} J(A,B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| = 4 / 8 = 0.50 // with 1-word shingles the same pair scores 6/8 = 0.75; choose shingle size and threshold together

Formatting is part of the data. For supervised finetuning each example becomes the model's chat template with a system, user and assistant turn. The loss is usually computed only on the assistant tokens, so the model learns to answer, not to repeat questions. Inconsistent templates between training and serving are a common, silent cause of poor results.

Annotation guidelines are a product spec. Writing them forces you to decide what a good response is, which is the same work as writing your evaluation rubric in chapter 4. Reuse one for the other.

In plain words

A model learns like an apprentice from worked examples. Ten thousand copies of the same easy job teach less than two hundred different, well-done ones. Synthetic examples are like practice problems written by a senior colleague: very useful, as long as someone checks the answer key.

Contemplate
  1. If you had to write 100 perfect examples of your task by hand, what would the hardest 10 be? Those are probably the ones your data is missing.
  2. What signal does your product (or one you use) already produce that could become training data, and who should consent to that?
  3. Where is the line between a model learning from another model and simply copying its mistakes?

Chapter 9 · Inference optimisation

How do you make a model faster and cheaper to run?

The short answer

Know what you're waiting on. Reading the prompt (prefill) is limited by raw compute; writing tokens one by one (decode) is limited by how fast weights and the KV cache stream from memory. Then optimise at three levels: the model (quantise, distil, decode speculatively), the attention mechanism (shrink and page the KV cache, fused kernels) and the service (batching, caching, parallelism).

The big ideas

  • Compute-bound versus memory-bound. Arithmetic intensity is the number of operations done per byte moved. Prefill processes all prompt tokens at once, so it does many sums per byte: compute-bound. Decode generates one token per step and must reread every weight to do so: memory-bandwidth-bound.
  • The latency metrics. Time to first token (TTFT) is mostly prefill. Time per output token (TPOT) is decode. Total latency ≈ TTFT + TPOT × output tokens. Throughput is tokens per second across all users, and goodput counts only requests that met your latency targets.
  • Utilisation. MFU (model FLOPs utilisation) compares achieved FLOP/s to the chip's peak; MBU (model bandwidth utilisation) does the same for memory bandwidth. They tell you how much of the hardware you are really using.
  • Accelerators. Judge a chip on compute (FLOP/s at each precision), memory size, memory bandwidth and power. Fast on-chip SRAM is tiny; high-bandwidth memory (HBM) holds the model; CPU memory is big and slow.
  • Model-level tricks. Quantisation shrinks every weight, which directly speeds up memory-bound decode. Distillation trains a smaller model. Speculative decoding lets a small draft model guess several tokens that the big model checks in one pass. Inference with reference copies spans straight from the input (useful for editing documents or code), and parallel decoding (Lookahead, Medusa) predicts several future tokens at once.
  • The KV cache. During decoding, each token's attention keys and values are stored so they aren't recomputed. It grows with batch size × sequence length × layers × model width and can exceed the model itself. Shrink it with multi-query or grouped-query attention (heads share keys and values), local windowed attention, cross-layer sharing, or quantising the cache; manage it with PagedAttention, which allocates it in small pages like an operating system.
  • Kernels and compilers. FlashAttention computes attention in tiles that stay in fast SRAM, avoiding slow trips to main memory. Compilers fuse operations and tune them to specific hardware.
  • Service-level tricks. Batching serves many requests in one pass; continuous batching lets a finished request leave and a new one join every step instead of waiting for the slowest. Splitting prefill and decode onto different machines stops them fighting. Prompt caching reuses work on repeated prefixes like system prompts. Parallelism (replicas, tensor, pipeline, context and sequence) spreads a model across chips.
Going deeper

Worked example: the KV cache and the speed limit of decoding

// KV cache size bytes = 2 × B × S × L × H × M // 2 = keys and values; B batch, S sequence, L layers, H model width, M bytes/number Llama-2-13B: L = 40, H = 5,120, FP16 (M = 2), B = 32, S = 2,048 = 2 × 32 × 2,048 × 40 × 5,120 × 2 ≈ 53.7 GB // twice the 26 GB of weights if it used grouped-query attention (8 KV heads for 40 query heads) → ÷5 ≈ 10.7 GB // decode is memory-bound: each step reads every weight once 7B model in FP16 = 14 GB; GPU bandwidth 3.35 TB/s best-case time per token ≈ 14 / 3,350 s = 4.2 ms → ≈ 240 tokens/s for one user in INT4 (3.5 GB) ≈ 1.0 ms // quantising helps decode almost linearly // speculative decoding: draft proposes γ tokens, each accepted with probability α expected tokens per big-model pass = (1 − αγ+1) / (1 − α) α = 0.8, γ = 4 → (1 − 0.85) / 0.2 = 3.36 tokens instead of 1

Why batching is nearly free for decode. If each step must read 14 GB of weights anyway, serving 32 users in that same read costs little extra time, because the bottleneck is the read, not the arithmetic. Batching raises throughput enormously until the work becomes compute-bound or the KV cache fills memory. That is why the KV cache, not the weights, often sets how many users a GPU can serve.

In plain words

A model writing an answer is a chef who must walk to the pantry and back for every single word. Quantising shrinks the pantry so the walk is shorter; batching cooks for many tables on each trip; the KV cache is the notes about each table's order, which can end up taking more shelf space than the recipes; and speculative decoding is a commis guessing the next few steps so the chef only has to nod.

Contemplate
  1. For a product you know, which matters more to users: time to first token or tokens per second? How would you design differently for each?
  2. Why does making a model's weights smaller help decoding much more than prefill?
  3. If the KV cache sets how many people one GPU can serve, what does that imply about the true cost of very long context windows?

Chapter 10 · AI engineering architecture and user feedback

How do the pieces fit into one system that keeps getting better?

The short answer

Start with the simplest thing, a query going to a model, and add components only as you need them: context, guardrails, a router and gateway, caches, then agent patterns and write actions. Wrap it all in monitoring and observability. Then close the loop with user feedback, the data that tells you what to fix next and eventually what to train on.

The big ideas

  • Step 1: enhance context. Retrieval, tools and other data sources give the model what it needs to answer. This is the biggest quality lever for most applications.
  • Step 2: add guardrails. Input guardrails stop private data leaking to an external API and catch malicious prompts. Output guardrails catch quality failures (malformed output, unsupported claims, plain bad answers) and security failures (toxic content, private data, unsafe code or tool calls, brand risk). On failure you can retry, fall back or hand off to a human. Every guardrail adds latency, especially on streamed output.
  • Step 3: router and gateway. A router classifies the request and sends it to the right model or pipeline: cheap model for easy questions, strong model for hard ones, a fixed reply for out-of-scope ones. A gateway gives one interface to many model providers, with access control, cost limits, logging and fallback when a provider is down.
  • Step 4: caches. An exact cache returns a stored answer for an identical request. A semantic cache returns it for a similar one, found by embedding similarity; it saves more but can serve a wrong answer if "similar" isn't similar enough.
  • Step 5: agent patterns. Loops, branching and tools that change the world. Each write action deserves its own permission design.
  • Monitoring versus observability. Monitoring tracks known metrics; observability means you can work out why something happened from logs and traces you kept. Watch quality signals (format failures, factual consistency, toxicity, refusal rate), length, latency and cost, plus engineering metrics like mean time to detect (MTTD), mean time to respond (MTTR) and change failure rate (CFR).
  • Drift comes from three places. Your own changes (a new system prompt), your users changing behaviour, and the model provider silently updating the model behind the same API name. Log versions of everything.
  • Conversations are full of feedback. Explicit signals (thumbs, ratings, "this is wrong") are rare. Implicit ones are everywhere: the user stops a response early, corrects it, rephrases the question, regenerates, copies the answer, or keeps chatting. Each hints at success or failure.
  • Design when and how to ask. Ask at natural moments, when something seems to have gone wrong, or when the model is unsure. Make it effortless: accept a suggestion with one key, choose one of two images. Ask too often and people stop answering.
  • Feedback has biases and loops. People are lenient, random, and biased toward the first or longest option. Worse, optimising for what users reward can create a degenerate feedback loop: a model that learns flattery gets more thumbs up and becomes more sycophantic.
Going deeper

Worked example: what a cache is worth

// average latency with a cache in front of the model E[latency] = lookup + (1 − h) × model model = 2,400 ms, lookup = 30 ms (embedding + vector search), hit rate h = 0.25 = 30 + 0.75 × 2,400 = 1,830 ms // 24% faster on average // cost saved per day 200,000 requests × 25% hits × $0.01 per model call = $500/day // minus the risk: at 2% wrong hits, 1,000 users a day get an answer meant for someone else's question

Orchestration. Tools like LangChain or LlamaIndex (and many others) chain components into pipelines. They speed up the start, but add abstraction you must debug through. Before adopting one, check that it supports the models, retrievers and evaluation you need, and that you can still see the exact prompts it sends.

The whole book in one loop. Plan the use case (ch. 1) → understand and choose a model (ch. 2, 4) → define evaluation (ch. 3, 4) → prompt (ch. 5) → add context and tools (ch. 6) → finetune if needed (ch. 7) with good data (ch. 8) → serve it efficiently (ch. 9) → monitor, collect feedback and go round again (ch. 10).

In plain words

An AI product is a restaurant, not a recipe. The model is the chef, but you also need a host who seats people at the right table (router), a food-safety inspector at the door and the pass (guardrails), a fridge of ready dishes (cache), a manager watching the room (monitoring), and you need to notice which plates come back half-eaten (feedback).

  1. Draw the simplest architecture that would work for your idea. Which of the five steps does it truly need on day one?
  2. What implicit signal in your product would most honestly say "this answer failed"?
  3. How would you notice if your users were slowly teaching your model to flatter them?
