Part I · the freedom goal

The Freedom Goal

Before any property, there's a question most people skip: freedom from what, and how much does it cost? These three sections set the target and the tools. Your freedom number turns a vague wish into a figure. Leverage explains why property, bought with mostly borrowed money, can grow wealth faster than savings alone, and why it cuts both ways. And the foundations, a budget, a savings habit, a buffer and clean credit, are what every later step stands on.

The idea behind this part

Freedom has a price you can calculate, and property is one way to pay it, but only if your own money habits can carry the debt.

Section 1 · Setting the target before the plan

What Freedom Costs

In short

Financial freedom is not a feeling or a net worth. It's the point where the income your assets pay you, after costs, covers what your life costs, so working becomes a choice. Put a number on that, and a property plan stops being a dream and becomes a sum you can work backwards from.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026Your freedom number got harder to reach, not easier to calculate

The method still works: start from what your life costs. But with inflation running above target and forecast to stay above 3% until late 2027, today's living costs are higher, and a debt-free property's rent is taxed as income. Build your number in today's dollars and add the tax on rent. Expect the date you reach it to sit further out than older examples suggest.

The big ideas

  • Start from spending, not income. Your freedom number comes from what your life costs a year, not what you earn. A household that lives well on $70,000 needs a very different plan from one that needs $150,000.
  • Net rent is the income that counts. A property's rent is not its income. After rates, insurance, management, repairs and empty weeks, a debt-free property might pay you two to four percent of its value each year.
  • Work backwards to a portfolio. Divide your yearly cost of living by the net rent one debt-free property pays, and you know roughly how much property you need to own outright. That number is usually smaller, and further away, than people guess.
  • Time is the biggest input. Property builds wealth slowly through growth and paying down debt. The earlier you start, the more of the work compounding does instead of you.
A worked example

Imagine Priya and Sam, both 34, spending $80,000 a year after tax on the life they want. A debt-free property worth $900,000 in their city rents for about $750 a week, or $39,000 a year. Take off council and water rates, insurance, a property manager, repairs and a few empty weeks, and roughly $28,000 a year is left, a little over 3% of the value. To cover $80,000 they'd need about three such properties with no debt on them, around $2.7 million of property in today's money. That sounds impossible from where they sit. But it's a target they can plan towards: the next sections are about how leverage, equity and time can get them there, and how to get there without being knocked over on the way.

Worth knowing

Living off rent is taxed: rent is income, so your freedom number should include the tax you'd pay on it. The labs here leave tax out to keep the picture simple. The figures in this example are illustrative, not a forecast.

Try it this week

Add up last year's actual spending from your bank statements, not a guess. Write down that figure and your freedom number next to it.

In plain words

Work out what your life costs each year. Then work out how much property you'd need to own outright for its rent, after costs, to pay that. That's your target.

Section 2 · Why property, and why it cuts both ways

The Power of Leverage

In short

Property is one of the few investments a bank will lend you most of the money for. With a 20% deposit you control the whole asset, so its growth lands on a much smaller stake of your own. That magnifies returns in good years and losses in bad ones, which is why the finance matters as much as the property.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026The lever got more expensive to hold

With the cash rate at 4.85%, investor loan rates of roughly 7% to 8% are typical, not the 6% this section's lab starts at. That makes the yearly shortfall on a highly geared property much bigger. And for an established property bought after 12 May 2026, that shortfall can no longer be deducted from your salary, so you carry it with after-tax dollars. Read the leverage example as the best case: set the lab's growth to 3% and remember the top-ups now cost you more.

The big ideas

  • You control the whole asset. Put $160,000 down on an $800,000 property and every dollar of growth on the full $800,000 is yours. Five percent growth is $40,000, a 25% gain on your deposit before costs.
  • Holding costs are the price of the lever. Interest, rates, insurance and repairs usually exceed the rent at first. That gap is what you pay each year to keep control of the asset while it grows.
  • The lever works in reverse. If prices fall 10%, a 20% deposit loses half its value on paper. That only hurts for real if you're forced to sell, which is why time and buffers matter so much.
  • Banks lend on houses, not on shares. Lenders will go to 80% or more against a home because it's stable collateral. Few people could borrow that much to buy shares, which is a big part of why property builds so much ordinary wealth.
A worked example

Take two neighbours with $160,000 each. One leaves it in a savings account earning 4% a year. The other uses it as a 20% deposit on an $800,000 house and rents it out. Suppose the house grows at 5% a year and the rent covers most, but not all, of the interest and costs: the shortfall starts around $16,000 a year and shrinks as rents rise. After ten years the saver has about $237,000. The investor's house is worth about $1.3 million against a $640,000 loan, so their equity is about $660,000, less the roughly $95,000 they topped up over the decade. Change growth to 0% and the story flips: the investor has paid about $160,000 in shortfalls for nothing. Leverage didn't create the return; it multiplied whatever the market did.

Worth knowing

Over the past few decades Australian dwelling values have grown at roughly six to seven percent a year on average in nominal terms, by CoreLogic's long-run indices, but never smoothly. Sydney fell about 15% between 2017 and 2019, and Perth and Darwin went backwards for most of the 2010s. Averages hide the decade you happen to buy in.

Try it this week

Before you look at any listing, write down how much of a shortfall you could top up each month for ten years without strain. That number, not the bank's maximum, is your real lever length.

In plain words

Borrowing most of the price lets your small deposit benefit from the growth of the whole property. That's powerful when prices rise and painful when they don't, so you need time and a buffer.

Section 3 · Budget, savings, buffer and credit

Foundations Before Bricks

In short

A property plan is only as strong as the habits under it. You need a deposit, a buffer for bad months, and a financial record a lender will trust. All three come from the same place: knowing where your money goes and keeping a gap between what you earn and what you spend.

The big ideas

  • Your savings rate sets your pace. The share of your income you keep decides how fast you reach a deposit, and lenders read it as proof you can carry a loan. Raising it from 10% to 25% can cut the wait by years.
  • Lenders read your statements. Banks look at three to six months of transactions. Regular buy-now-pay-later debts, gambling or overdrawn accounts all count against you, sometimes more than the amount suggests.
  • Credit limits count, even unused. A lender treats a credit card limit as if you could max it out, assessing a monthly repayment of around 3% to 3.8% of the limit. A $20,000 card you never use can cut tens of thousands from what you can borrow.
  • A buffer is part of the deposit. Have cash for the purchase costs, plus several months of repayments set aside. Buying with every dollar you have leaves nothing for the first broken hot water system.
A worked example

Consider Mei, who takes home $6,500 a month and spends about $5,600 without quite knowing where it goes. Saving $900 a month, her $90,000 target (a deposit plus purchase costs and a buffer) is more than seven years away. She tracks three months of spending, cancels four subscriptions, cooks more, moves her savings into a separate account that's paid on payday, and closes an old $15,000 credit card. Her spending drops to $4,500, so she saves $2,000 a month and reaches the target in under four years. The card she closed also lifts what a lender will let her borrow. Nothing about her income changed; the foundations did.

Try it this week

Open a separate account for your deposit and set an automatic transfer for payday. Then look up every credit card limit you hold and ask whether you need it.

In plain words

Spend less than you earn, save it automatically, keep some cash for emergencies and keep your credit clean. Everything else in the book is built on that.

Part II · the finance

The Finance

This is where the book's title starts: finance first. A lender decides how much you can borrow, on what terms, and therefore how far a property plan can go. These four sections look at property from the bank's side of the desk: how your income and spending are tested, what deposit and LVR mean, how a loan's structure changes what you pay, and how the equity growth creates can be borrowed against to buy again.

The idea behind this part

Your borrowing power is an asset in itself. Protect it, structure it well, and it decides how many steps your plan can take.

Section 4 · Serviceability and borrowing power

Through the Lender's Eyes

In short

How much you can borrow isn't a multiple of your salary. A lender works out your income after tax, subtracts your living costs and every other debt, then checks whether what's left could still pay the loan if the interest rate were three percentage points higher. That test, called serviceability, is the ceiling on every property plan.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026Borrowing power has shrunk, and there's a new ceiling

At a 7% to 8% loan rate, the 3% buffer means lenders test you at about 10% to 11%. In the lab, move the rate to 7.5% and watch borrowing power fall. Since 1 February 2026, APRA also limits each bank to 20% of new investor lending at six or more times income, so a second or third property can be refused even when the surplus looks fine. Treat older 'how much can I borrow' figures as too high.

The big ideas

  • The 3% buffer. Australia's banking regulator, APRA, expects lenders to test your repayments at a rate at least three percentage points above the actual loan rate. A loan at 6% is assessed as if it cost 9%.
  • Your spending is benchmarked. Lenders compare the expenses you declare with a benchmark (often the Household Expenditure Measure, or HEM) and use whichever is higher. Understating your spending won't help.
  • Every commitment counts. Car loans, HECS-HELP debt, buy-now-pay-later plans and credit card limits all reduce the surplus that has to cover the new loan.
  • Rent helps, but only partly. For an investment property, lenders usually count around 70% to 80% of the rent as income, allowing for vacancies and costs. Different lenders treat this differently, which is a big reason brokers compare them.
A worked example

Picture Arjun, a nurse earning $105,000 with a $10,000 credit card, a $450-a-month car loan and living costs of $3,200 a month. At a 6% loan rate the bank tests him at 9%. After tax and Medicare he takes home about $6,700 a month; take off living costs, the car loan and about $380 a month the bank assigns to the card, and roughly $2,690 is left. At 9% over 30 years, that surplus supports a loan of about $335,000. Close the card and finish the car loan, and the same income supports nearer $435,000. A broker's first job is often not finding a lender but tidying up the file before anyone applies.

Worth knowing

This lab is a teaching model, not a calculator. Real lenders use their own tax tables, expense benchmarks, shading of bonus and rental income and policy on debt-to-income ratios, and results vary by tens of thousands between them. Regulators also watch loans of six or more times income closely.

Try it this week

Make a list of every debt and limit in your name, including ones you've forgotten, and what each costs a month. Then ask a broker which ones are costing you the most borrowing power.

In plain words

A bank works out what you have left each month after tax, living costs and other debts, then checks you could still pay the loan if rates were three points higher. That decides how much you can borrow.

Section 5 · How much of the price is yours

Deposits, LVR and LMI

In short

The loan-to-value ratio, or LVR, is the share of the price the bank lends. At 80% or below, most lenders don't charge mortgage insurance; above it, you pay lenders mortgage insurance (LMI), a one-off fee that protects the bank, not you. Choosing your LVR is a trade-off between waiting to save more and getting into the market sooner.

The big ideas

  • LVR is the bank's share. Borrow $640,000 on an $800,000 property and the LVR is 80%. The higher it is, the riskier the loan looks to the lender, and the more it may cost you.
  • LMI protects the lender. If you borrow above 80%, you usually pay a one-off LMI premium, often added to the loan. It can run from a few thousand dollars at 85% to tens of thousands at 95%.
  • Paying LMI can be a fair trade. If saving the extra 10% would take three years while prices rise, paying LMI to buy sooner can come out ahead. It can also be a bad trade in a flat market. It's a calculation, not a rule.
  • Budget for more than the deposit. Stamp duty (set by each state, with concessions for many first home buyers), legal fees, inspections and loan costs can add around 4% to 6% of the price for an investor.
A worked example

Suppose Lena wants a $700,000 apartment and has $105,000 saved. A 20% deposit is $140,000 plus about $30,000 of stamp duty and costs, so she's about $65,000 short, about three years of saving. With a 10% deposit she can buy now: $70,000 down, a $630,000 loan at 90% LVR, and an LMI premium of perhaps $12,000 to $15,000 added to the loan, depending on the insurer. If prices in her area rise 5% a year while she saves, the same apartment would cost about $810,000 in three years. In that market paying LMI was cheaper than waiting. If prices were flat, waiting would have saved her the premium. The decision turns on the market and her time, not on a rule that LMI is always bad.

Worth knowing

For first home buyers, the federal First Home Guarantee lets eligible buyers purchase with as little as a 5% deposit without paying LMI, because the government guarantees part of the loan. It doesn't apply to investment properties, and eligibility rules change, so check the current terms with Housing Australia or a broker.

Try it this week

Get a stamp duty estimate from your state revenue office's calculator for the price you have in mind, and add it to your savings target.

In plain words

The LVR is how much of the price you borrow. Borrow more than 80% and you'll usually pay mortgage insurance. Sometimes that's worth it to buy sooner, sometimes it isn't.

Section 6 · Offsets, interest-only and keeping loans separate

Structuring the Loan

In short

Two people can borrow the same amount at the same rate and end up tens of thousands apart, purely because of how the loan is set up. Offset accounts, the choice between principal-and-interest and interest-only, fixed or variable rates, and keeping each property's loan separate all shape what you pay and how flexible you stay.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026Structure matters even more at higher rates

An offset is worth more when rates are higher: $60,000 offset against a 7.5% loan saves about $4,500 a year, against $3,600 at 6%. Interest-only periods ending in 2026 and 2027 are rolling onto much higher principal-and-interest repayments. And because losses on newer established purchases now only offset rental income, keeping each investment loan separate and well documented is more important for tax.

The big ideas

  • An offset is savings that cut interest. Money in an offset account is still yours, but the bank charges interest only on the loan minus that balance. $50,000 sitting there on a 6% loan saves about $3,000 a year.
  • Interest-only costs more over the life. Paying only interest keeps repayments low for a few years, which can help cash flow on an investment. But the debt doesn't shrink, and when the interest-only period ends, repayments jump because the full debt must be repaid over fewer years.
  • Keep personal and investment debt apart. Interest on a loan used to buy a rental is generally tax-deductible; interest on your own home isn't. Mixing the two in one loan or redrawing for personal spending can muddy the tax position.
  • Avoid tying properties together. When one loan is secured by two properties (cross-collateralisation), the bank controls both. Selling or refinancing one can force a revaluation of the other. Many brokers prefer separate loans with separate securities.
A worked example

Think of two couples, each borrowing $600,000 at 6% over 30 years. The Nguyens keep $60,000 of savings in an ordinary account. The Kellys keep the same $60,000 in an offset account and pay the same monthly repayment. Because less interest is charged each month, more of the Kellys' repayment goes to principal, and their loan is paid off about five years sooner, with roughly $230,000 less interest over the life of the loan, on the same income and the same savings. A third couple takes the loan interest-only for five years: their repayments start lower, but they pay about $45,000 more interest overall, because the debt doesn't start shrinking until year six.

Try it this week

Look at your loan statement: is it principal-and-interest or interest-only, when does any interest-only period end, and does it have an offset? If not, ask your lender or broker what one would cost.

In plain words

How your loan is set up matters as much as the rate. Keep savings in an offset, know what happens when an interest-only period ends, and keep each property's loan separate.

Section 7 · Borrowing against growth without selling

Unlocking Equity

In short

Equity is the part of a property's value that's yours: its value minus the loan. As the property grows and the loan shrinks, equity builds. Lenders will usually let you borrow against it, up to about 80% of the property's current value, and that borrowed equity can become the deposit for your next purchase.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026Equity is there, but using it is harder

Many owners still have equity from the 2020 to 2022 run-up. What has changed is the second half of the test: drawing that equity means a larger loan assessed at 10% or more, inside a debt-to-income cap. Read 'usable equity' as the most you could draw, not what a lender will approve today.

The big ideas

  • Usable equity is less than equity. If a property is worth $800,000 with a $480,000 loan, your equity is $320,000. But usable equity is 80% of the value ($640,000) minus the loan, so $160,000.
  • It's still debt. Drawing equity means a bigger loan, with repayments that must pass the same serviceability test. Equity without borrowing power is a number on a valuation, not money you can use.
  • A revaluation starts the process. Your lender (or a new one, if you refinance) orders a valuation. Bank valuations are often conservative, so a broker may compare lenders to find one that values the property fairly.
  • Equity plus costs is a deposit. To buy again you usually need around 25% of the next price: 20% deposit plus stamp duty and costs. $160,000 of usable equity could fund a purchase of about $640,000 without LMI.
A worked example

Consider Tom and Aisha, who bought their first property for $600,000 with a $480,000 loan. Six years later it's valued at $820,000, and their principal repayments have brought the loan down to about $440,000. Eighty percent of $820,000 is $656,000, so they can access up to about $215,000 of equity. They set it up as a separate loan split, used only for the next purchase, and because their incomes have risen they pass serviceability. The equity funds a 20% deposit and costs on a $850,000 property. They haven't sold anything or saved a new deposit; growth and repayments on the first property paid for the start of the second.

Try it this week

Look up a recent sale of a property like yours to get a rough value. Work out 80% of it, subtract your loan balance, and write down your usable equity.

In plain words

As your property grows and your loan shrinks, you build equity. Banks will lend against part of it, which can give you the deposit for the next property without selling the first.

Section 8 · Why land and demand do the growing

Location Before House

In short

A building wears out; the land under it is the part that tends to grow in value. That's why location, and how much land you get for your money, matters more than granite benchtops. Strong locations are places where more people will want to live than there will be homes for them, close to jobs, transport, schools and amenity.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026Tax now tilts towards new builds, against the land-first rule

The land-first principle is still how values tend to grow. But from 12 May 2026, new builds keep negative gearing against your salary and the choice of the old 50% CGT discount, while established homes don't. That makes some new builds look better after tax and established houses on land look worse in the first years. Weigh the tax gain against the lower land share, new-build premiums and builder risk. Check exactly what counts as a 'new' dwelling under the new rules before relying on it.

The big ideas

  • Land appreciates, buildings depreciate. A house is a structure that ages, needs repairs and eventually gets replaced. The land is scarce, especially near jobs and transport, so over long periods most of a property's growth comes from the land.
  • Land share differs by property type. A house on its own block is mostly land value. An apartment in a 200-unit tower owns a tiny slice of land, so more of its price is building, and growth often lags.
  • Demand drivers. Population growth, new jobs, infrastructure such as rail lines, good schools and lifestyle all lift demand. Look for things already funded and under construction, not just announced.
  • Supply is the other half. Areas with lots of land for new estates, or many new apartments approved, can keep prices flat even when demand grows. Low vacancy rates and long-term shortages point the other way.
A worked example

Picture two properties bought for the same $750,000 in the same city. One is a three-bedroom house on a 550 square metre block in an established suburb, where the land is worth about $520,000 of the price. The other is a new two-bedroom apartment in a large tower, where the land share is perhaps $100,000 and the rest is building, plus a premium for being new. Over the next decade the suburb's land grows strongly, while the building components of both properties age. The house tends to come out well ahead, because most of what its owner bought was the thing that grows. The apartment owner bought mostly the thing that wears out. This is a broad pattern, not a law: well-located apartments in tightly held areas can do well, and houses in oversupplied areas can stall.

Try it this week

For a suburb you're interested in, find the land size of three recent sales and work out price per square metre of land. Compare with a suburb next door.

In plain words

Buildings get older and lose value; land in good places gets scarcer and gains it. Buy as much well-located land as your budget sensibly allows.

Section 9 · Choosing what each property is for

Growth or Cash Flow

In short

Every investment property sits somewhere between two ends. Growth properties tend to rise in value faster but pay low rent relative to price, so they cost you money to hold. Yield properties pay more rent but tend to grow slowly. A plan needs growth to build wealth and enough cash flow to keep you able to hold and keep borrowing.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026Cash flow matters more now

Higher rates and the end of salary-offset negative gearing for established purchases both hurt growth properties that lose money each week. A yield property that roughly breaks even is easier to hold and helps serviceability. In the lab, the growth property's red bars are now larger and paid fully from after-tax income, so lean towards the yield side unless you have a strong reason not to.

The big ideas

  • Gross yield is rent over price. A $600,000 property renting at $550 a week has a gross yield of about 4.8%. City houses often sit around 3% to 4%; some regional houses and units can be 5% to 7%.
  • Growth builds equity, yield builds capacity. Growth creates the equity that funds your next deposit. Yield keeps your cash flow and your borrowing power healthy so you can actually use that equity.
  • High yield often means more risk. Very high yields can signal a town that depends on one employer or industry, a small market that's hard to sell into, or high vacancy. Ask why the rent is so high relative to price.
  • Most portfolios mix both. Many investors start with growth while incomes are high and add yield later as serviceability tightens, or balance the two from the start.
A worked example

Imagine two $600,000 purchases. Property A is a city house: it rents for 3.3% of its value and grows at 6% a year. Property B is a regional house: it rents for 6% and grows at 3%. In the first year A costs its owner about $16,000 to hold, while B roughly breaks even and moves into surplus as rents rise. After ten years, A is worth about $1.07 million and B about $806,000, so A's owner is well ahead on equity even after the cash they topped up. But B's owner had more spare cash each year and found it easier to pass serviceability for a second loan. Neither is the right answer on its own; the question is what you need next from the property you're buying.

Try it this week

For any property you're looking at, write down which job it's for: growth, cash flow, or both. If you can't say, you're buying on feel.

In plain words

Some properties grow fast but cost you money each week; others pay their way but grow slowly. Know which you're buying and why, and keep enough cash flow to keep going.

Section 10 · What a property really costs each week

Running the Numbers

In short

A listing tells you the price and, if you're lucky, the rent. What you need to know is what the property will cost you each week once interest, rates, insurance, management, repairs and empty weeks are paid, and what that looks like after tax. Do the sum before you fall in love with the kitchen.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026Negative gearing no longer works this way for most new purchases

This section describes the old rule. For an established property bought after budget night, 12 May 2026, a rental loss can only be deducted against rental income; it is carried forward, not used to reduce tax on your wages. In the lab, set your marginal tax rate to 0% to see what such a property really costs you each week while it runs at a loss. The old treatment still applies to properties you held before budget night and to qualifying new builds. Interest rates in the lab should also be nearer 7% to 8% today.

The big ideas

  • Net yield is the real one. Take the yearly costs off the rent before you divide by the price. A 4.5% gross yield can easily become 3% net.
  • List every cost. Council and water rates, strata levies for units, landlord and building insurance, property management (often around 6% to 8% of rent plus letting fees), land tax above your state's threshold, repairs, and a few weeks of vacancy.
  • Negative gearing softens, doesn't remove, a loss. In Australia, if a rental's costs exceed its income, the loss can generally be deducted from your other income, so the after-tax cost is lower. But you're still losing money each year in the hope growth makes up for it.
  • Depreciation is a paper deduction. Owners can usually claim 2.5% a year of the construction cost of the building, which reduces tax without costing cash. A quantity surveyor's depreciation schedule tells you how much.
A worked example

Take a $700,000 house renting at $620 a week, bought with a $560,000 loan at 6%. The rent is about $32,200 a year. Interest is $33,600. Rates and water about $2,800, insurance $1,800, management at 7% about $2,300, repairs $2,500 and two weeks of vacancy $1,240. The property costs its owner about $12,000 a year before tax, roughly $230 a week. If the owner pays a 30% marginal rate plus the Medicare levy, the deduction brings that down to about $155 a week. Whether that's a good deal depends on growth: at 5% the house gains $35,000 in its first year, but that gain is only on paper until you sell or borrow against it, while the $155 is real money every week.

Worth knowing

Negative gearing and the capital gains tax discount have been debated politically for years and have been changed or proposed for change before. Treat tax settings as something that can shift over a twenty-year plan, and get advice from an accountant on your own position.

Try it this week

For the next listing you look at, find the council rates and strata levies (they're often in the contract or available from the agent) and do this sum on paper before you inspect.

In plain words

Rent isn't profit. Take off interest and every holding cost, then the tax effect, and you'll know what the property costs you each week. Make sure you can carry that for years.

Section 11 · Due diligence and negotiation

Buying Well

In short

A sound strategy can still be sunk by one bad purchase. Before a contract becomes unconditional, check the building, the strata, the contract, the zoning and flood maps, and the recent sales that tell you what it's really worth. Negotiate from those numbers, and walk away when the checks say so.

The big ideas

  • Get pre-approval first. Knowing what a lender will approve, in writing, lets you bid with confidence and stops you falling for a property you can't finance.
  • Inspect what you can't see. A building and pest inspection costs a few hundred dollars and can find termites, structural cracks or water damage that cost tens of thousands. For units, read the strata minutes for defects, special levies and disputes.
  • Read the contract with a professional. A solicitor or conveyancer checks the title, easements, special conditions and the cooling-off rules for your state. Auctions usually have no cooling-off period, so checks must happen before the hammer falls.
  • Price comes from comparables. Recent sales of similar properties nearby tell you the fair range. Your top price should come from that range and your numbers, not from the asking price or the excitement of an auction.
A worked example

Picture Daniel, keen on a three-bedroom townhouse that seems a bargain at $40,000 under nearby sales. He has pre-approval and the rent numbers work, so he signs with a five-day cooling-off period and orders checks. The building report is fine, but the strata minutes show the owners' corporation has been discussing waterproofing defects for two years, with a quote of $1.2 million across 24 units and a special levy likely. That's about $50,000 for his unit, more than the discount. He asks for a price reduction to cover it; the seller refuses, so he withdraws during cooling-off, losing the small fee that applies in his state. The bargain wasn't one; the checks cost him a few hundred dollars and saved him tens of thousands.

Try it this week

Make your own due diligence checklist now, before you find a property: pre-approval, comparable sales, building and pest, strata records, contract review, flood and zoning maps, rental appraisal.

In plain words

Do your checks before you're locked in: the building, the strata, the contract, the maps and the comparable sales. Let the numbers set your price, and be ready to walk away.

The Freedom

The last part turns one property into a plan. Equity from the first becomes the deposit for the next, until your ability to repay, not your ambition, sets the ceiling. Then the focus moves from growing to protecting, because a portfolio held for twenty years will meet rate rises, empty months and bad luck. And finally, the end game: turning a large, indebted portfolio into a smaller debt-free one that pays for your life.

The idea behind this part

Growing a portfolio takes years of patience; keeping it takes buffers; and freedom usually means owning less, outright.

Section 12 · Equity, serviceability and the next step

Building the Portfolio

In short

A portfolio isn't bought all at once. Each property, once it has grown, supplies equity for the next deposit, while your income and the rents decide whether a lender will let you borrow again. Most portfolios stop growing not when equity runs out but when serviceability does, so the order and type of purchases matter.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026Expect the staircase to be shorter and slower

The portfolio lab caps debt at about six times income plus rent, which happens to match APRA's new high-DTI threshold; in practice many investors now hit it sooner because each loan is tested near 10% to 11%. Plan for fewer properties, bought further apart, with more of the deposit from savings than from recycled equity, and favour purchases that pay their own way.

The big ideas

  • Two engines, two brakes. Equity growth and savings supply deposits; income and rent supply borrowing power. Run short of either and the next purchase waits.
  • Serviceability is the usual ceiling. Each new loan is tested at the actual rate plus 3% across all your debts, while only part of each rent counts as income. Many investors hit this wall at two or three properties.
  • Order matters. Buying growth first while your income has room, then adding yield to support serviceability, is a common sequence. Buying several low-growth properties first can leave you with cash flow but no equity to move on.
  • Pace with care. Buying the next property the moment equity allows leaves no buffer. A steady pace with time to settle each purchase is slower on paper and safer in practice.
A worked example

Picture a couple, Rosa and Ben, earning $190,000 between them. They buy a $650,000 house, and three years later its growth funds the deposit on a second. Two years after that, the equity is there for a third, but the bank says no: across two loans tested at the higher rate, and with only 80% of the rents counted, their surplus has run out. A broker suggests a higher-yielding third property and a lender with a more favourable view of rental income, and a year later they qualify. The equity had arrived on time; it was the borrowing power that set the pace. They now plan each purchase around both numbers instead of just the equity.

Try it this week

Write down your next purchase and the two numbers it needs: usable equity or savings for the deposit, and a borrowing power that passes. Ask a broker to check the second before you chase the first.

In plain words

Each property's growth can fund the next deposit, but your income and rents decide whether a bank will lend again. Plan around both, and don't rush.

Section 13 · Buffers, rate rises and staying in the game

Protecting What You Build

In short

Over a twenty-year plan, interest rates will rise, tenants will leave, roofs will leak and incomes will wobble. Wealth in property comes to people who can keep holding through those moments rather than selling at the worst time. Buffers, insurance and loans you could still pay at higher rates are what let you hold.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026The storm in this section is happening again

The 2022 to 2023 rises described here took the cash rate to 4.35%. After cuts in 2025, the RBA raised it five more times in 2026, to 4.85% on 3 November 2026, and has not ruled out more in 2027. Run the lab's stress test with today's debt and a further 1% rise on top of current rates, and size your buffer for losses you can no longer deduct from your salary.

The big ideas

  • Buffers buy time. Cash in an offset, enough for several months of the portfolio's shortfall and a major repair, turns a crisis into an inconvenience.
  • Plan for rate rises. The 3% serviceability buffer exists because rates do move. Check that you could pay your loans at that rate from your own budget, not just on the bank's spreadsheet.
  • Insure the right things. Landlord insurance for rent default and damage, building insurance, and income protection and life cover for the people whose income carries the loans.
  • Diversify within property. Different cities or regions, property types and tenant markets reduce the chance that one local downturn or one employer leaving hits everything at once.
From recent history

Between May 2022 and November 2023 the Reserve Bank of Australia raised its cash rate from 0.10% to 4.35%, thirteen increases in eighteen months, the fastest rise in a generation. A typical variable mortgage rate went from around 3% to over 7%, so repayments on a $600,000 loan rose by roughly $1,400 a month. Many borrowers who had fixed at very low rates in 2020 and 2021 rolled off onto much higher repayments in 2023. Investors with cash buffers and room in their budgets held on; some without them had to sell into a softer market. The rate rises didn't decide who kept their properties. The buffers did.

Try it this week

Work out what your loans would cost each month at three percentage points above today's rate. If that number would break your budget, start a buffer before buying anything else.

In plain words

Bad things will happen to any portfolio over twenty years. Keep cash aside, insure properly and make sure you could pay your loans at higher rates, so you never have to sell at the worst moment.

Section 14 · Consolidating into income you can live on

From Portfolio to Freedom

In short

A large portfolio with a large debt pays little income: most of the rent goes to the bank. Freedom usually comes from consolidation, selling some properties, using the proceeds to pay off the loans on the rest, and living on the rent of what's left, now debt-free. The plan's final step is to own less, outright.

Read this in 2026 · as at 8 November 2026The exit is taxed differently from 1 July 2027

The 50% capital gains discount used in this section's example ends for gains made after 1 July 2027. Gains are then taxed only above inflation, but at a minimum rate of 30%. Gains built up before that date stay under the old rules, and new builds can choose either method. The lab's tax figure uses the old 50% discount, so treat it as a rough guide only, and get an accountant to model a consolidation sale that falls after mid-2027.

The big ideas

  • Debt-free rent is income. Once a property has no loan, most of its rent is yours after costs and tax. A few debt-free properties can pay more than many highly geared ones.
  • Selling has costs. Agent's fees, marketing and capital gains tax come off the sale price. In Australia, individuals who hold an investment for more than twelve months generally pay CGT on only half the gain, but on large gains that's still a big amount.
  • Sell the weakest, keep the best. Usually you sell the properties with the lowest yield or growth prospects, and keep the ones with strong rent and good land in demand.
  • Paying down is the other route. Instead of selling, some investors switch loans to principal-and-interest and use surplus income and offset balances to clear debt over the final decade. It's slower but avoids selling costs.
A worked example

Imagine Rosa and Ben twenty years on. They own four properties worth $5.2 million against $2.4 million of interest-only debt. The rent is substantial, but after interest and costs they clear only about $12,000 a year. They sell the two with the lowest yields, worth $2.4 million together. After agent's fees and capital gains tax (with the 50% discount), about $2.1 million is left, enough to clear most of the debt. The two remaining properties, worth $2.8 million, now carry about $270,000 of debt and net about $68,000 a year; once that debt is paid down from savings over the next few years, they'll clear about $84,000, right around their freedom number of $85,000. They own half as much property and earn more than twice the income from it.

Worth knowing

Capital gains tax depends on your cost base, how long you held, your income in the year you sell and current rules. The lab's tax figure is a rough illustration; get an accountant's estimate before planning a sale.

Try it this week

Write down which of your properties (or the ones you plan to buy) you'd keep forever, and which you'd sell to clear debt. That's your consolidation plan, years in advance.

In plain words

To live off property, you usually need to own some of it outright. That often means selling some properties to pay off the loans on the rest, then living on the rent.
