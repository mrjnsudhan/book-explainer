A chapter-by-chapter guide to Steven Pressfield's book on creative battle

The War of Art,
Explained

Steven Pressfield spent decades failing to finish things before he became a bestselling novelist. The War of Art is his short, punchy account of the enemy that stopped him, which he calls Resistance, and the habits that finally beat it. This guide follows the book's three parts in fifteen chapters, each with a lab where you can try the idea on your own work.

The hard part is not the work. It's sitting down to do it.

Book One · defining the enemy

Resistance

Pressfield starts by naming the thing that stops us. It is not laziness or a lack of talent. It is a force, as steady as gravity, that pushes back whenever we try to move toward something that matters. Book One describes how it behaves and the many costumes it wears.

The idea behind this part

You can't fight an enemy you can't see. Once you recognise Resistance as a force rather than a flaw in your character, you can stop blaming yourself and start beating it.

Book One · What stands in the way

The Unlived Life

In short

Most of us live one life and carry another inside, unlived. The force that keeps them apart is Resistance. It shows up whenever you try to do something that trades a pleasure now for growth later.

The big ideas

  • The battle is sitting down. Pressfield's working day is ordinary: get up, have breakfast, do the chores, then start. What he finds hard is not the writing itself. It is the moment of sitting down to begin.
  • Two lives. Anyone who owns a guitar they never play, a novel they keep meaning to start, or a business plan in a drawer knows the gap between the life they live and the one waiting inside them.
  • The enemy has a name. Pressfield calls the pull away from that unlived life Resistance. Naming it matters. It turns "something is wrong with me" into "something is pushing against me", which you can fight.
  • What draws it out. Creative work of any kind, starting a business, a diet or fitness plan, study, kicking a bad habit, a stand on principle, committing to a person, helping others. All of them give up comfort now for something better later.
  • What doesn't. Anything that gives you a quick reward and asks nothing of you slides past without a fight. Resistance has no reason to stop you from staying where you are.
From the book

Before he starts work, Pressfield reads aloud the invocation to the Muse that opens Homer's Odyssey, and he keeps a few small good-luck objects close by. Then he works his session through. When he stops, he says, he usually can't tell whether the day's pages are any good. What he knows is that he showed up and fought, and that is what counts.

In plain words

If there is something you have wanted to do for years and somehow never start, you are not broken. You are meeting a force that every serious person meets. The first step is to see it for what it is.

Book One · Know your enemy

How Resistance Behaves

In short

Resistance is invisible, comes from inside you, never gets tired and is not personal. Its most useful trait is that it never misses: the more a piece of work matters to who you want to become, the harder it pushes. That makes it a compass.

The big ideas

  • Internal. It feels like it comes from outside: the boss, the kids, the bills. In fact it starts in you, which is good news, because that's where you can deal with it.
  • Insidious. It will say anything to keep you from the work. It bargains, flatters, threatens and lies, and it always sounds like a reasonable version of you.
  • Impersonal and universal. It isn't out to get you in particular. Like the weather, it hits everyone who tries to create, the famous and successful included, every single day. It never sleeps.
  • Infallible, so use it as a compass. The project you most dread starting is usually the one you most need to do. Pressfield's rule: the more important the call, the more Resistance you will feel.
  • Strongest at the finish line. Resistance surges again just before you're done, which is why so many projects stall when they are nearly complete.
  • Only pushes one way. It opposes moving up, never sliding down. Nothing tries to stop you from giving up.
From the book

Pressfield points out that as a young man Adolf Hitler wanted to be a painter and was turned down by the art academy in Vienna. His deliberately provocative point: it may have been easier for Hitler to start a world war than to face a blank canvas each morning. Resistance, he says, plays for keeps, and an unlived calling doesn't just disappear. It can turn into something much worse.

In plain words

The voice that says "not today" is not wisdom. It's a predictable force, and it is loudest exactly where it matters most. Instead of obeying the dread, read it like a map: the thing you're most scared to start is probably the thing to do first.

Next: Resistance in disguise →
Book One · The costumes it wears

Resistance in Disguise

In short

Resistance almost never says "don't do your work". It shows up in costume: putting things off until tomorrow, a quick hit of pleasure, a crisis you half created, something to numb the feeling, or the role of the victim.

The big ideas

  • Procrastination is the favourite. Nobody tells themselves "I will never write my book." We say "I'll start on Monday." It is the easiest disguise to excuse, and Monday keeps moving.
  • Cheap pleasures. The moment we face hard work, an easy reward calls out: food, sex, a screen. Pressfield's point is not that pleasure is wrong, it's that the timing gives it away.
  • Trouble and drama. Picking fights, letting things fall apart or turning life into a soap opera gets you attention without the work of earning it.
  • Self-medication. Drink, drugs, shopping, endless snacks: ways to dull a restlessness that is really a signal you're not doing your work.
  • Victimhood. Being ill, wronged or unlucky can become a way of life that excuses you from creating, and makes other people pay attention to you instead.
From the book

Pressfield is candid about his own years in disguise. Through his twenties and thirties he drifted: odd jobs, moving from place to place, and a novel he got almost to the end of before walking away from it, along with much else in his life. Only later did he see that the drama and the drifting had been Resistance all along.

In plain words

Next time you find yourself suddenly busy, suddenly hungry, suddenly in the middle of a crisis or suddenly too unlucky to work, ask one question: did this show up right when I was about to start something that matters? If so, you have probably caught Resistance in costume.

Next: Fear is a signpost →
Book One · Reading your own fear

Fear Is a Signpost

In short

The more you love a project, the more Resistance you will feel toward it, and the more scared you will be. Fear and self-doubt are not reasons to stop. They are signs that the work matters to you.

The big ideas

  • Resistance tracks love. Massive Resistance means there is massive love behind it. If you didn't care, it wouldn't bother to stop you.
  • Fear points the way. Paralysed by fear of a project? Pressfield says that's good: the more scared you are of a piece of work, the surer you can be that you need to do it.
  • Self-doubt can be an ally. Doubt shows you care about doing it well. The ones who are completely sure they're geniuses are often the fakes. The real innovators tend to be terrified.
  • The fear of success. What we often fear most is not failing but succeeding: finding out we really do have the power we suspect, and having to leave our old, comfortable self and its friends behind.
  • "I'm all alone." Feeling isolated is one of Resistance's lines. The work can be lonely, but Pressfield argues we're never really alone with it, and loneliness is a cost of the job, not a reason to quit.
From the book

Pressfield notes that when the call to create first gets serious, it often feels less like joy and more like dread. Answering it means stepping away from the people who knew you as you were. That, he suggests, is why so many of us stay just short of the thing we most want to do: not because we doubt we can, but because part of us fears we can.

In plain words

Feeling scared of your own project doesn't mean you're the wrong person for it. It usually means it's the right project. Expect the fear, name it, and do a little of the work anyway.

Next: Criticism, support and healing →
Book One · Resistance in other people, and in self-help

Criticism, Support and Healing

In short

When people around us start to change, Resistance makes us want to knock them down, and makes them want to pull us back. It also hides in therapy, workshops and the idea that we need to "heal" first. The work itself is the cure.

The big ideas

  • Criticism is a mirror. If you catch yourself sneering at someone, you are probably acting out of Resistance. Seeing another person go after their unlived life stings because it reminds us of ours.
  • The people close to you may push back. When you start to change, friends and family can feel judged, even if you never say a word. Some will try, kindly or not, to keep you as you were.
  • "Heal first" is a trap. Resistance loves the idea that you must sort yourself out before you can begin. Pressfield argues the part of you that creates doesn't need fixing first.
  • Support is not the work. Groups, courses and pep talks can feel like progress and can even help. But they can't sit down for you, and they easily become a comfortable place to avoid doing so.
From the book

Pressfield is blunt about the self-improvement world. You can spend years in seminars, support groups and therapy getting ready, and never write a word. He doesn't say these things are worthless. He says that when they replace the work, they are Resistance with a friendly face, and that the deepest healing comes from doing what you were meant to do.

In plain words

Two practical lessons. First, when you feel the urge to mock someone for going after something, turn the question around. Second, when people push back on your new habit, don't take it as proof you are wrong. They are meeting their own Resistance.

Next: Rationalization →
Book One · The spin doctor

Rationalization

In short

Rationalization is Resistance's spin doctor. It hands us excuses that are often perfectly true: we're tired, busy, broke, needed at home. What makes them Resistance is that they're offered as reasons not to work today. And Resistance can be beaten.

The big ideas

  • True, and beside the point. Your reasons may be completely legitimate. The question is not "Is it true?" but "Does it actually stop me doing some of the work today?"
  • It speaks in your voice. Resistance's frontal attack is fear. Rationalization is the backup: it doesn't sound like an enemy, it sounds like the sensible grown-up in your head.
  • Others did it anyway. Pressfield's example is Tolstoy, who had thirteen children and still wrote War and Peace. Big obligations are real, and people work in the gaps around them all the time.
  • Resistance can be beaten. If it couldn't, there would be no symphonies, no great novels, no great bridges. Every finished work is proof that someone sat down anyway.
From the book

Pressfield closes Book One on a hopeful note. After pages describing how cunning the enemy is, he reminds us that it loses all the time. Every symphony, cathedral and novel that exists was made by someone who felt the same pull to stop, and who, on some ordinary morning, sat down and did the work.

In plain words

Stop arguing with your excuses about whether they're true. Most are. Instead, shrink the job until the excuse no longer covers it: fifteen minutes, one page, one sketch. Then do that.

Next: Turning pro →
Book Two · combating Resistance

Turning Pro

The answer to Resistance, Pressfield says, is not more talent or more inspiration. It's a change of attitude: stop working as an amateur and start working as a professional. Book Two lists what that means in practice.

The idea behind this part

Amateurs wait to feel like it. Professionals show up anyway. Resistance can beat a mood, but it struggles against a habit.

Book Two · Professionals and amateurs

Turning Pro

In short

The amateur works for love, when the mood strikes. The professional works for keeps, every day. Pressfield's best news: you already know how to be a pro, because you are one at your day job.

The big ideas

  • Both love it. The difference between amateur and pro isn't how much they care. It's that the amateur's commitment is part time, so on the days the feeling isn't there, Resistance wins.
  • You're already a pro somewhere. At work you show up every day, stay all day, keep going when you're sick of it and accept being judged on results. Pressfield asks: why not bring that to your calling?
  • What a pro does. Shows up every day, no matter what. Stays on the job all day. Is in it for the long haul. Has real stakes. Accepts being paid. Doesn't over-identify with the job. Masters technique. Keeps a sense of humour. Takes praise or blame from the real world.
  • It's a decision. Turning pro isn't a credential or a talent. It's a choice to treat your work as your job, and you can make it today.
From the book

After years of false starts, Pressfield finally finished a novel. He went to tell his mentor, the filmmaker Tom Laughlin, expecting celebration. Laughlin congratulated him and then told him to start the next one, that same day. The novel never sold. The habit of starting the next one is what made him a writer.

In plain words

You don't need to become a new kind of person. You need to transfer a set of habits you already have, turning up, staying, being judged, from the job you are paid for to the work you care about most.

Book Two · Showing up on schedule

Inspiration Keeps Office Hours

In short

Amateurs wait to be inspired. Pros sit down at a set time and let inspiration find them already working. Pressfield argues that showing up every day is what brings the Muse.

The big ideas

  • Nine o'clock sharp. Pressfield loves the novelist Somerset Maugham's joke that he wrote only when inspiration struck, and luckily it struck every morning at nine.
  • Routine summons help. When you sit down every day, something gathers around the work. Ideas arrive and connections form, as if help turns up for people who turn up.
  • Mood is not a schedule. If you wait until you feel like it, you'll mostly be waiting. Feelings follow action more often than the other way round.
  • Good days need ordinary days. Most sessions produce ordinary work. The great ones only happen because you were already there on the ordinary ones.
From the book

Pressfield works the same hours each day, whether he feels like it or not. He compares the writer to a soldier or a farmer: the job doesn't ask how you feel this morning. He says that when we commit to the routine, power seems to gather around us, as if the Muse takes note of who keeps their appointments.

In plain words

Pick a time, put it in the calendar like a meeting with your boss, and keep it. Don't judge each day's output. Judge whether you showed up.

Book Two · The long haul

Patience, Order and Craft

In short

A pro takes the long view. They pace themselves for a marathon, keep their workspace and schedule in order, see the work as a craft rather than magic, and act even while they are afraid.

The big ideas

  • Patient. Resistance loves a burst of enthusiasm that burns out in a week. The pro plans for the length of the race, not the excitement of the start.
  • Seeks order. A cluttered desk and a chaotic schedule give Resistance places to hide. The pro clears both so nothing stands between them and the work.
  • Demystifies. To a pro, the work is a craft with techniques that can be learned and practised, not a mysterious gift that might or might not show up today.
  • Acts in the face of fear. The pro isn't fearless. Nobody is. They know fear can't be beaten for good, so they act alongside it.
From the book

At the start of a project Pressfield reminds himself that it's the Iditarod, the long sled-dog race across Alaska, not a sprint. He knows a book will take many months and that he'll have bad weeks. So he sets a pace he can keep and saves his energy for the distance.

In plain words

Choose a pace you could keep for a year, not one that would impress people for a week. Tidy the space, learn the craft, and expect to be nervous. None of that is a reason to stop.

Next: Play it as it lays →
Book Two · No excuses

Play It as It Lays

In short

Conditions are never perfect. The pro accepts the day they're given, is prepared for setbacks, doesn't show off, keeps studying the craft and asks for help when they need it.

The big ideas

  • Accepts no excuses. Being tired, ill or rejected is real. The pro does what they can with it and doesn't let it become a day off.
  • Plays it as it lays. A golfer can't move the ball to a nicer spot. You work with the conditions you've actually got.
  • Is prepared. Setbacks will come. The pro expects them, so a bad day is a bump, not a disaster.
  • Doesn't show off. Style serves the work. The pro isn't trying to make every page prove how clever they are.
  • Masters technique and asks for help. The pro keeps studying for life, and has no ego about going to a teacher or a better practitioner.
From the book

Golf runs through Pressfield's thinking; his breakthrough novel, The Legend of Bagger Vance, is about the game. In golf you play the ball where it lands, in the rough or the sand. The pro doesn't waste energy on how unfair the lie is. They choose the best shot from where they are.

In plain words

There will never be a day with no reason to skip. So stop waiting for one. Shrink the session if you have to, but keep the chain of days unbroken.

Next: Don't take it personally →
Book Two · Rejection and praise

Don't Take It Personally

In short

The pro cares deeply about the work but doesn't confuse it with their self. Rejection and praise land on the work, not on who they are. They validate themselves, know their limits and reinvent themselves when they need to.

The big ideas

  • Distance from the instrument. The pro treats their talent, voice and body as tools to look after and use, not as the whole of who they are.
  • Neither failure nor success is personal. A rejection is information about the work or the market, not a verdict on your worth. Praise isn't either.
  • Endures adversity. The pro expects to take hits, from critics, markets and plain bad luck, and keeps going.
  • Self-validates. The pro doesn't wait for anyone's permission to call themselves a writer, painter or founder.
  • Knows their limits, and reinvents. They get an agent, an accountant, a lawyer when needed. And when the market changes, they change with it.
From the book

In Hollywood, Pressfield co-wrote the film King Kong Lives (1986). Everyone involved was sure it would be a hit. It flopped badly, and he was crushed. Then something shifted. He had finally had a real failure, in the real arena, with real money at stake. He realised that made him a professional.

In plain words

Pour yourself into the work, then let it go out into the world as a thing you made, not as a piece of you on trial. That's what lets you take the hundredth rejection and still start the next one.

Next: The Muse →
Book Three · beyond Resistance

The Higher Realm

In the last part Pressfield turns from tactics to belief. If Resistance pushes us away from our calling, he argues, other forces pull us toward it. Call them the Muse, angels, the unconscious or the Self. Book Three is about how to work with them.

The idea behind this part

The work comes from somewhere deeper than our everyday, anxious mind. Do it for its own sake, and it will sustain you whatever the world says.

Book Three · Angels in the abstract

The Muse and the Magic of Beginning

In short

Pressfield believes that when you commit to your work, unseen forces come to help. You can call them the Muse or the unconscious. The way to call them is to start, and to keep showing up in the same way.

The big ideas

  • Forces that pull up. Just as Resistance pushes us down, Pressfield believes in forces that pull us toward our calling. The name matters less than the idea.
  • The magic of a start. He quotes the mountaineer W. H. Murray: the moment you truly commit, all sorts of unexpected help seems to follow, help no one could have planned.
  • Ideas arrive while you work. Answers pop up in the shower or on a walk, but only once you've been doing the work, as if part of your mind kept going without you.
  • Make a ritual. Same time, same place, a few words to begin, a small object for luck. A ritual tells you, and whatever is listening, that you're serious.
From the book

Murray wrote about commitment in his account of a Scottish expedition to the Himalayas, published in 1951. Until you commit, he says, there is hesitancy; once you do, events seem to line up behind you. He ends with a couplet he credits to Goethe, about boldness having genius, power and magic in it. Pressfield adopts the passage as his own creed.

In plain words

You don't have to believe in a literal Muse. You only have to notice that when you show up the same way every day, the work starts to meet you halfway. A ritual makes the first step automatic, so Resistance has less to grab onto.

Next: Ego and Self →
Book Three · A map of the mind

Ego and Self

In short

Borrowing from Carl Jung, Pressfield splits the mind in two. The Ego is the small, practical part that handles daily life and fears change. The Self is the larger whole, including dreams and the unconscious, where the work comes from. Resistance is the Ego defending its turf.

The big ideas

  • The Ego. It believes death is final, time is short, everyone is separate and the point of life is to get ahead and stay safe. It's useful for running a life, and it hates losing control.
  • The Self. It holds the parts of us the Ego can't see: dreams, intuition, the urge to make things. It believes in connection, in love and in work that matters beyond us.
  • Why creating feels risky. When we create, we open a door to the Self. The Ego feels its grip loosening and fights back. That fight is what we feel as Resistance.
  • Work is a spiritual act. Pressfield sees the artist as serving something larger. That's why the work feels frightening, and also why it can sustain you.
Worth knowing

Pressfield says plainly that this is a metaphor, not science, and that you can use whichever language suits you: the Muse, God, the unconscious. The useful part is the idea that the anxious, scorekeeping voice in your head is not the one that does your best work.

In plain words

When a thought is about what people will think, whether it's worth it or how it'll look, that's usually the Ego. When it's curious, playful or just wants to see what happens, that's the Self. Work from the second voice.

Next: Hierarchy and territory →
Book Three · Who you work for

Hierarchy and Territory

In short

Animals and people orient themselves in one of two ways: by their rank in a pecking order, or by their territory. Artists who work for rank and approval are at the mercy of others. Artists who work for their territory, the work itself, are fed by it.

The big ideas

  • Hierarchy. In a pecking order you compete with everyone, judge yourself by your rank, act to make an impression, and let other people's opinions define you. For an artist that's exhausting, and it's a trap.
  • Territory. Your territory is the place or practice that feeds you. Pressfield's examples: Arnold Schwarzenegger's territory was the gym; Stevie Wonder's is the piano.
  • How territory works. It sustains you without anyone else's input. It can only be claimed alone, and only by working. And it gives back exactly what you put in.
  • The test. Ask: if I were the last person on Earth, would I still do this? If the answer is yes, it's your territory.
  • The hack works for the hierarchy. Guessing what will sell or impress puts you back in the pecking order. The artist works from the territory and lets the audience come.
From the book

Pressfield asks how a hierarchy-minded artist reads a review: their whole sense of worth moves with it. The territorial artist reads the same review, takes what's useful, and goes back to the desk, because the desk is where their satisfaction comes from. They get the same news. Only one of them gets knocked over by it.

In plain words

Find the thing you'd do even if nobody ever saw it, and get your satisfaction from doing it, not from how it's received. Approval is nice when it comes. Don't hand it the steering wheel.

Book Three · Work for its own sake

The Artist's Life

In short

Pressfield ends with a call. Do the work for its own sake: as the Bhagavad Gita puts it, we have a right to our labour, but not to its fruits. And your work isn't only for you. It's a gift the rest of us need.

The big ideas

  • Labour, not fruits. You control the work: showing up, the hours, the honesty of it. You don't control the results: sales, reviews, being chosen. Put your heart into the first and let go of the second.
  • Serve the work. The pro isn't the star of the show. They report for duty, do their part and step aside.
  • You may have been put here for this. If you were born to make something and you don't, Pressfield says, you don't only hurt yourself. You rob everyone who'd have benefited.
  • Give us what you've got. Creative work isn't selfish or a bid for attention. It's a gift to the world. His last words are an appeal not to hold it back.
From the book

In the Bhagavad Gita, the warrior Arjuna loses his nerve on the eve of battle. His charioteer, the god Krishna, tells him to do his duty and fight, without clinging to the outcome. Pressfield reads the scene as a picture of every artist on every morning: frozen in front of the work, needing to act without a guarantee.

In plain words

Measure your days by the part you control. Did you show up? Did you do honest work? That's the whole job. Whatever comes of it is a bonus, and it isn't yours to demand.
