Cues,
Explained

Before anyone weighs your words, they have already read your face, your hands, your voice and even your profile picture. Vanessa Van Edwards runs Science of People, a human behaviour research lab, and Cues is her field guide to those signals: more than forty of them, sorted by whether they make you look warm, competent, or both. This guide takes the introduction, all ten chapters and the conclusion one at a time, with a lab in each where you can try the cue for yourself.

The book in one line

Charisma isn't a gift you're born with. It's a mix of warmth and competence cues, and every one of them can be learned.

Part one · the code

Charisma

Van Edwards starts with a founder who lost a room before he finished his pitch, then gives the formula behind every first impression. People are asking two silent questions about you, and they answer them from cues long before they weigh your words.

The idea behind this part

Charisma is warmth plus competence, and both are sent and read through cues that you can learn to see and to use.

Introduction · Why good ideas still lose

The Signals That Are Secretly Shaping You

In short

A cue is any signal that changes how people see you. We send them all the time through our bodies, voices, words and images, and other people read them before they have heard a word of our argument.

The big ideas

  • A good idea isn't enough. Van Edwards opens with a founder who walked into a TV investor show with a product that would later be worth a fortune, and still walked out with nothing. The problem wasn't the idea. It was the signals he sent while presenting it.
  • Four channels. The book sorts cues into four kinds: nonverbal (face, hands, posture, space), vocal (pitch, pace, volume), verbal (the words you choose) and imagery (photos, colours, clothes and objects).
  • Fast and mostly unconscious. We read cues in a flash and act on them without noticing. That's why someone can leave a meeting liking or doubting you and be unable to say why.
  • Cue literacy. The fix is to become literate in cues: learn to read them in others, notice the ones you send by accident, and choose the ones that match what you mean.
From the book

In 2013, Jamie Siminoff pitched his video doorbell company, then called Doorbot, on the TV show Shark Tank. By the book's reading, his cues worked against him from the first second: his greeting rose at the end like a question, as if he wasn't sure who he was, and when he named his price one shoulder lifted in a little shrug. The investors read doubt, and he left without the deal he came for. The product survived anyway. Renamed Ring, it was bought by Amazon in 2018 for around a billion dollars, and Siminoff later came back to the show as a guest shark.

Try it this week

Record yourself on your next video call or practice talk, then watch it back with the sound off. Write down three cues you send without meaning to.

In plain words

People respond to how you come across as much as to what you say. Learning to read and choose your cues is a skill, not a personality trait, and it starts with noticing the ones you already send.

Chapter 1 · Warmth plus competence

Cue for Charisma

In short

Charisma is not mystery or magnetism. It is the combination of two traits: warmth, which answers "Can I trust you?", and competence, which answers "Can I rely on you?". The most charismatic people send cues for both.

The big ideas

  • Two questions. When we meet someone, we quietly ask whether they mean us well (warmth) and whether they can act on it (competence). By the book's account, these two judgements make up more than 82 percent of our overall impressions.
  • Too warm. All warmth and no competence reads as likable but a pushover: the people-pleaser who is liked, interrupted and passed over.
  • Too competent. All competence and no warmth reads as cold, intimidating or even suspicious: respected, perhaps, but not trusted or followed.
  • Balance on purpose. Most of us lean one way by habit. Charisma comes from noticing your lean and deliberately adding the cues of the other side, not from changing who you are.
From the book

Van Edwards points out how often famous partnerships pair the two traits: one partner is the warm face people love, the other the capable mind people rely on. Teams and couples do it naturally because each half covers the other's gap. Her argument is that you don't need a partner to fill yours. You can learn to send the missing set of cues yourself, and the people who seem effortlessly charismatic are usually doing exactly that.

Try it this week

Think of the last time someone underestimated you or kept their distance. Was it warmth they doubted, or competence? Pick one cue from the other side to add next time.

In plain words

Charisma has two halves. If people like you but don't take you seriously, add competence cues. If they respect you but keep their distance, add warmth cues. If you're aiming for both, the book's charisma cues do double duty.

Chapter 2 · The cue cycle

How Cues Work

In short

Cues move in a loop. We decode the cues others send, we internalise them so they change how we feel, and then we encode new cues of our own, which the next person decodes. That makes cues contagious.

The big ideas

  • Decode. Your brain reads other people's cues constantly, mostly before you are aware of it: a frown, a flat voice, a short reply.
  • Internalise. Those cues don't stay outside you. Seeing tension tends to make you tense; seeing someone relaxed tends to relax you.
  • Encode. Then you send cues of your own, often copies of the ones you just took in, and the next person decodes them. The loop keeps turning.
  • Cues are contagious. Because of the loop, one person's mood can move a whole room. That cuts both ways: a stressed leader spreads stress, and a warm one spreads warmth.
From the book

The book's simplest example is a joke that lands badly. You tell it, a friend frowns, and before you've thought anything at all you feel a small drop in your stomach and your own face tightens. Your friend reads your tension, decides something is off, and the next minute of conversation is a little stiffer for both of you. Nobody chose any of it. Van Edwards's point is that once you can see the cycle, you can step into it, and choose the cue you send next instead of passing the last one along.

Try it this week

Before your next meeting or call, decide on one cue you'll hold for the first minute, such as a real smile or a calm, slow voice. Watch whether others pick it up.

In plain words

You catch moods from the people around you, and they catch yours. Since you can't control their cues, the useful move is to notice yours and send the one you'd like the room to copy.

Part two · nonverbal cues

Body Language

Four chapters on the body: the cues that make leaders look both warm and capable, the cues that make people like you, the cues that make them take you seriously, and the danger-zone cues that quietly break trust.

The idea behind this part

Your body is talking all the time. The goal is to make sure it says what you mean, and nothing you don't.

Chapter 3 · Cues that signal both warmth and competence

The Body Language of Leaders

In short

A few cues say "I'm with you" and "I'm capable" at once. Lean in, face people fully, stay open, use space wisely and hold eye contact with purpose.

The big ideas

  • Lean like a leader. A small lean towards someone shows interest and agreement. Leaning back can read as doubt, boredom or disapproval.
  • Open body, open mind. Crossed arms, a laptop held like a shield or a bag on your lap put a barrier between you and others, and people tend to close up in return. Clear the space between you.
  • Front forward. Point your three Ts, toes, torso and top (your head), at the person you're talking to. Facing someone fully tells them they have your attention.
  • Smart with space. There are rough zones of distance: intimate (up to about 18 inches), personal (about 1.5 to 4 feet), social (about 4 to 7 feet) and public (beyond that). Stepping into a closer zone too fast feels pushy; staying too far feels cold. On camera, sit around two feet from the lens.
  • Engage with gaze. Eye contact builds connection. The book suggests aiming for roughly 60 to 70 percent of a conversation: enough to show you're listening, not so much it feels like a stare.
From the book

Van Edwards's team ran an online quiz asking people to read emotions from photos of eyes alone, and more than 15,000 people took it. Most found negative emotions such as contempt or fear the hardest to spot. Her takeaway: we lose a lot of information when we don't really look at people, and we miss the most when it matters most. Holding someone's gaze isn't only a cue you send. It's how you gather the cues they send you.

Worth knowing

The figure that 65 to 90 percent of communication is nonverbal is a wide estimate, and it varies a lot with the situation. The famous "93 percent" version comes from small studies about tone of voice and single words, so don't take any exact share too literally. The point that body language matters a great deal still holds.

Try it this week

In your next conversation, check your three Ts. If your toes or torso are pointing at the door or your screen, turn them towards the other person and see if the conversation warms up.

In plain words

To look like someone people want to follow, turn towards them, lean in a little, keep your arms and the space between you clear, stand at a comfortable distance and really look at them.

Chapter 4 · Warmth cues

The Wow Factor

In short

Warmth cues make people feel seen and safe: the head tilt, the nod, the eyebrow raise, the real smile, a well-placed touch and mirroring. Use them on purpose and people open up.

The big ideas

  • Time your tilts. Tilting your head shows curiosity and says "tell me more". It exposes the neck, which reads as trust. Use it while listening; overdo it in a negotiation and it can look submissive.
  • Nod to know. Slow nods encourage people to keep talking, and a triple nod in particular invites them to go on. Nodding yes tends to draw more agreement back.
  • Eyebrows raise expectations. A quick lift of the brows is a near-universal signal of interest and recognition. It's the face we make when we spot a friend across a room.
  • Savor smiles. A real smile reaches the eyes and crinkles the corners. Don't wear a permanent smile; be smile-ready, and let a real one spread slowly when you mean it.
  • A touch of trust and mirroring. A light, appropriate touch on the arm or shoulder can build trust. Mirroring someone's positive cues, such as their gestures, volume or pace, makes you feel in step; when they send a negative cue, don't copy it, change it.
From the book

The book cites a study of professional basketball. Researchers counted the touches among teammates early in the 2008 to 2009 NBA season: high fives, fist bumps, chest bumps and hugs. Teams that touched more early on tended to cooperate more and to play better later in the season, even after accounting for how good the players were expected to be. Van Edwards also notes research on profile photos, where pictures with a slight head tilt and a smile showing some teeth were rated among the most likable.

Try it this week

Next time someone is telling you something that matters to them, try the triple nod and a slight head tilt, and notice whether they keep going for longer.

In plain words

Warmth is shown, not told. Tilt your head to listen, nod to encourage, lift your brows when you greet people, smile only when you mean it, and match the good cues people send you.

Chapter 5 · Competence cues

How to Look Powerful

In short

Competence cues tell people you're capable and in control: a confident posture, a focused lower eyelid, the steeple, gestures that explain, and visible palms. A few more cues help you hold the floor when you're interrupted.

The big ideas

  • Powerful posture. Roll your shoulders back and down, keep your feet a little apart, and let your hands rest loose and visible. You look relaxed and ready at the same time.
  • All seeing, all knowing. Tightening your lower eyelids slightly, the look of someone concentrating, signals focus and intensity. The book borrows Tyra Banks's word for smiling with your eyes, the smize.
  • The steeple. Touch your fingertips together with the palms apart, like a church steeple. It's a classic sign of calm, confident thinking.
  • Excel at explaining. Gestures that show size, number, direction or yourself help people follow you. Show three fingers when you list three points.
  • Palm power and holding the floor. Open, visible palms say you have nothing to hide and invite people to listen. If someone interrupts, try the fish (a slightly open mouth, as if about to speak), the bookmark (a raised hand or finger meaning "I'm not done") or the anchor touch (a light touch on the arm to regain attention).
From the book

Van Edwards's lab watched hundreds of hours of TED talks and had people rate the speakers. The most popular talks used far more hand gestures than the least popular ones, roughly 465 against 272 in the same eighteen minutes, and people who watched with the sound off rated the speakers much as people who heard them did. Hands, the book argues, are one of the first places we look to decide whether someone can be trusted and whether they know what they're talking about.

Try it this week

Before your next presentation, plan one explanatory gesture for each key point: a number on your fingers, a size with your hands, a sweep for a timeline.

In plain words

To look capable, stand easy and open, keep your hands where people can see them, and use them to explain. When someone talks over you, a small cue can win the floor back without a fight.

Chapter 6 · Danger-zone cues

How to Spot a Bad Guy… and Not Be One Yourself

In short

Some cues quietly signal discomfort, distrust or contempt: distancing, self-soothing, blocking, the shame touch and an accidental bothered face. No single one proves anything, but a cluster is worth noticing, in other people and in yourself.

The big ideas

  • Distancing. Leaning back, looking away, edging away or reaching for a phone puts space between you. Physical distance tends to create emotional distance.
  • Self-comfort. Rubbing the neck or arm, fanning yourself (ventilating) or fixing hair and clothes (preening) are ways of calming ourselves. They show stress and pull attention from what we're saying.
  • Blocking. Covering the notch at the base of the throat, clutching a bag or cup, covering the face or eyes, or shifting eye contact away are ways of protecting ourselves.
  • Shame and the bothered face. Touching the forehead while looking down often goes with shame or embarrassment. And some resting faces send cues we never meant: a furrowed brow reads as anger, drooping lids and mouth as sadness, a one-sided smirk as contempt.
  • Clusters and congruence. There is no single cue for lying. Look for several danger-zone cues together, and for words that don't match the body, such as "I'm thrilled" said with a flat face and crossed arms.
From the book

The book suggests an experiment you can run on yourself. Film three short clips: one telling the truth about your day, one describing something that makes you anxious, and one telling a small, harmless lie. Then watch them side by side. Most people are surprised by how much changes: they touch their face, lean back, slow down or speed up, and their smile changes shape. Seeing your own danger-zone cues is the fastest way to stop sending them by accident. Van Edwards also mentions research on Botox: people who can no longer frown report feeling a little less angry, a sign that our faces feed back into our moods.

Try it this week

Film the three clips: truth, anxious, harmless lie. Write down the cues that change between them. Those are the ones to watch for in yourself.

In plain words

Watch for groups of uneasy cues, not single ones, and treat them as a prompt to check in rather than proof that someone is lying. Then make sure your own resting face and habits aren't saying something you don't mean.

Part three · vocal cues

Voice

People decide how they feel about a voice in a fraction of a second. These chapters cover the vocal cues that make you sound sure of yourself, and the ones that make you sound like someone people want to keep talking to.

The idea behind this part

How you say it is a message of its own. Pitch, pace, pauses and volume can carry warmth or competence, or quietly cancel your words.

Chapter 7 · Vocal competence cues

Sound Powerful

In short

Your voice can make you sound sure or unsure regardless of your words. Speak from your lowest comfortable pitch, end statements on a downward note, skip the vocal fry, vary your volume and use pauses instead of fillers.

The big ideas

  • Sound confident. Nerves push our voices up and tighten them. Speaking in your lowest comfortable pitch, the one you hit as you breathe out, not your deepest possible growl, sounds steadier and surer.
  • Drop the question inflection. Ending a statement with a rising tone turns it into a question. "I charge $100 per hour?" invites a haggle; "I charge $100 per hour." doesn't.
  • Eliminate vocal fry. The creaky, crackling sound at the end of a sentence when we run low on breath tends to make listeners rate us as less competent. Breathe before you run out.
  • Volume control and power pauses. Getting a little louder for your key point marks it as important. And a deliberate pause, instead of "um", "like" or "you know", makes you sound in control and gives people time to take in what you said.
From the book

The book points to a study of surgeons. Researchers recorded doctors talking with their patients, then filtered the clips so the words could no longer be understood, leaving only the tone of voice. Listeners rated each surgeon on how dominant or caring they sounded. Those ratings alone were linked to which surgeons had been sued for malpractice: the ones who sounded more domineering and less concerned were more likely to have been. Patients were reacting to vocal cues, not to medical skill.

Try it this week

Read your rates, prices or main ask aloud into your phone. Listen for a rising ending or fry, then rerecord it with a downward finish and a pause before the number.

In plain words

If you want to be taken seriously, finish statements on a falling note, speak from the bottom of your comfortable range, breathe so you don't fry, and let silence do the work of "um".

Chapter 8 · Vocal warmth cues

Vocal Likability

In short

People decide how they feel about your voice almost instantly. Warm up your greeting, vary your delivery so you sound interested, and use small listening sounds that invite others to keep talking.

The big ideas

  • A fast first impression. Listeners form an impression of a voice within a fraction of a second, around 200 milliseconds by one estimate. Don't answer the phone mid-sigh, distracted or holding your breath.
  • Sound friendly. A vocal hug, such as "Oh, it's so good to hear from you!", said like you mean it, warms the start of any call. A real smile changes the sound of your voice, and people can hear it.
  • Sound more interesting. A flat, unchanging voice tells people you're bored. Vary pitch, pace and volume. The book suggests marking up scripts with small symbols: a smiley where you should smile, a line where you pause, underlines for stress.
  • Sound encouraging. Small sounds such as "mm-hmm" and "ooh", and nudges like "tell me more", show you're listening and invite people to go on.
  • Channel your charisma. Thinking of a charismatic person you admire as you speak can shift your own delivery. In one study, people asked to channel Steve Jobs while presenting spoke more loudly, held more eye contact and gestured more clearly.
From the book

Van Edwards admits she used to swallow her own name. Whenever she introduced herself while nervous, her name came out rushed and quiet, and people would ask her to repeat it or just get it wrong. Her fix was a vocal cue paired with a nonverbal one: she now touches her chest and says her name slowly and clearly, as if it's worth hearing. A name, she argues, is the first thing people remember you by, so it deserves the most care in your delivery.

Try it this week

Mark up your opening line for your next call or talk: one smile, one pause, one stressed word. Say your name slowly, as if it matters. It does.

In plain words

Warmth in a voice comes from a real smile, variety in how you speak, and small sounds that tell people you're with them. Plan where you'll pause and smile, and slow down on your name.

Part four · verbal and imagery cues

Words and Visuals

Cues don't stop when you go quiet. Emails, texts, profile pictures, colours and the objects behind you on a video call all send warmth or competence signals of their own.

The idea behind this part

Everything people see or read from you is a cue. Choose your words, colours and images as carefully as your posture.

Chapter 9 · Verbal cues

How to Communicate with Charisma

In short

Words carry warmth and competence too. Audit your emails, make your openers and closers memorable, pick words to suit the person, and ask questions that start real conversations.

The big ideas

  • Run an email audit. Look at five emails you sent recently and count the warm words (thanks, glad, excited, appreciate), the competent words (plan, results, data, deadline) and the words that do both. Most of us lean hard one way.
  • Stop being boring. Openers and closers are the most read parts of any message. Swap "Hi" and "Best" for something with a little life, like "Happy Monday, team!" or "Appreciatively".
  • Inspirational or informational. Warmth-minded people are moved by stories, people and social proof; competence-minded people by data, research and results. Match your message to your reader.
  • Better questions. Questions like "Been busy?" or "How are you?" get autopilot answers. Try "Working on anything exciting recently?" or "Doing anything fun this weekend?" instead.
  • Be a verbal chameleon. Notice the words and style people use, short and direct or chatty and emoji-rich, and mirror them a little. It tells them you're on the same wavelength.
From the book

When Hotmail launched in 1996, its founders, Sabeer Bhatia and Jack Smith, had almost no marketing budget. So they added one line to the bottom of every email their users sent: "PS: I love you. Get your free email at Hotmail." Every message became an advert with a warm sign-off, and the service grew to millions of users with astonishing speed. The book pairs the story with research from the pollster Frank Luntz suggesting that a postscript is one of the most read parts of any letter, second only to the opening.

Try it this week

Do the audit on your last five sent emails. Then rewrite one opener and one closer so they sound like you on a good day.

In plain words

Your written words are cues as well. Notice whether you sound warm, competent or both, give your openers and closers some life, and speak each reader's language.

Chapter 10 · Imagery cues

Creating a Powerful Visual Presence

In short

Photos, colours, clothes, objects and packaging all send cues. Choose them so they say what you want: premium or friendly, warm or competent, and never accidentally biased.

The big ideas

  • Elevate. Visual cues can raise value. Tiffany's turquoise box makes whatever is inside feel precious before it's opened. A warm drink in your hand can even make others seem warmer: in one study, people holding a hot coffee rated a stranger as warmer than those holding an iced one.
  • Images that inspire. The pictures around you, your video background, profile photo, slides, even your phone wallpaper, nudge how you and others feel. Pick ones that match the message.
  • Your nonverbal brand. Objects carry meaning. In one study, people primed with a briefcase were more competitive than those primed with a backpack. Glasses tend to read as competent. A dating-app study found women rated men pictured holding cats as less masculine and less dateable.
  • Colour me confident. In Western settings, red tends to signal action and urgency, blue calm and trust, green go and nature, and yellow warmth and joy, though too much yellow overwhelms. Typography matters too: text in ALL CAPS is harder to read.
  • Cue that bias. People make biased snap judgements from appearance, such as seeing women as warm but less competent. Van Edwards describes deliberately adding competence cues, from word choice to dress to her video background, to get ahead of them.
From the book

In 2016, Van Edwards's company hired a marketing agency and invited several firms to pitch. One proposal stood out from the start. That agency had built its slides in the company's own brand colours, fonts and photos, so its ideas already looked like they belonged. The team found itself picturing the work as finished before it had been agreed. The lesson she draws: when people can see themselves in your visuals, they find it much easier to say yes.

Worth knowing

Colour meanings vary between cultures (white is a colour of mourning in parts of East Asia, for example), and studies of colour and of priming with objects have had mixed results when repeated. Treat these as tendencies to test with your own audience.

Try it this week

Open your camera and look at your frame as a stranger would. Change one thing, the backdrop, the light, the distance or an object, so it says what you want it to.

In plain words

Everything people see around you is a cue. Frame yourself well on camera, choose colours and objects on purpose, and use visuals your audience can see themselves in.

Part five · the practice

Practice

The book closes with three rules for using everything that came before, and a method for turning forty-odd cues into habits.

The idea behind this part

Assume the best about people, never fake a cue, and practise each one three times in each direction until it becomes yours.

Conclusion · Three rules for using cues

Cues Best Practices

In short

Use cues with good intentions and good sense. Expect the best of people, never fake a cue you don't feel, and learn each one with the rule of three.

The big ideas

  • Rule 1: expect the best. A crossed arm may just mean a cold room. Give people the benefit of the doubt, and use what you read to ask better questions, not to judge.
  • Rule 2: don't fake it. A cue that doesn't match what you feel tends to look phony, and people sense it. Find the real feeling first: think of something that truly makes you smile before you smile.
  • Rule 3: the rule of three. Take one cue at a time. Spot it in other people three times, use it yourself three times, and jot down what happens. Then move on to the next.
  • A lifelong skill. The book contains more than forty cues. Nobody uses them all at once. Learn them one by one, and in time the useful ones become how you naturally show up.
From the book

Van Edwards ends where she began: cues are a language, and like any language you get fluent by using it, not by reading about it. She asks readers not to try to change everything on Monday morning. Pick one cue that addresses your own gap, warmth or competence, and spend a week on it. Notice it on television, in meetings, across the dinner table. Then try it, and watch what changes in the people around you.

Try it this week

Pick one cue from your weaker side in the lab above and practise it for a week. Come back and tick it off.

In plain words

Learn cues slowly and honestly. Assume the best about others, never fake it, and practise one cue at a time: three times reading it, three times using it.
