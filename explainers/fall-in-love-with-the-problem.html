A chapter-by-chapter guide to Uri Levine's book

Fall in Love with the Problem,
Explained

Uri Levine co-founded Waze, the crowd-sourced traffic app Google bought in 2013, and has since started or backed a string of companies that each began with a single annoyance. Fall in Love with the Problem, Not the Solution is his handbook for founders: start from a painful problem that many people share, expect to fail many times on the way, find product-market fit before anything else, and only then work out the money, the growth, the move abroad and, maybe, the exit. This guide covers the introduction, all twelve chapters (the fundraising chapter comes in two halves, 5a and 5b) and the closing "Happylogue", with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

Your first solution will probably be wrong. If you love the problem, you'll keep going until you find the one that isn't.

Part one · the problem

The problem

The book has no formal parts, so this guide groups its chapters into four stretches of a founder's journey. The first is about where a company should begin: not with a clever idea, but with a pain that many people feel. Levine then warns that the road from that pain to a working answer is mostly failure, and that real disruption means changing how people behave, which the people who profit from the old way will fight.

The idea behind this part

A startup is only as good as the problem it chooses. Pick a big, shared pain, stay loyal to it, and treat every failed solution as a step toward the right one.

Introduction · Why this book starts with a problem

Introduction

In short

Levine has built companies around traffic jams, airfares, hidden fees and public transport. Each began with something that annoyed him and many other people. He wrote the book to give founders the lessons he learned the hard way, so they can raise their odds of success.

The big ideas

  • Start with an annoyance. Every company Levine describes started with a frustration he felt himself: sitting in traffic, paying more for a flight than the price a week later, finding fees buried in savings. The question is always whether lots of other people feel it too.
  • A problem worth solving. A good problem is big (many people have it), frequent (they hit it often) and painful (they'd change their habits to be rid of it). If you can't explain it in a sentence that makes people nod, it may not be worth a company.
  • A handbook, not a memoir. Waze is the book's main example, but Levine draws on many startups he founded, invested in or mentored. He gives practical rules of thumb: the 30-day test for hires, a hundred noes when raising money, one priority per phase.
  • Odds, not certainties. Most startups fail. Levine doesn't promise a recipe for success, only ways to fail faster and cheaper, so you get more chances to find what works.
From the book

Waze grew out of a community mapping project that Ehud Shabtai had started in Israel, which turned GPS phones into map makers. Levine, Shabtai and Amir Shinar founded the company around a simple, shared pain: being stuck in traffic and wishing someone had warned you. Drivers' phones reported their speed, so each driver helped the ones behind. Google bought Waze in 2013 for a reported figure of about 1.1 billion dollars. Levine went on to found or back other companies built the same way, including Moovit for public transport, FairFly for airfares that drop after you've booked, and Pontera (first called FeeX) for hidden fees in retirement savings. The book opens with a foreword by Apple co-founder Steve Wozniak.

Try it this week

Write down three things that annoyed you this week. For each one, ask two people whether it happens to them too, and how often.

In plain words

Don't start from a gadget you want to build. Start from something that bugs lots of people, often, and badly. If that's true, there's room for a company; if not, there isn't, however clever the idea.

Chapter 1 · The problem is your north star

Fall in Love with the Problem, Not the Solution

In short

Founders who fall in love with their solution defend it even when users ignore it. Founders who fall in love with the problem keep changing the solution until users stay. The problem gives you direction; the solution is just your current guess.

The big ideas

  • The problem is the story. When you tell people about your company, start with the problem. If they recognise it straight away and tell you their own version, you have something. If you start with the solution, they'll judge your features instead.
  • Talk to people, a lot. Levine urges founders to describe the problem to many potential users before building, and to listen for emotion: do they say "yes, that happens to me all the time"? Surveys miss that; conversations catch it.
  • You're not the market. Feeling the pain yourself is a great start, but it proves only that one person has it. You need evidence that many others do.
  • Solutions are disposable. Because your loyalty is to the problem, you can drop a solution that isn't working without losing your purpose. Ego stays out of it, so feedback hurts less.
  • Simple wins. The products people keep using tend to do one important thing simply. Levine points out that the basics of Google search or Waze barely changed once they worked.
From the book

Levine's description of Waze has stayed the same since its first day: help drivers avoid traffic jams, using the drivers ahead of them. The way Waze did it changed many times. Early on, its maps were thin in many places, so the app even turned driving on unmapped roads into a small game, rewarding users for filling in the map. FairFly shows the same habit of mind. Levine noticed that air fares often fell after he had booked, so the money was simply lost. The company's answer was to watch the price after booking and rebook when it dropped, but the starting point was the annoyance, not the software.

Try it this week

Write your product's problem in one sentence with no mention of your solution. Say it to three people and note whether they tell you a story of their own.

In plain words

Your first idea is probably not the answer. If you're married to the idea, you'll polish something people don't want. If you're married to the problem, you'll keep trying things until one works.

Chapter 2 · Fail fast to get more tries

A Startup Is a Journey of Failures

In short

Building something new means trying things that haven't been done, so most of them won't work. The founders who win aren't the ones who avoid failure but the ones who fail quickly, learn, and still have money and energy for the next try.

The big ideas

  • Failure is the normal path. Levine describes the startup journey as one failure after another until something clicks. If you're afraid to fail, you've already failed, because you won't try.
  • The desert of no traction. The longest and loneliest stretch is when you've launched and almost nothing happens. Users trickle in and leave. Many companies die here, not from a single disaster but from running out of time.
  • Speed buys attempts. If each try takes a year, you may get two before the money runs out. If each takes a couple of months, you get many. More attempts means better odds, like taking ten shots from half court instead of one.
  • Good enough, then ship. A slightly embarrassing first version that reaches real users teaches more than a polished one still on the whiteboard.
From the book

Waze did not take off at launch. It worked reasonably well in Israel, where the community had already built much of the map, but in many other countries new users found a sparse map and too few other drivers to make the traffic data useful. Levine describes years of releasing version after version, fixing whatever was stopping people from staying, before the product reached fit. He uses that long stretch to make his point: the company survived because it kept trying quickly and kept enough money to try again, not because its first version was right.

Try it this week

Pick the thing you're polishing right now. Decide the smallest version that could reach a real user this week, and ship that instead.

In plain words

Most tries won't work, so the real game is how many tries you get before the money runs out. Make each one small and fast, learn from it, and go again.

Chapter 3 · Change the market's behaviour

Embrace Disruption

In short

Disruption isn't a new technology; it's a change in how people behave and in who holds power in a market. Inefficient markets with unhappy customers are where disruption happens, and the people who profit from the old way will resist it.

The big ideas

  • Behaviour, not gadgets. What made Netflix, Uber or Airbnb disruptive wasn't the code. It was that people started renting films, getting around or booking rooms in a different way, and the old balance of the market broke.
  • Look for inefficient markets. Markets where customers are overcharged, kept in the dark or forced to wait are ripe. Transparency is often the weapon: show people prices, times or fees they couldn't see before.
  • Incumbents don't see it. The leaders of a market usually dismiss the newcomer. Levine recalls Microsoft's then chief executive Steve Ballmer laughing off the first iPhone in 2007, and the long list of companies like Blockbuster and Kodak that saw change coming and didn't move.
  • Expect resistance. Disrupting a market means taking money or power from someone. Expect lawsuits, regulators and partners who won't talk to you. Plan for it rather than hoping it won't happen.
From the book

Levine recounts how established players treated Waze as a small curiosity. Early on, the company tried to work with an existing Israeli navigation business, Telmap, and got little more than polite dismissal: a small crowd-sourced map couldn't matter to a company with professional maps. Within a few years, crowd-sourced, real-time traffic had become what drivers expected, and the old way of selling navigation through expensive devices and map licences was in trouble. His wider point is that incumbents almost always judge a disruptor by today's product, while the disruptor is betting on how people will behave tomorrow.

Try it this week

Name one market you deal with that annoys you. Write down what is hidden from you as a customer there: a price, a wait, a fee. That's where a disruptor would start.

In plain words

A disruptor changes what people do, not just what they use. It usually starts in a market where customers are poorly served, and the people who benefit from the old way will fight it, so be ready.

Part two · the company

The company

Once you're committed to a problem, you have to build something that can survive long enough to solve it. These chapters cover how to organise the work into phases with one priority each, how to raise money from investors who mostly say no, how to keep those investors on side, and how to build a team by hiring carefully and letting go quickly.

The idea behind this part

A company is a vehicle for the problem. Focus it on one thing at a time, fund it patiently, and protect it from the wrong people.

Chapter 4 · One most important thing at a time

Operate in Phases

In short

A startup goes through phases: find product-market fit, then a business model, then growth, then going global. In each phase there is one most important thing, and doing everything at once means doing none of it well.

The big ideas

  • Product-market fit comes first. Until users come back, nothing else matters. Revenue, partnerships and expansion all rest on a product people already keep using.
  • The MIT. Levine's shorthand is the most important thing. Each phase has one, and the team's job is to push it forward and say no to the rest, even good ideas.
  • Focus means saying no. Small teams can only do a few things. Every partnership, feature or market you take on early steals effort from the one thing that decides whether you survive.
  • Phases overlap only a little. You can think ahead, but serious effort should go to the next phase only once the current one is done. Building a business model on a product nobody uses is building on sand.
From the book

Levine describes Waze's path as a series of phases. For a long time, the only question was whether drivers would keep using it, and the team put almost everything into that. Money from advertising, which became the company's business model, came later, once there were enough loyal users for local businesses to want to reach. Going global properly came after that. He contrasts this with startups he has seen try to sign big partners, plan revenue and launch in several countries before anyone loved the product, and run out of time doing all of it halfway.

Try it this week

Write your team's one most important thing for this month on a sticky note. Then list three things you'll say no to because of it.

In plain words

Do things in order. First make something people keep using, then work out how it makes money, then grow it, then take it abroad. Trying to do all four at once mostly means redoing work later.

Chapter 5a · A roller coaster in the dark

Ride the Fundraising Roller Coaster

In short

Raising money is a long series of ups and downs where you can't see what's coming. Expect to hear no again and again, lead with the problem, and know that investors move slowly until they fear missing out.

The big ideas

  • A roller coaster in the dark. One meeting goes brilliantly, the next investor goes quiet, a term sheet appears and then falls apart. You can't see the next drop, so you need the stamina to keep going.
  • The dance of the hundred noes. Levine tells founders to expect something like a hundred rejections. Each no is normal and teaches you something about the pitch; it's not a verdict on the company.
  • Lead with your strongest point. Investors form a view in the first minutes. Open with the problem and with your best evidence, such as traction or a striking number, not with background slides.
  • Slow until they're not. Investors take their time, until another firm shows interest. Then they move fast. Momentum and competition for the deal matter as much as the slides.
  • Know the term sheet. The amount and valuation matter, but so do the rights and preferences investors get and who will sit on your board. Read all of it.
From the book

Levine writes from both sides of the table, as a founder raising money and as an investor hearing pitches. Waze needed several rounds; its first sizeable one, in 2008, was reported at about 12 million dollars, and each later round meant going back out to investors. He describes the same pattern each time: weeks of meetings, a long run of polite rejections, then one firm commits and others who had hesitated suddenly want in. His advice is to treat the noes as part of the process, tighten the story after each one, and keep going until the first yes, because the first yes is what makes the others easier.

Try it this week

Count how many investors, customers or partners you've actually asked this month. If it's under twenty, the number of noes isn't your problem yet.

In plain words

Expect lots of rejection when you raise money. Start with the problem and your best evidence, keep pitching, and remember that the first yes makes the next ones come faster.

Chapter 5b · No surprises, ever

Manage Your Investors

In short

Once investors are on board, you have to manage the relationship. Keep them informed, never surprise the board, and remember that your job is to build the company, not to keep every investor happy.

The big ideas

  • Never surprise the board. Bad news delivered early, with a plan, builds trust. Bad news that turns up at a board meeting destroys it.
  • Update them regularly. A short, honest update every month or two keeps investors close and makes them more useful when you need help or more money.
  • Interests can split. Investors, founders and employees want the same thing most of the time, but not always: on when to sell, at what price, or how to raise the next round. Know where the tensions are.
  • Results, not approval. Your job is to deliver results for the company. Making investors comfortable at the cost of the right decision is a mistake.
  • Look after founders and early staff. Selling a few shares in later rounds can let founders and key people stay calm about the long run instead of pushing for an early exit.
From the book

Levine's experience of boards is that trouble rarely comes from bad news itself; it comes from bad news investors didn't see coming. He describes the habit of calling board members before a meeting when something has gone wrong, so the meeting is about what to do, not about who knew what. He also discusses the moments when interests split most sharply, such as when an offer to buy the company arrives or when later investors want terms that hurt earlier ones, and argues that founders should negotiate fairly for themselves and for the employees who built the company.

Try it this week

Draft a five-line investor update: the one most important thing, the number that shows progress, the biggest problem, what you're doing about it, and where you need help.

In plain words

Treat investors as partners you keep well informed. Share bad news early and with a plan, and don't let keeping them happy replace doing what the company needs.

Chapter 6 · The 30-day test

Firing and Hiring

In short

Levine puts firing before hiring on purpose. Hiring the right people is important, but letting the wrong ones go quickly matters even more, because one wrong hire can drag down a small team for months.

The big ideas

  • The 30-day test. About a month after someone joins, ask yourself: knowing what I know now, would I hire this person again? If the answer is no, act now. If it's a clear yes, tell them, and think about giving them more.
  • Hiring is easy, firing is hard. Founders hire quickly and fire slowly because firing is painful. The cost of waiting falls on everyone else on the team.
  • Define your DNA. Decide early what kind of people fit: how they work, how they treat others. Levine's version includes a firm rule against people who make others miserable, however talented.
  • Generalists first. Early on you need people who can do many things. Specialists matter later, when the company knows exactly what it needs.
  • Ask the team. Peer feedback, even a simple anonymous ranking of who people would want to keep working with, can show problems a manager misses.
From the book

Levine admits that, like most founders, he has kept people too long, hoping they would improve. His conclusion is that the first month nearly always tells you enough. A person who doesn't fit a fast, uncertain startup is usually visible within weeks, and keeping them lowers the bar for everyone else. The best people notice when a weak colleague stays, and some leave. So he recommends the 30-day question for every new hire, a quick and humane exit when the answer is no, and real recognition, sometimes extra equity, when the answer is a strong yes.

Try it this week

For each person who joined in the last few months, answer the 30-day question honestly in one word. Then do something about every answer.

In plain words

After about a month you know whether a hire works. If it doesn't, act quickly and kindly, because your team pays for every week you wait. If it clearly does, tell them.

Part three · the product

The product

This is the heart of the handbook. Levine argues that founders badly misjudge how ordinary users will meet their product, that product-market fit is the only thing that matters until you have it, and that making money only comes after users already keep coming back.

The idea behind this part

If new users don't get the value quickly and come back for more, nothing else you do will matter. Find fit first, then charge for it.

Chapter 7 · You are not your user

Understand the User: You Are Only a Sample of One

In short

Founders know their product far too well to see it the way a new user does. Most users aren't early adopters; they need the value to be obvious, quick and free of hurdles, or they leave and never come back.

The big ideas

  • A sample of one. Your own reactions tell you about one person who already understands the product. Real users arrive with no context and little patience.
  • Different kinds of users. Levine uses the familiar split of innovators, early adopters, an early majority and a late majority. Innovators will forgive a lot; the majority, where the big numbers are, will forgive almost nothing.
  • The first time matters most. People decide in the first use whether a product is worth their time. A confusing sign-up, unclear instructions or value that shows up only later loses most of them.
  • Watch, don't ask. Sit beside new users and watch them try it. Ask why they did what they did. Where they hesitate is where the product is failing.
From the book

Levine describes how often founders are amazed when they watch strangers use their product. Steps that seem obvious to the team stop newcomers cold: a sign-up form before any value, a button whose meaning only an insider would guess, a screen that assumes you know what to do next. In Waze's case, a new driver in a place with few other users opened an app whose map and traffic information were thin, so the first experience didn't show what Waze could do. The fix in every case is the same: find the barrier, remove it, and watch again.

Try it this week

Hand your product, website or form to someone who has never seen it. Say nothing, watch them for five minutes, and write down every place they hesitate.

In plain words

You're the worst judge of your own product because you already know how it works. Watch real newcomers, find where they get stuck, and take those obstacles away.

Chapter 8 · Conversion and retention

Figure Out Product-Market Fit or Die

In short

Product-market fit means you've made something that creates real value for users, and the proof is that they keep coming back. Levine measures it with two numbers, conversion and retention, and says that until you have it, nothing else matters.

The big ideas

  • Retention is the test. Downloads, sign-ups, press and even early paying customers can all happen without fit. If users don't come back, the product isn't solving their problem well enough.
  • Two numbers. Conversion is the share of new users who get to the value on their first try. Retention is the share who keep using it over time. Work on conversion first, then retention.
  • Map the funnel. Draw every step from hearing about the product to using it regularly, and measure how many people drop at each step. Fix the worst drop first.
  • Hype isn't fit. Levine mentions Bump, an app for swapping contact details by bumping phones together, which was downloaded by huge numbers of people but used too rarely to last. Popularity at launch isn't the same as a habit.
  • Fit takes time. For many companies, including Waze, finding fit took years of versions. That's normal; running out of money before you get there is the danger.
From the book

Levine's own account of Waze's search for fit is a long list of small changes, each aimed at a specific drop in the funnel. If new drivers didn't see useful traffic on their first trip, the team looked for ways to show value sooner; if they used it once and didn't return, the team asked why. Bump is his counter-example: a clever trick that went viral and was downloaded by many millions, but most people only needed it now and then, so it never became a habit, and it was shut down after Google bought the team in 2013. Big numbers at the top of the funnel, he warns, can hide the fact that almost nobody stays.

Worth knowing

Levine gives rough retention numbers for different kinds of products. The bar in this lab is illustrative; what counts as good depends on how often people naturally need what you make.

Try it this week

Write your product's funnel as five steps and put a real or estimated number on each. Circle the biggest drop; that's next week's work.

In plain words

If people don't keep using your product, you haven't found fit, however many downloaded it. Measure how many reach the value and how many come back, and fix the biggest leak first.

Chapter 9 · The business model comes after fit

Making Money

In short

Once users stay, the next phase is working out how the company makes money. A business model works when the value of a customer over time is clearly more than the cost of winning them, and finding the right price and payer takes experiments.

The big ideas

  • The user isn't always the customer. The person who uses the product and the one who pays can be different. Waze's drivers paid nothing; businesses paid to reach them.
  • Lifetime value against acquisition cost. Add up what an average customer brings in over the time they stay, and compare it with what it costs to win one. If winning a customer costs more than they're worth, growth only loses money faster.
  • Value proposition, market, price. Be clear about the value you create, how many people or companies will pay for it, and how: subscription, one-off payment, ads or a share of savings.
  • Test prices. You won't guess the right price. Try several, measure what happens, and change them.
From the book

Waze shows the split between user and customer. Drivers used it for free, which was essential for gathering the traffic data that made it work. The business model, which came only after users stayed, was location-based advertising: a coffee shop or petrol station along your route could pay to appear on the map. Levine describes his other companies working through the same questions in different ways. Pontera, for example, deals with fees hidden in retirement savings, and had to find who would pay for revealing and reducing them. In every case, he says, the model only became clear after real customers had been tried at real prices.

Try it this week

Write down who uses your product and who pays for it. If they're different people, write one sentence on why the payer gets value.

In plain words

A business works when a customer brings in more over time than it cost to win them. Figure out who pays, how much and how, by trying real prices, and do it after people already love the product.

Part four · scale and exit

Scale and exit

With fit and a business model, the questions change: how to reach a very large number of users without paying for each one, how and when to go abroad, and whether to sell. Levine closes with a short, personal Happylogue on why the whole bumpy ride is worth taking.

The idea behind this part

Grow by word of mouth, go global one well-chosen market at a time, and judge an exit by what's best for the problem, the team and you.

Chapter 10 · Growth that pays for itself

How to Get to a Billion Users

In short

Once you have fit and a business model, growth is the next phase. The best growth comes from users telling other users; paid marketing helps, but only if each new user is worth more than they cost. Above all, growth only works if the users you get actually stay.

The big ideas

  • Word of mouth first. A product that solves a real problem gets talked about. That's the cheapest and most trusted kind of marketing, and it's what grew Waze.
  • Paid growth needs the numbers. Buying users works only when a user's lifetime value is higher than their cost. Otherwise you're buying losses.
  • Fix the leaks first. Pouring users into a product with poor retention fills a leaking bucket. Growth multiplies whatever you already have, good or bad.
  • Make sharing natural. Products that are better when your friends use them, or that give users a reason to mention them, spread themselves.
  • Think in phases still. Growth is its own phase with its own most important thing. Don't start it before fit and a working model.
From the book

Waze spent very little on traditional marketing. Its growth came from drivers who arrived somewhere early, saved twenty minutes, and told someone. Each new driver also made the traffic picture better for everyone else, so the product improved as it spread. By the time Google bought it, Waze had tens of millions of users. Levine contrasts this with companies that try to buy growth before people stay, and with products like Bump that went viral without lasting value: lots of new users, but the bucket emptied as fast as it filled.

Try it this week

Ask your last five happy users how they first heard about you. If none heard from a friend, work on why people don't mention it.

In plain words

Grow mostly by being worth talking about. Pay for users only when the maths works, and stop the leaks before you pour more people in.

Chapter 11 · One well-chosen market at a time

Go Global

In short

Most big problems are global, and a small home market won't support a large company. But going abroad is expensive and every market is different, so choose markets carefully, localise properly, find local partners and avoid spreading the team too thin.

The big ideas

  • Think global early. For founders in a small country, like Levine's Israel, the home market is a test bed, not the destination. Plan for the big markets from the start.
  • Pick markets on purpose. Size, how hard the market is, local competitors and regulation all matter. The US is huge and hard; a smaller market can be a better next step.
  • Localise for real. Language is the start. Habits, payment, laws and what people expect all differ. Treat each new country almost like a new launch, with its own small first version.
  • Use local people. Partners and communities on the ground do what a head office far away can't. Moovit grew city by city with local partners and volunteer editors who mapped transit lines.
  • Don't spread too thin. Launching in ten countries at once with the effort for two leaves all ten weak. Products that depend on many local users, like Waze, need enough density in each place to work at all.
From the book

Waze and Moovit both had to solve a local problem in many places at once. A traffic app is useless in a city where too few people use it, because there's no one ahead of you to report the jam. Waze leaned on local communities of volunteer map editors to build and fix maps in each country. Moovit, which Levine backed as an early investor and board member, combined data from transit agencies with a community of local users who added and corrected routes, growing into thousands of cities. Intel bought Moovit in 2020 for a reported figure of about 900 million dollars. The lesson Levine draws is to concentrate enough effort in each market to cross the point where it starts to grow by itself.

Try it this week

List the next three markets you could enter. For each, write one local partner or community that could help you there. If you can't name one, that market isn't next.

In plain words

Big companies solve problems for the world, but you get there market by market. Pick carefully, adapt properly, find local help, and put enough into each place for it to take off.

Chapter 12 · Selling, and what it really means

The Exit

In short

Selling a company is a decision about the future of the product, the team and the founders, not just a price. Levine explains how exits happen, why offers come and go, and how to think about whether to take one.

The big ideas

  • Companies are bought, not sold. You can't easily put a startup up for sale. Buyers come when they see that your company does something they need, often because you've become a threat or an opportunity.
  • Compare the offer with the risk. A future worth more on paper isn't worth more today if it may not happen. Weigh the offer against the chance of reaching that future and how long it would take.
  • Look after the people who built it. Levine cares about how employees share in a sale. Equity, retention bonuses and fair terms decide whether an exit feels like a win for everyone.
  • An exit is a beginning. For the product it may mean far more users; for founders it often means the start of the next company.
From the book

Waze's sale to Google in June 2013 was one of the biggest exits in Israeli tech at the time, at a reported price of about 1.1 billion dollars. It came after talks with other suitors; reports at the time said Facebook's talks broke down partly over whether the team would stay in Israel, which Waze insisted on. Levine describes how the board weighed the offer against the risk of carrying on alone, and he takes pride in how widely the proceeds were shared with Waze's employees. Waze kept operating as its own app inside Google, and its team stayed in Israel.

Worth knowing

This lab is a simple illustrative model with a fixed 15% yearly discount. It isn't Waze's real numbers or how the board decided, and real exits also weigh the team, the mission and the founders' own lives.

Try it this week

If someone offered to buy your project or your role tomorrow, write the one number and the one condition that would make you say yes. Knowing them in advance makes the real conversation easier.

In plain words

Selling is a bet against your own future. Compare the offer with the chance your bigger dream actually happens, and think about your team and the product, not only the price.

Happylogue · Why the ride is worth it

Happylogue

In short

Levine closes with a happy ending instead of an epilogue. The startup journey is hard and mostly made of failures, but solving a real problem for many people is deeply satisfying, and loving the problem is what keeps you going.

The big ideas

  • Impact over exits. The real reward is changing how many people live with a problem: fewer hours in traffic, fairer prices, less hidden fees. The money is a side effect.
  • Love keeps you going. A founder who loves the problem doesn't want to do anything else, which is exactly the stamina the long desert of failures demands.
  • Pass it on. Levine spends much of his time mentoring other founders, and he wrote the book to raise their odds.
  • Start now. His closing message is practical: find a problem you care about, talk to people who have it, and begin, knowing you'll fail along the way.
From the book

Levine looks back on the companies he has started and backed and says the best part was never the sale. It was hearing that a driver got home in time for dinner, or that someone got money back they didn't know they'd lost. He describes the thrill of the moment users first start coming back on their own, and the friendships built through the hard years. His message to readers is that being an entrepreneur is a choice to keep solving problems, and that if you pick one you truly care about, the hard parts are easier to bear.

Building something is hard and you'll fail a lot. Choose a problem you care about enough that you'd rather work on it than anything else, and the rest becomes worth it.
