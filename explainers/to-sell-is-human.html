A chapter-by-chapter guide to Daniel H. Pink's book

To Sell Is Human,
Explained

Daniel Pink's argument is that almost all of us are in sales now, even if nobody calls it that. Teachers, nurses, engineers, parents and founders spend a large part of every day trying to move other people to give up something they have in exchange for something we offer. To Sell Is Human explains why this kind of selling has changed, and what works now that buyers know as much as sellers do. This guide takes the introduction and all nine chapters, with a lab in each where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

The old ABC was Always Be Closing. The new ABC is Attunement, Buoyancy, Clarity, and the goal is to serve, not to trick.

Part one · rebirth of a salesman

Rebirth of a Salesman

Pink opens by widening the meaning of selling. Only about one American worker in nine has a sales job, but nearly everyone else spends a big share of the day persuading, influencing and convincing. He calls this non-sales selling, explains the three forces that spread it to almost every job, and shows why the old image of the slick, pushy salesman belongs to a world where the seller knew more than the buyer.

The idea behind this part

Selling is no longer a separate profession. It is part of most work, and it now happens in a world where buyers can check everything.

Introduction · We're all selling

Introduction

In short

Most of us spend a large part of each working day trying to move other people, persuading a colleague, a client, a boss or a child. That is selling, even when nobody calls it that.

The big ideas

  • Selling, broadly. Pink defines selling as persuading, influencing and convincing others to part with something they have, such as money, time, attention or effort, in exchange for what you offer. By that definition almost everyone sells.
  • Non-sales selling. A teacher getting a class to care about fractions, a developer convincing the team to change an approach, a doctor getting a patient to take the pills. None of them has a sales title, and all of them are selling.
  • Two kinds of selling. Traditional sales means getting someone to make a purchase. Non-sales selling has no money changing hands, but it works on the same people, with the same psychology.
  • The question of the book. If we all sell, what actually works now? Pink draws on social science and on people who do it well to answer three things: why selling has changed, how to be, and what to do.
From the book

To find out how much time people really spend moving others, Pink worked with the research firm Qualtrics on a survey called "What Do You Do at Work?". More than nine thousand full-time workers described their days. On average, people said about 40 percent of their time at work went on non-sales selling: persuading, influencing and convincing people in ways that didn't involve a purchase. That is around 24 minutes of every hour. They also rated this part of the job as crucial to their success.

Try it this week

For one day, put a tick on a sticky note every time you try to get someone to do, agree to or believe something. Count them in the evening.

In plain words

Selling isn't only what people in sales do. Whenever you try to get someone to give their time, attention or effort to your idea, you're selling, and most of us do it for close to half the day.

Chapter 1 · The last Fuller Brush man

We're All in Sales Now

In short

The traditional salesman is fading, but selling isn't. About one American worker in nine works in sales, and almost all the other eight spend much of their day selling something too.

The big ideas

  • One in nine. Government figures Pink cites put roughly one in nine American workers in a sales job, from shop staff to account managers. The share is similar in other rich countries.
  • The other eight. For everyone else, selling hides inside the job. People pitch ideas in meetings, convince suppliers to deliver early, and talk patients or students into doing what's good for them.
  • It's not optional. In Pink's survey, people rated moving others as one of the most important parts of their work, even though most had never been trained in it.
  • A new picture. The image of the lone door-to-door salesman is out of date. Selling today is something we all do, every day, mostly without a commission.
From the book

Pink tracks down Norman Hall, believed to be the last Fuller Brush salesman in San Francisco. The Fuller Brush Company, founded by Alfred Fuller in 1906, once had thousands of men going door to door with brushes, mops and cleaning products. Hall had been at it for decades, walking the same hilly streets every day, getting turned away again and again and coming back the next morning. Pink uses him as a symbol of a kind of selling that is fading, even as selling of another kind spreads to everybody else.

Try it this week

Write down the three people you most need to move this month, and what you need each of them to give: money, time, attention or effort.

In plain words

Only a small share of people have sales jobs, but almost everyone spends their day trying to move others. That makes selling a skill for everyone, not a niche.

Chapter 2 · Why selling spread

Entrepreneurship, Elasticity, and Ed-Med

In short

Three trends pulled selling into nearly every job: more people working for themselves or in tiny firms, jobs that stretch across many skills, and the growth of education and health care.

The big ideas

  • Entrepreneurship. Cheap tools, online marketplaces such as Etsy and funding sites such as Kickstarter make it easy to start something small. In a company of one or five, everyone has to sell.
  • Elasticity. Even in big organisations, roles stretch. An engineer who once only wrote code is now asked to present to customers and win over other teams. Some software firms, such as Atlassian at the time, grew large with no traditional sales team at all, because everyone helped persuade.
  • Ed-Med. Education and health care became the biggest and fastest-growing sources of jobs. Teachers and clinicians spend their days moving people: getting students to learn and patients to change how they live.
  • The result. Put these together and the line between sales and everything else blurs. Selling becomes a basic part of work rather than a department.
From the book

Pink points to companies that grew without the usual sales force. The Australian software firm Atlassian was a well-known case at the time: it sold its tools to developers mostly through its website and word of mouth, without a team of traditional salespeople. Selling didn't disappear there. It was spread across everyone, from the engineers who made the product easy to try to the support staff who turned users into fans.

Try it this week

Look at your own job description. Underline every line that only works if someone else says yes to you.

In plain words

Selling spread because more people work in small outfits, because jobs now stretch across many skills, and because the biggest employers are schools and hospitals, where moving people is the job.

Chapter 3 · When buyers know as much as sellers

From Caveat Emptor to Caveat Venditor

In short

Sales got a bad name in a world where the seller knew far more than the buyer. Now that buyers can look up almost anything, that gap has closed, and the rule has flipped from buyer beware to seller beware.

The big ideas

  • What people think of sales. Pink asked people for the first word that came to mind when they thought of sales. The most common was "pushy", and most of the rest were negative too.
  • Information asymmetry. That reputation grew up when the seller had information the buyer didn't. If you can't check a used car's history, the seller can tell you anything, and the sleazy salesman can win.
  • The gap closed. The internet gave buyers reviews, price comparisons and vehicle-history reports. When both sides know roughly the same, trickery stops paying.
  • Seller beware. In the old world it was caveat emptor, let the buyer beware. Now it is caveat venditor, let the seller beware: a seller who misleads gets found out and talked about.
From the book

In 1970 the economist George Akerlof published a paper called "The Market for 'Lemons'". Its idea was simple: when buyers can't tell good used cars from bad ones, they offer only an average price, owners of good cars refuse to sell, and the market fills up with lemons. Several leading journals rejected the paper as too trivial or obvious before it was published. It went on to help win Akerlof a share of the Nobel Prize in economics in 2001. Pink uses it to explain why selling got its sleazy image, and why that image no longer fits.

Worth knowing

Pink's argument isn't that information asymmetry has vanished. Doctors, mechanics and financial advisers can still know far more than we do. It's that in most everyday markets the gap is much smaller than it was, and the balance of power has shifted toward the buyer.

Try it this week

Before your next pitch, assume the other person has already searched for everything about it. What would they have found, and what would you need to say differently?

In plain words

Selling got its bad reputation when sellers knew more than buyers. Now buyers can check nearly everything, so honesty and real help work better than tricks.

Part two · how to be

How to Be

If the old motto was Always Be Closing, Pink offers a new ABC: three qualities that help anyone move others. Attunement is seeing the situation from the other person's side. Buoyancy is staying afloat in an ocean of rejection. Clarity is helping people see their situation in a fresh way and find problems they didn't know they had.

The idea behind this part

Moving people starts with who you are in the conversation: tuned in, resilient, and clear.

Chapter 4 · Seeing it from their side

Attunement

In short

Attunement means bringing your actions and outlook into harmony with other people and the situation you're in. Pink gives three rules for it, and shows that the best sellers are neither loud extraverts nor quiet introverts but somewhere in between.

The big ideas

  • Increase your power by reducing it. In a study led by Adam Galinsky, people who were first made to feel powerful were much more likely, when asked to draw a capital E on their own forehead, to draw it so they could read it, rather than so someone facing them could. Power makes us worse at seeing other people's point of view.
  • Use your head as much as your heart. In negotiation experiments, people asked to imagine what the other side was thinking found deals more often than people asked to imagine what the other side was feeling. Empathy matters, but perspective-taking works better for finding a solution.
  • Mimic strategically. People who subtly mirrored the other side's phrases and posture reached more deals in negotiation studies, and waiters who repeated orders back word for word got bigger tips. The key word is subtly: obvious copying backfires.
  • Be an ambivert. Adam Grant studied call-centre sales reps and found that the people who brought in the most revenue weren't the strongest extraverts. They were ambiverts, near the middle of the scale, who could talk and also listen.
From the book

In Galinsky's experiment, one group of people first wrote about a time they'd had power over someone; another group wrote about a time someone had power over them. Then each person was asked to draw a capital letter E on their own forehead with a marker. People in the high-power group were about three times as likely to draw it the way they themselves would read it, so it appeared backwards to anyone looking at them. A small task, but a telling one: feeling powerful made them forget that someone else was looking.

Worth knowing

Grant's study was of one call centre over three months. Later research has supported the idea that moderate extraversion tends to do best in sales, but the gap between types is smaller than popular retellings sometimes suggest.

Try it this week

In your next meeting, before you speak, ask yourself what the other person is thinking, not only what they're feeling. Then mirror one phrase they used, once, without making a show of it.

In plain words

Get in tune with the other person: step down from your power, think about their thinking, and mirror them gently. You don't need to be loud to do it well.

Chapter 5 · Staying afloat in an ocean of rejection

Buoyancy

In short

Anyone who tries to move people meets a lot of no. Buoyancy is the quality that keeps you afloat, and Pink breaks it into what to do before, during and after an attempt.

The big ideas

  • Before: ask, don't tell. Instead of the classic pep talk, "I can do this", ask yourself "Can I do this?". In a study by Ibrahim Senay and colleagues, people who asked themselves "Will I?" solved more anagrams than people who told themselves "I will". A question makes you rehearse the reasons and the plan.
  • During: keep positivity high. Pink draws on Barbara Fredrickson's work: positive emotions widen what we notice and help us bounce back. People who flourish have far more positive than negative moments, though not wall-to-wall cheer.
  • After: explain it well. Martin Seligman found that how you explain a setback matters. Optimists see a rejection as temporary, specific and not all about them. Pessimists see it as permanent, pervasive and personal.
  • Not blind optimism. Pink is careful: buoyancy isn't pretending everything is fine. Some negative feeling helps you learn. The aim is to stay realistic and keep going.
From the book

In the 1980s the insurance company MetLife was losing huge numbers of new sales agents, who quit after the endless rejections of selling life insurance. Martin Seligman gave applicants a test of explanatory style alongside the usual industry test. MetLife then hired a special group who had failed the industry test but scored high on optimism. Those agents outsold the pessimists among the regular hires, and the gap widened in their second year. How they explained a no turned out to matter more than the usual measure of aptitude.

Worth knowing

Pink cites a specific "positivity ratio" of about three positive emotions to one negative as a tipping point for flourishing. In 2013, the year after the book came out, the mathematical model behind that exact figure was shown to be flawed, and Fredrickson withdrew the precise number. Her broader finding, that positive emotions help people cope and recover, still stands.

Try it this week

Before your next difficult ask, sit for a moment and ask yourself, "Can I do this?" Then answer with three specific reasons why you can.

In plain words

To keep going in the face of no, question yourself before, keep your positive moments flowing during, and afterwards explain a setback as temporary, limited and not all your fault.

Chapter 6 · Finding the right problem

Clarity

In short

When information is everywhere, the valuable skill isn't giving answers, it is helping people see their situation clearly and find the problem they actually have. Then frame the offer well and give them an easy way to act.

The big ideas

  • Problem finding beats problem solving. In a classic study of art students, Jacob Getzels and Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi found that those who spent longest exploring, rearranging and changing what to draw produced the work experts rated most original, and were more successful years later. Finding the problem was the creative act.
  • Curate. When buyers can find any information themselves, the seller's value shifts to sorting it: pointing out what matters and what doesn't, and asking questions that surface the hidden need.
  • Five frames. Pink lists frames that change how an offer lands: less (fewer options), experience (what they'll do, not just own), label (what the situation is called), blemish (a small drawback stated after strengths) and potential (what something could become).
  • Give an off-ramp. Clarity isn't only about the why. People also need the how: a clear, specific next step. Without it, even persuaded people don't act.
From the book

In the 1960s Howard Leventhal at Yale gave seniors a booklet about tetanus and urged them to get a free jab at the campus health centre. Some booklets were frightening, with vivid photos; others were mild. The scary version changed what students said they'd do, but hardly anyone acted. What made the difference was a map of the campus with the health centre circled and its opening hours listed. Among those who got the map, 28 percent went for the shot; among those who didn't, 3 percent. Pink calls that kind of practical push an off-ramp.

Worth knowing

The "less" frame often comes with the famous jam study, where shoppers offered 6 jams bought far more often than those offered 24. Later reviews of many choice-overload studies found the effect is real in some settings but small or absent in others, depending on how familiar and complex the choice is.

Try it this week

Next time someone asks you for help, spend the first few minutes asking questions to find the real problem before suggesting anything. Finish by naming one specific next step.

In plain words

Help people find the real problem, show them only what matters, frame it so they see it fresh, and always tell them exactly how to take the next step.

Part three · what to do

What to Do

With the qualities in place, Pink turns to three skills. Pitch, because attention is short and the elevator pitch is out of date. Improvise, because conversations rarely go to plan. And serve, because the point of moving someone is to leave them, and the world, better off.

The idea behind this part

Have a short, sharp pitch, be ready to build on whatever comes back, and make the exchange worth it for the other person.

Chapter 7 · Six successors to the elevator pitch

Pitch

In short

The elevator pitch was built for a world of captive listeners. Attention is shorter now, so Pink offers six alternatives, each designed to get the other person to engage, not just to listen.

The big ideas

  • Why a pitch at all. A pitch isn't meant to close the deal. Its job is to start a conversation, to get the other person to join in and help shape the idea.
  • One word, a question, a rhyme. The one-word pitch distils the idea, like a campaign built on "Forward". The question pitch invites people to argue with themselves and works best when your case is strong. The rhyming pitch is easier to remember, and studies found rhyming sayings are even judged more true.
  • A subject line and a tweet. Emails get opened for utility or curiosity, and specific beats vague. The Twitter pitch has to say something worth reading in 140 characters, often by asking or engaging rather than broadcasting.
  • The Pixar pitch. Borrowed from Pixar story artist Emma Coats: Once upon a time… Every day… One day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally… It forces your idea into a story with change in it.
From the book

In the 1980 presidential debate, with the economy struggling, Ronald Reagan closed by asking voters a question: were they better off than they were four years ago? He could have listed statistics about inflation and jobs. Instead, the question made each viewer run the numbers on their own life, and reach the answer themselves. Pink uses it to show why a question can beat a statement: people believe the reasons they come up with more than the reasons they are given, provided the facts are on your side.

Try it this week

Take something you need to propose this week and write it as a one-word pitch and a question pitch. Use whichever one makes a friend ask "tell me more".

In plain words

Don't recite a speech. Write a short pitch that gets the other person thinking and talking: a word, a question, a rhyme, a subject line, a tweet or a six-line story.

Chapter 8 · Yes, and

Improvise

In short

Even the best pitch meets objections and surprises. Pink borrows three rules from improvisational theatre for handling the conversation once it leaves the script.

The big ideas

  • Hear offers. In improv, everything your partner says or does is an offer you can build on. Listening for offers, rather than waiting for your turn, changes what you hear. An objection can be an offer in disguise.
  • Say "Yes, and". "Yes, but" shuts an idea down. "Yes, and" accepts what's there and adds to it. It doesn't mean agreeing with everything; it means building rather than blocking.
  • Make your partner look good. Good improvisers try to make the other person shine. In selling, that means aiming for an outcome both sides feel good about, rather than winning the exchange.
  • Planned, then not. Preparation still matters. Improvisation is what you do with it once the other person responds in a way you didn't expect.
From the book

Pink takes part in a workshop run by Cathy Salit, a former improv performer whose company teaches businesspeople to use improvisation. In one exercise, pairs plan a party together, first by answering every suggestion with "Yes, but", then with "Yes, and". The "but" version stalls almost at once: each idea gets a reason it won't work. The "and" version builds into something lively and odd, and both partners leave it feeling more involved. The point lands quickly once you feel it in your own conversation.

Try it this week

For one day, catch yourself before you say "Yes, but". Replace it with "Yes, and" and see what happens to the conversation.

In plain words

When a conversation goes off-script, listen for what the other person is offering, build on it with "yes, and", and aim for a result that makes them look good too.

Chapter 9 · Upserve, don't upsell

Serve

In short

The best reason to move someone is to make their life better. Pink argues that service, making it personal and making it purposeful, is not only right but effective.

The big ideas

  • Make it personal. When an Israeli radiologist, Yehonatan Turner, attached a photo of the patient to their scans, radiologists wrote longer reports and caught more problems they hadn't been asked to look for. Seeing a face reminded them a person was on the other end.
  • Make it purposeful. Adam Grant found that people work harder for others than for themselves. In one hospital, a sign at the soap dispenser saying hand hygiene protects patients raised soap and gel use far more than one saying it protects you.
  • Servant selling. Robert Greenleaf's idea of servant leadership, that a leader serves first, applies to selling too. Pink's version: upserve, don't upsell. Do more for the person than they expected.
  • Two questions. Before trying to move anyone, ask: if they agree, will their life improve? And when the exchange is over, will the world be better than when you began? If not, you're doing it wrong.
From the book

At a university fundraising call centre, Grant split callers into groups before their shifts. One group read nothing new. A second read stories from former callers about how the job had helped their own careers. A third read stories from students whose scholarships had been paid for by the money these calls raised. In the following weeks, the first two groups performed about the same. The third group, who had seen whom their work helped, raised more than twice as much in pledges and money. Purpose did what self-interest didn't.

Try it this week

Before your next request, answer Pink's two questions in writing: will this person's life be better if they say yes, and will the world be? If either answer is shaky, change the offer.

In plain words

Move people for their sake, not just yours. Picture the person, remind yourself whom the work helps, and aim to leave them better off than they expected.
