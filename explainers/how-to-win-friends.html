A principle-by-principle guide to Dale Carnegie's classic

How to Win Friends,
Explained

Dale Carnegie's 1936 book is one of the best-selling self-help books ever written, and it is mostly common sense that almost nobody practises. It boils down to 30 principles in four parts: how to handle people, how to make them like you, how to win them to your way of thinking, and how to lead without causing resentment. This guide takes them one at a time, with the story Carnegie uses, how it sounds in a real conversation, and a lab where you try it.

Part one · principles 1 to 3

Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

Carnegie opens with the foundations. Before any technique for persuading or leading, he asks you to change how you treat people in general: stop attacking them, start noticing what they do well, and learn to see your requests from their side.

The idea behind this part

Nobody thinks of themselves as the villain. If you want people to change, criticism makes them defend themselves; appreciation and their own wants are what move them.

Principle 1 · Why criticism backfires

Don't Criticise, Condemn or Complain

In short

Criticism makes people defensive and resentful, and it rarely changes what they do. Try to understand why they acted as they did instead.

The big ideas

  • Everyone justifies themselves. Even people who have done real harm usually see themselves as decent and misunderstood. If they don't blame themselves, your blame just bounces off.
  • Criticism wounds pride. It puts people on the defensive and sets them looking for reasons why they were right. The resentment can last for years after the issue is forgotten.
  • Understanding is harder and better. Any fool can criticise. It takes character to ask what pressures, fears or information led someone to act that way, and that question is what opens the door to change.
  • Start with yourself. If you want to reform someone, Carnegie suggests, begin with your own habits first. It is more useful and a lot less dangerous.
From the book

Abraham Lincoln once wrote a furious letter to General Meade, who had let the defeated Confederate army escape after Gettysburg. Lincoln never sent it. It was found among his papers after his death. He had learned, Carnegie says, that sharp criticism gains nothing, and that a commander under fire might have acted no differently in Meade's place.

How it sounds

You

The report was late again. I'm guessing something got in the way this week. What happened?

Them

Honestly, I was waiting on numbers from finance. I should have flagged it.

In plain words

When you're annoyed, your first impulse is to tell the person what they did wrong. Carnegie's advice is to hold that back. People who feel attacked fight back or shut down; people who feel understood are far more willing to fix things.

Next: Sincere appreciation →
Principle 2 · The hunger to feel valued

Give Honest, Sincere Appreciation

In short

One of the deepest human desires is to feel important and appreciated. Notice what people genuinely do well and tell them, and they will give you their best.

The big ideas

  • The desire to be important. Carnegie borrows the idea from the philosopher John Dewey: beneath food, sleep and safety, people crave a feeling of significance. Much of what we do is driven by it.
  • Appreciation is not flattery. Flattery is cheap, insincere and about you: it says what people want to hear to get something. Appreciation is specific, true and about them. People can usually tell the difference.
  • It costs almost nothing. We feed bodies daily but can go weeks without a kind word for the people around us. A sincere thank-you is one of the rarest and most valuable things you can give.
  • Look for it on purpose. Stop thinking about your own achievements for a moment and pay attention to other people's good points. You will find something real to praise.
From the book

Charles Schwab, one of the first people in America to earn a million-dollar salary, said his greatest asset was not knowing steel but knowing how to bring out the best in people. His method was to be generous with praise and slow to find fault. He said he had never met anyone who did better work under criticism than under approval.

How it sounds

You

The way you rewrote the welcome email: it was clear and warm, and three customers replied to say thanks. That's rare.

Them

Oh, that's great to hear. I wasn't sure anyone read those.

In plain words

Flattery is a counterfeit coin: it may pass once, but people catch on. Real appreciation is noticing something true and specific that a person did, and saying so. It makes people feel seen, and it is free.

Next: An eager want →
Principle 3 · Talk about what they want

Arouse in the Other Person an Eager Want

In short

The only way to get someone to do something is to make them want to do it. Start from what they care about, not from what you want.

The big ideas

  • Fish like worms. You may love strawberries and cream, but when you go fishing you bait the hook with what the fish wants. People are the same: your wants interest only you.
  • See it from their side. Before you ask, think: why would this person want to do this? Henry Ford said success lies in understanding the other person's point of view as well as your own.
  • Create desire, not pressure. You can make people act out of fear or obligation for a while. They act gladly, and keep acting, when they see what is in it for them.
  • It is not manipulation if both win. The point is to find where your goal and theirs overlap, and present your request in those terms. If there is no real benefit to them, the technique won't save it.
From the book

Ralph Waldo Emerson and his son once tried to push a calf into a barn. The son pulled and Emerson pushed, and the calf dug in its heels, because they were thinking only of what they wanted. A housemaid who had grown up on a farm put her finger in the calf's mouth to suck on and gently led it inside. She thought about what the calf wanted.

How it sounds

You

You said you want to get better at presenting. Would leading the client demo on Thursday help with that?

Them

Actually, yes. I'd be up for it if you'll review my slides.

In plain words

"I need you to do this" makes the request about you. "Here's how this helps you get what you want" makes it about them. People are always tuned to their own station; to be heard, talk on it.

Next: Be interested →
Part two · principles 4 to 9

Six Ways to Make People Like You

The second part is the one most people remember. It is a practical list for being good company: be curious about others, look pleased to see them, use their names, listen properly, talk about what they care about, and let them feel they matter.

The idea behind this part

You make more friends in two months by being interested in other people than in two years of trying to get them interested in you.

Principle 4 · Curiosity makes friends

Become Genuinely Interested in Other People

In short

People are interested in themselves. Be honestly curious about their lives and they will warm to you far faster than if you try to impress them.

The big ideas

  • The dog's secret. A dog greets you with delight every time and asks nothing back. It makes friends without trying to be interesting, just by being interested.
  • Impressing pushes people away. When you try to show how clever or successful you are, people may admire you but they rarely like you more. Interest in them does the reverse.
  • Interest takes effort. Learn about what people do, ask follow-up questions, remember what they told you last time, mark their birthdays. Small attention adds up.
  • It has to be real. Fake interest is easy to spot. Carnegie means real curiosity: people are fascinating if you look, and interest that is genuine pays you back as well.
From the book

Theodore Roosevelt was adored even by his staff and servants. He knew the names of the people who worked for him, asked after their families, and once walked over to greet old White House staff by name long after he had left office. One of the staff said it was the happiest day they had had in nearly two years.

How it sounds

Them

I spent the weekend repairing an old radio.

You

No way. How did you get into that? Was it hard to find the parts?

In plain words

Want to be interesting? Be interested. Ask about the other person, listen to the answer, and ask a follow-up. Most people rarely get that kind of attention, so they remember the person who gives it.

Next: Smile →
Principle 5 · The first message you send

Smile

In short

A warm, genuine smile tells people you are glad to see them before you say a word. It is the cheapest way to start on the right foot.

The big ideas

  • Actions speak first. Your face announces your attitude before you open your mouth. A real smile says "I like you, you make me happy, I'm glad to see you".
  • Real smiles reach the eyes. A smile that is only in the mouth looks forced. The warm kind lifts the cheeks and crinkles the eyes, because it comes from actually feeling pleased.
  • You can hear a smile. Carnegie recommends smiling on the phone. It changes your tone of voice, and the listener picks it up.
  • Act happy to feel happy. If you don't feel like smiling, Carnegie suggests behaving as if you were cheerful anyway. Feelings often follow actions.
From the book

Carnegie tells of a stockbroker who rarely smiled at his wife in eighteen years of marriage. He set himself the challenge of smiling at her over breakfast, greeting the doorman with a smile and smiling at the cashier. Within two months, he said, his home was happier and his work easier than in years, and people actually sought him out.

How it sounds

You

(smiling) Morning! Good to see you. How was the trip?

Them

(smiling back) Long, but good. Thanks for asking.

In plain words

People decide how they feel about you in seconds, and your face carries most of that message. A real smile is a signal of welcome. It is contagious, it is free, and it makes the next sentence easier for everyone.

Worth knowing

Carnegie's "act cheerful and you'll feel cheerful" echoes an old idea called the facial feedback hypothesis. Later research found the effect on mood is real but small, and a famous 1988 pen-in-the-mouth study did not replicate well. The social effect of smiling at others is much better supported than the effect on your own mood.

Next: Remember names →
Principle 6 · Remember names, and use them

A Person's Name Is the Sweetest Sound

In short

A person's name is, to them, the most important sound in any language. Remembering it shows respect; forgetting it says they didn't matter.

The big ideas

  • Names carry identity. Hearing your own name tells you that someone noticed you as an individual, not as one of the crowd. It is a small, powerful compliment.
  • Forgetting costs more than you think. Getting a name wrong or forgetting it puts people at a quiet disadvantage, and they notice. It suggests you weren't paying attention.
  • Make the effort. Most people say they are bad with names. Usually they just never tried: they didn't listen, didn't repeat it, and didn't link it to anything.
  • The method. Hear it clearly, ask how it's spelled if unsure, repeat it in conversation, and link it to something about the person. Write it down afterwards if it matters.
From the book

James Farley, who ran Franklin Roosevelt's presidential campaign, was said to know around fifty thousand people by first name. When he met someone he learned their full name, family and work, and the next time he saw them, even a year later, he would greet them by name and ask after their family.

How it sounds

Them

Hi, I'm Mireille.

You

Mireille, lovely to meet you. Is that French? Mireille, how do you know Sam?

In plain words

Remembering a name is one of the simplest ways to show that someone matters. It isn't a talent; it is attention plus a little technique. Say it back, use it once or twice, and connect it to something about them.

Next: Be a good listener →
Principle 7 · Let them talk about themselves

Be a Good Listener

In short

To be thought a great conversationalist, talk less and listen more. Encourage people to talk about themselves, and really pay attention.

The big ideas

  • Listening is a compliment. Exclusive attention to what someone is saying is one of the highest forms of respect you can pay them, and it is rare.
  • Ask questions they enjoy answering. People love to talk about their work, their plans, their trips and their troubles. Give them the opening.
  • Don't wait for your turn. Many people "listen" while planning what to say next, then jump in. Real listening means following their thread, not steering back to yours.
  • Angry people need to be heard. Carnegie notes that even furious complainers often calm down once someone listens patiently and fully to the whole complaint.
From the book

At a dinner party, Carnegie met a botanist and spent hours asking him about plants and gardens, hardly saying anything about himself. At the end of the evening the botanist told the host that Carnegie was a most stimulating companion and a wonderful conversationalist. All Carnegie had done was listen with real interest.

How it sounds

Them

We finally moved house last month.

You

How's it going so far? What made you choose that area?

In plain words

Most people would rather talk than listen, so a good listener is precious. Ask, follow up, and resist turning the story into one about you. You will be remembered as excellent company while having said very little.

Next: Their interests →
Principle 8 · Find what lights them up

Talk in Terms of the Other Person's Interests

In short

The road to someone's heart is to talk about the things they treasure most. Find out what they care about and start there.

The big ideas

  • Everyone has a favourite subject. For one person it is sailing, for another their kids, their football club or their garden. On that subject they open up.
  • Do some homework. Before meeting someone important, learn a little about what they care about. It shows respect and gives you somewhere to start.
  • It pays both ways. Carnegie notes that people who do this find that they learn a great deal and widen their own lives, not just win friends.
  • Your agenda can wait. Leading with your interests puts people off. Leading with theirs creates goodwill, and your topic often comes up naturally later.
From the book

Visitors to Theodore Roosevelt were amazed at how much he knew about their field, whether they were cowboys, politicians or diplomats. Carnegie explains why: whenever Roosevelt expected a visitor, he stayed up the night before reading about the subject he knew they cared most about.

How it sounds

You

I saw your team won the regional robotics prize. What did the robot have to do?

Them

Oh, it's a great story. Have you got five minutes?

In plain words

Before you talk about what you want, find out what the other person cares about. People listen happily to anything connected to their own interests, and switch off quickly when the subject is only you.

Next: Feel important →
Principle 9 · The golden rule, applied

Make the Other Person Feel Important, Sincerely

In short

Almost everyone you meet feels superior to you in some way. Find that way, honour it sincerely, and you will win them over.

The big ideas

  • Treat people as you'd like to be treated. Carnegie calls this the most important law of human conduct: always make the other person feel important.
  • Everyone knows something you don't. Each person you meet is your superior in some area. Recognising that, genuinely, is the key to their respect.
  • Use the small courtesies. "I'm sorry to trouble you", "Would you mind?", "Thank you". These little phrases tell people that they matter.
  • Sincerely, or not at all. The rule only works if you mean it. Hollow praise is noticed and resented.
From the book

Waiting in a long post office queue, Carnegie watched a bored clerk weighing letters and set himself a challenge: find something about this man he could honestly admire. He noticed the clerk's fine head of hair and told him so. The clerk lit up, and Carnegie walked away knowing he had made someone's day without wanting anything in return.

How it sounds

You

You always know the shortcut in this software. Could you show me how you do the report so fast?

Them

Sure, it took me ages to figure out. Pull up a chair.

In plain words

People blossom when they feel valued. Look for what a person truly does well and recognise it, with no strings attached. It is the simplest way to make someone glad they met you.

Next: Avoid arguments →
Part three · principles 10 to 21

How to Win People to Your Way of Thinking

The longest part is about disagreement. Carnegie argues that you almost never change a mind by proving it wrong. Instead you keep the conversation friendly, find what you agree on, let the other person talk, and help them reach the idea themselves.

The idea behind this part

A person convinced against their will is of the same opinion still. Your job is to make agreeing feel like their own choice.

Principle 10 · You can't win an argument

The Only Way to Win an Argument Is to Avoid It

In short

If you lose an argument, you lose. If you win it, you still lose: the other person feels humiliated and holds their view tighter. Avoid them.

The big ideas

  • Winning creates resentment. Prove someone wrong in public and you have hurt their pride. They may go quiet, but they won't agree with you, and they won't like you.
  • Welcome the disagreement. A different view might be telling you something you missed. Thank people for raising it.
  • Control your first reaction. Our natural response to being contradicted is to defend. Pause, listen first, and look for points where you agree.
  • Promise to think about it. Be honest about where you might be wrong, and genuinely agree to consider their ideas. It takes the heat out of the room.
From the book

At a dinner, a guest attributed a famous line to the Bible. Carnegie knew it was Shakespeare and said so, rather smugly. His friend, a Shakespeare scholar, kicked him under the table and said the guest was right. On the way home he explained: the guest hadn't asked for a correction, so why prove him wrong and make him dislike you? Always avoid the acute angle.

How it sounds

Them

Electric cars are a total scam.

You

I can see why some people feel that way. What's put you off them?

In plain words

Arguments are rarely about facts; they are about pride. Once someone has dug in, every new fact you throw is taken as an attack. Step back from the fight and you leave room for them to change their mind later, on their own terms.

Next: Never "you're wrong" →
Principle 11 · Disagree without attacking

Show Respect for Their Opinions. Never Say "You're Wrong"

In short

Telling someone flatly that they're wrong is a challenge to their intelligence. They will fight back, not reconsider. Show respect and soften your language.

The big ideas

  • "You're wrong" starts a fight. It insults their judgement and pride, and you have made it nearly impossible for them to change their mind without losing face.
  • Leave room for your own mistakes. Saying "I may be wrong, I frequently am, let's look at the facts" is disarming. Nobody will object to it.
  • Drop the absolute words. Benjamin Franklin trained himself to avoid "certainly" and "undoubtedly" and to say "I imagine" or "it appears to me". He found people far readier to agree.
  • Teach without seeming to teach. Present new ideas as things the other person may have forgotten, not as corrections.
From the book

As a young man, Benjamin Franklin was blunt and argumentative, until an old Quaker friend told him that his habit of contradicting everyone had made him unbearable company. Franklin took it to heart. He banned himself from flat contradictions, and later credited that change with much of his influence in public life.

How it sounds

Them

The meeting is definitely on Tuesday.

You

I might have this wrong, but I thought the email said Wednesday. Shall we check it?

In plain words

You can disagree without telling someone they are wrong. Leave room for your own error, ask to look at the facts together, and avoid words like "obviously". People are more willing to change a view they haven't been forced to defend.

Next: Admit it quickly →
Principle 12 · Beat them to the criticism

If You Are Wrong, Admit It Quickly and Emphatically

In short

When you have made a mistake, own it before anyone else points it out. Say it frankly and fully. It disarms criticism and earns respect.

The big ideas

  • Say what they were going to say. If you criticise yourself first, there's nothing left for the other person to attack. Very often they respond by going easy on you.
  • It feels better. Admitting a mistake clears the air. Defending an error drains energy and keeps the conflict going.
  • Any fool can defend a mistake. Most people do. Admitting one gives you a sense of dignity and sets you apart.
  • Be quick, and be clear. A half-apology wrapped in excuses doesn't work. Name the mistake, its effect, and what you'll do about it.
From the book

Carnegie often walked his small dog, Rex, unleashed and unmuzzled in a park, until a mounted policeman warned him it was illegal. The next time he was caught, Carnegie spoke first: he said he was guilty, had no excuse, and deserved a fine. The policeman softened at once, said a little dog could do no harm, and suggested he simply walk Rex over the hill where nobody would see.

How it sounds

You

I got this wrong, and it's on me. I sent the old price list. I've already emailed the right one and called the two clients.

Them

Thanks for jumping on it. These things happen.

In plain words

When you are in the wrong, the fastest way out is straight through: admit it, fully and quickly, before you are accused. It costs a little pride and saves a great deal of conflict.

Next: Begin friendly →
Principle 13 · A drop of honey

Begin in a Friendly Way

In short

If you start a conversation angry or hostile, people dig in. Start friendly, and they are far more likely to come round to your view.

The big ideas

  • Honey catches more flies. Lincoln used the old saying: a drop of honey catches more flies than a gallon of gall. Win someone over as a friend first.
  • Hostility closes doors. An angry opening may feel satisfying, but it puts the other person on the defensive before the real topic comes up.
  • Gentleness is strength. A calm, friendly approach in a tense situation shows confidence and self-control, not weakness.
  • Say what you share. Opening with what you have in common, or with appreciation, changes the tone of everything that follows.
From the book

In Aesop's fable, the sun and the wind argue about which is stronger, and test it on a man in a coat. The wind blows hard, and the man only wraps his coat tighter. Then the sun shines gently and warmly, and soon the man takes his coat off. Carnegie uses the fable to make his point: kindness persuades where force fails.

How it sounds

You

Thanks for meeting me. I know you've had a busy month. I'd like to sort out the noise issue in a way that works for both of us.

Them

Sure. Honestly I didn't realise it was carrying that far.

In plain words

Start conversations, especially difficult ones, the way you would want them started with you: warmly, with respect. Once people feel attacked, they stop listening and start defending.

Next: Yes, yes →
Principle 14 · Build momentum with agreement

Get the Other Person Saying "Yes, Yes" Immediately

In short

Begin by stressing the things you agree on, and get the other person saying "yes" early. Once they have said "no", their pride makes them stick to it.

The big ideas

  • A "no" is hard to take back. Once someone has said no, they feel they must defend it. Avoid giving them reason to say it early on.
  • Agreement builds momentum. Each "yes" puts the listener in an open, cooperative frame of mind and makes the next one easier.
  • Start with shared goals. Point out that you both want the same end result. The disagreement is usually only about the method.
  • Socrates did it first. He asked questions his opponents had to agree with, one after another, until they found themselves accepting a conclusion they would have rejected at the start.
From the book

Carnegie describes a bank teller faced with a customer who refused to fill in parts of an account form. Instead of insisting, she asked whether he would want the bank to be able to reach his next of kin if something happened to him. He said yes. Would he like his money to go to them? Yes. By the end he gladly completed the whole form.

How it sounds

You

We both want the launch to go well, right? And neither of us wants a weekend crunch?

Them

Definitely not.

You

So would freezing new features on Friday help us get there?

In plain words

Don't lead with the point of disagreement. Start where you agree, and get a few honest yeses. People who have been agreeing with you are already in the habit, and the decision feels like the next step, not a reversal.

Use it well

The questions must be honest, things the person really does agree with. A string of leading questions designed to trap someone feels like a sales trick, and it is.

Next: Let them talk →
Principle 15 · Talk less, learn more

Let the Other Person Do a Great Deal of the Talking

In short

Most people trying to persuade talk too much. Let the other person talk it out: they know more about their needs than you do, and they want to be heard.

The big ideas

  • They know their problems. Ask questions and let them explain. You will learn what matters to them, which is exactly what you need to persuade them.
  • Don't interrupt. It's tempting to jump in when you disagree. Don't. They won't hear you while they still have things to say.
  • Let them shine. Even friends would rather talk about their own achievements than hear about yours. Be modest; let them enjoy telling their story.
  • Listen generously. Encourage them to express their ideas fully, and be patient. Being heard is often what someone needs before they can agree.
From the book

Carnegie tells of a salesman pitching car upholstery to a large manufacturer. He had laryngitis and could barely whisper, so the company president did the talking for him, praising the samples to his colleagues. The salesman won the contract, and realised how much he had been losing by talking too much.

How it sounds

Them

We've tried three agencies and none of them got our brand.

You

What did they get wrong?

Them

Where do I start…

In plain words

The more someone talks, the more you learn about what they want, and the more ownership they feel. Ask good questions and leave pauses. The conversation will usually lead them to your point.

Next: Their idea →
Principle 16 · Plant a seed, let it grow

Let the Other Person Feel the Idea Is Theirs

In short

People trust ideas they discover themselves far more than ideas handed to them. Suggest, ask, and let them reach the conclusion.

The big ideas

  • Nobody likes to be sold to. People prefer to feel they are buying, not being sold, and acting on their own ideas, not following orders.
  • Ask for their input. Asking what someone thinks, or how they would solve a problem, often leads them to the same answer you had in mind.
  • Give up the credit. Letting someone else own the idea means it actually gets done. The credit matters less than the outcome.
  • Make suggestions, not demands. Offer hints and options, and let people think them through.
From the book

Carnegie tells of an X-ray equipment salesman who couldn't get a hospital to look at his machine. A rival company changed tactic: it wrote to the head of radiology saying its new equipment wasn't perfect yet and asking his expert opinion on how to improve it. Flattered, the doctor came to inspect it, and soon decided by himself that the hospital should buy it.

How it sounds

You

Customers keep getting lost at checkout. What do you think we should try?

Them

Maybe we cut it down to one page?

You

That could really work. Want to sketch it?

In plain words

Rather than presenting a finished plan and asking for approval, describe the problem and ask for ideas. When people get there themselves, they will defend the idea and carry it out far better than if you had insisted.

Next: Their point of view →
Principle 17 · There's a reason they think that

Try Honestly to See Things From the Other Person's Point of View

In short

People who seem wrong usually have reasons that make sense to them. Find those reasons, and you have the key to their behaviour.

The big ideas

  • Don't condemn; understand. Fools condemn. The wise try to understand, and only the tolerant and patient even bother.
  • Ask: how would I feel? Put yourself in their shoes. If you were them, with their information and worries, how would you see this?
  • It saves time and irritation. Once you understand the cause, you stop being annoyed at the effect, and you can solve the actual problem.
  • Prepare before you ask. Carnegie quotes a business-school dean who would rather walk outside an office for two hours than go into an interview without a clear idea of what the other person would say and why.
From the book

Carnegie liked to walk in a park near his home, and was upset that boys kept lighting fires there. At first he warned them sternly, threatening the police, and they sulked and went back as soon as he left. Later he tried a different approach: he said he used to love campfires as a boy too, but asked them to rake the leaves away and put the fire out properly. They agreed happily.

How it sounds

You

If I were in your position, I'd probably be worried about the cost too. What would make this feel safe for you?

Them

Honestly, a trial month would help.

In plain words

Before trying to change someone's mind, figure out why they hold their view. Their reasons, not yours, are what your argument needs to address. Often, once you see them, you'll find a better solution too.

Next: Be sympathetic →
Principle 18 · "I don't blame you for feeling that way"

Be Sympathetic With the Other Person's Ideas and Desires

In short

Most people are hungry for sympathy. Tell them you understand why they feel as they do, and arguments melt away.

The big ideas

  • A magic phrase. Carnegie offers a line along the lines of: "I don't blame you for feeling that way. If I were you, I'd feel the same." It calms almost anyone.
  • It is honestly true. If you had their background, experience and worries, you would likely feel as they do. Saying so isn't a trick; it is accurate.
  • Sympathy stops escalation. An angry person expects a fight. Real sympathy takes the fight out of their sails.
  • It works with complaints. People who complain often just want someone to understand how frustrating the experience was before they discuss a fix.
From the book

On a radio broadcast, Carnegie made a mistake about where the author Louisa May Alcott had lived. He was flooded with angry letters, one especially furious from a woman in Philadelphia. He was tempted to write back telling her what he thought, but instead he called her, thanked her, admitted the error, and told her she was quite right to feel as she did. By the end she was apologising to him.

How it sounds

Them

This is the third time my delivery has been late. It's ridiculous.

You

I don't blame you for being fed up. I would be too. Let's get it sorted for good.

In plain words

When someone is upset, the most useful first move is to agree with their feelings, even if not with their facts. Once people feel understood, they become reasonable remarkably quickly.

Next: Nobler motives →
Principle 19 · People like to be seen as good

Appeal to the Nobler Motives

In short

People usually have two reasons for doing something: one that sounds good, and the real one. Appeal to the good one, and they will often live up to it.

The big ideas

  • Everyone wants to be honest and fair. Most people see themselves as decent, reliable and fair. Assume they are, and treat them that way.
  • Appeal to fairness, honour, care. Ask people to act in a way that fits the best version of themselves, and many will.
  • Assume the best in them. Treat a customer as honest and they usually prove you right. Treat them as a cheat and they feel justified in behaving like one.
  • It doesn't work on everyone. Carnegie admits that some people won't respond, but argues it works far more often than suspicion does.
From the book

When a newspaper printed a photograph that Lord Northcliffe, the British press baron, didn't want published, he didn't threaten anyone. He wrote to the editor: please don't publish that picture any more, my mother doesn't like it. He appealed to a feeling everyone shares, respect for one's mother, and the request was honoured.

How it sounds

You

I know you're someone who cares about doing things properly. Would you be willing to double-check the figures before Friday?

Them

Of course. I'd hate to send it with errors.

In plain words

Rather than threatening or bargaining, appeal to someone's sense of fairness, honesty or kindness. People like to be the good person in the story, and usually step up when you assume they are.

Next: Dramatise →
Principle 20 · The truth, with showmanship

Dramatise Your Ideas

In short

Simply stating facts often isn't enough. Make your idea vivid, interesting and visual, the way films, adverts and shop windows do.

The big ideas

  • Facts alone are forgettable. People hear dozens of claims a day. To be noticed and remembered, an idea needs to be seen or felt, not just heard.
  • Show, don't tell. A demonstration, a striking image, an object put on the table: these do more than a page of statistics.
  • Make it concrete. Translate numbers into something people can picture, like a stack of coins, a full stadium, or hours of their own week.
  • It must still be true. Showmanship is in the presentation, not in exaggerating the facts.
From the book

Carnegie gives the example of a shop that wanted to sell rat poison. It set up a window display with two live rats. People stopped to stare, and sales jumped. The poison hadn't changed; the way it was shown had.

How it sounds

You

Instead of saying we lose time in meetings: last month we spent 412 hours in them. That's ten full working weeks.

Them

Wait, really? Okay, let's look at that.

In plain words

Your idea competes with everything else for attention. Present it in a way that people can see, picture or experience. A small, honest bit of theatre can make the difference between nodding along and acting.

Next: Throw down a challenge →
Principle 21 · The desire to excel

Throw Down a Challenge

In short

When nothing else works, appeal to people's competitive spirit. Most people want to prove themselves and to excel; a fair challenge brings that out.

The big ideas

  • People like to win. The desire to excel, to prove your worth, is a strong motivator, often stronger than money.
  • Make it about the game. Frame a task as a contest or a test of skill, and people bring energy that orders never produce.
  • Challenge, not pressure. A challenge is an invitation: "I wonder if it can be done." Pressure is a threat. The first motivates; the second creates fear.
  • Make it meaningful. The best challenges are worth meeting: a chance to show what you can do in work you care about.
From the book

Charles Schwab had a steel mill that never hit its targets. One evening he asked the day shift how many heats they had poured that day. They told him six, so he chalked a big "6" on the floor and left. The night shift saw it, asked what it meant, and poured seven, rubbing out his number and chalking their own. By morning the day shift had beaten them. Soon that mill was among the most productive in the company.

How it sounds

You

The record for clearing the support queue is two days. I'm not sure it can be beaten, but if any team can do it, it's this one.

Them

Two days? We'll do it in one and a half.

In plain words

Sometimes the best motivator is not reward or approval but a chance to prove what you can do. Set a meaningful, achievable challenge, and people will often push themselves further than any instruction could.

Next: Begin with praise →
Part four · principles 22 to 30

Be a Leader

The last part is for anyone who has to give feedback: managers, parents, teachers, team captains. Carnegie's nine rules show how to point out a problem so that people want to fix it, rather than resent you for mentioning it.

The idea behind this part

Feedback lands when the person keeps their dignity. Start warm, be indirect about the fault, and make improving feel easy and worthwhile.

Principle 22 · Lather before you shave

Begin With Praise and Honest Appreciation

In short

It is much easier to hear about a problem after hearing about what you did well. Start feedback with honest praise.

The big ideas

  • The barber's method. A barber puts lather on before shaving. Praise first makes the hard part easier to take.
  • It must be honest. Praise that is invented to soften the blow will be seen through, and makes your feedback seem manipulative.
  • It lowers the guard. People who feel recognised are more open to hearing what could be better.
  • Then be clear. Starting with praise isn't a way to dodge the problem. Once you have shown goodwill, say what needs to change.
From the book

Calvin Coolidge once told his secretary that she was wearing a pretty dress and that she was a charming young woman. She was so surprised she blushed. Then he added that he was sure she could be just as careful with punctuation in her letters from now on. His method was blunt, Carnegie says, but the psychology was sound.

How it sounds

You

Your research on this is really thorough, and the charts are so clear. The next step is cutting it to ten slides for the board.

Them

Got it. I'll trim it today.

In plain words

Before discussing what needs to change, recognise what is going well, honestly. People who feel valued can take criticism without feeling attacked, and are far more likely to act on it.

Next: Mistakes indirectly →
Principle 23 · Watch out for "but"

Call Attention to People's Mistakes Indirectly

In short

Pointing at a mistake head-on stings. Point to it indirectly, and swap "but" for "and" so your praise isn't cancelled out.

The big ideas

  • "But" erases what came before. "You did really well, but…" makes people hear the praise as a set-up. They brace for the real message.
  • Use "and" instead. "You did really well, and if you keep working on X, it'll be even better" keeps the praise sincere and makes the change feel like progress.
  • Indirect can be stronger. Setting an example, asking a question, or making a gentle hint lets people correct themselves without being shamed.
  • Especially for sensitive people. People who resent direct criticism often respond well to an indirect approach.
From the book

Charles Schwab once walked through one of his steel mills at noon and saw a group of men smoking right under a sign that said No Smoking. He didn't point at the sign. He handed each of them a cigar and said he'd appreciate it if they smoked these outside. They knew they had broken the rule, and they admired him for not making a scene.

How it sounds

You

The design is great, and if the button were bigger, people would find it even faster.

Them

Good point. I'll bump it up.

In plain words

Most feedback is made of praise and a correction, glued together with "but". The word tells people to ignore the first part. Join them with "and", or simply point to the improvement indirectly, and both halves land.

Next: Your mistakes first →
Principle 24 · I've been there too

Talk About Your Own Mistakes First

In short

Admitting your own past mistakes before pointing out someone else's makes criticism easier to accept. It shows you're on their side.

The big ideas

  • Humility opens ears. People accept correction more easily from someone who admits to having struggled with the same thing.
  • It isn't "us vs them". Sharing your own mistakes turns feedback into advice from someone who has been there, not a judgement from above.
  • It shows growth is possible. Your own example proves that the mistake is normal and fixable.
  • Keep it real. Don't invent mistakes. Use real ones, and keep the story short so the focus returns to them.
From the book

Carnegie hired his young niece Josephine as his secretary. She made the mistakes any nineteen-year-old new to the job would make, and he was tempted to criticise her. Then he reminded himself that he had made far worse mistakes at her age. So he began his corrections by saying that he had done the same kind of thing, and only then suggested a better way. She improved quickly.

How it sounds

You

When I started, I used to send drafts to clients without a second read. Cost me a big account once. It might be worth a quick check before this one goes out.

Them

Oof. Okay, I'll do a final pass.

In plain words

"I made the same mistake" is one of the gentlest ways to correct someone. It shows that you are not judging them and that the fix is possible. People find it far easier to accept advice from a fellow traveller.

Next: Ask, don't order →
Principle 25 · Suggestions, not commands

Ask Questions Instead of Giving Direct Orders

In short

Nobody likes taking orders. Ask questions and make suggestions instead, and people will cooperate more willingly and bring their own ideas.

The big ideas

  • Orders create resentment. Even when the instruction is reasonable, being ordered around makes people feel small.
  • Questions invite thinking. "What do you think of this?" or "Do you think this would work?" give people room to improve the idea.
  • People own what they help create. Asking draws out ideas and commitment, and people take more care over a decision they shaped.
  • It saves face, too. A question lets someone correct a mistake without feeling caught out.
From the book

Carnegie writes of a businessman, Owen D. Young, who was said never to give anyone a direct order. He would say things like "You might consider this" or "Do you think this would work?" After dictating a letter, he would ask his assistant what she thought of it. He let people do things themselves and learn from their mistakes.

How it sounds

You

What do you think about sending the update before lunch? That would give the client the afternoon to reply.

Them

Makes sense. I'll get it out by eleven.

In plain words

Swap commands for questions: "Could we…?", "What if…?", "How would you…?" People do better work, with more care, when they feel they chose to do it.

Next: Let them save face →
Principle 26 · Protect their dignity

Let the Other Person Save Face

In short

Even when you are completely right, humiliating someone helps no one. Give people a way out that protects their dignity.

The big ideas

  • Being right isn't the point. Crushing someone in front of others leaves wounds that last, and you lose their goodwill even if you win the point.
  • Correct in private. Praise in public, criticise in private. A few minutes alone keeps respect intact.
  • Give them a reason. Point out what was understandable about their mistake, such as missing information, pressure or bad instructions.
  • Let them retreat gracefully. If someone has to back down, make it easy, so they don't feel they lost.
From the book

General Electric once had to move Charles Steinmetz, a brilliant but sensitive engineer, out of a department head role he wasn't suited for. Instead of demoting him, the company gave him a new title, Consulting Engineer, which matched the work he was genuinely great at, and quietly let someone else run the department. He was happy, and nobody lost face.

How it sounds

You

The data you had was from last quarter, so anyone would have reached the same conclusion. Let's rerun it with the new numbers.

Them

Ah, that explains it. Thanks.

In plain words

When someone is wrong, the goal is to fix the problem, not to make them feel small. A few kind words, a private conversation or a good reason for the mistake lets people correct course without resenting you.

Next: Praise every step →
Principle 27 · Hearty approval, lavish praise

Praise the Slightest Improvement, and Every Improvement

In short

Praise every small step forward, sincerely and generously. People repeat what gets noticed, so notice the progress.

The big ideas

  • Reward the behaviour you want. Animal trainers know it: reward a small step in the right direction and it gets repeated. People work the same way.
  • Don't wait for perfection. If you only praise the finished result, you will rarely praise, and people may give up before they get there.
  • Be specific. General praise ("good job") is pleasant but empty. Saying exactly what improved shows you were paying attention.
  • Praise inspires. Many people can trace a turning point to a single word of encouragement at the right moment.
From the book

Carnegie describes an old friend, Pete Barlow, who trained dogs and ponies for a travelling show. Every time an animal made even the smallest progress, Pete patted it, praised it and gave it a treat. Carnegie wondered why we don't use the same common sense with people, praising small improvements instead of only criticising mistakes.

How it sounds

You

Your last two invoices went out on time. That's a real improvement, and it makes the end of month so much easier.

Them

Thanks! I set up a reminder; it seems to be working.

In plain words

Small steps add up to big changes, but only if people keep taking them. Catch someone doing a little better and say so, specifically. It makes the next step more likely.

Worth knowing

Later research on praise refines Carnegie's point. Praise works best when it is specific and tied to effort or a strategy ("you planned this out step by step") rather than fixed traits ("you're so clever"). Praise for traits can make people avoid challenges in case they fail.

Next: A fine reputation →
Principle 28 · People rise to expectations

Give the Other Person a Fine Reputation to Live Up To

In short

Treat someone as if they already have the quality you want them to develop. Most people will work hard not to disappoint that belief.

The big ideas

  • Assume a virtue. If you want someone to improve, act as if they already have that trait. "I know you're someone who…" sets a standard to live up to.
  • People rise to your expectations. Tell someone they are careless and they may give up trying. Tell them you know they take pride in their work and they often begin to.
  • Make it public and positive. A good reputation, spoken in front of others, becomes something a person wants to protect.
  • Believe it a little. This only works if you can sincerely see the trait in them, even partly.
From the book

Carnegie tells of a mechanic whose work had slipped. Instead of scolding him, his manager called him in and told him he had been a fine mechanic for years, the kind other people looked up to, and that he was sure he could get back to that standard. The mechanic didn't want to lose that reputation. His work quickly improved.

How it sounds

You

You're always the one who catches the details others miss. Could you be the last pair of eyes on this before it goes out?

Them

Of course. I'll go through it properly.

In plain words

If you want someone to be more reliable, treat them as reliable. People tend to become what they believe others expect of them, especially when the expectation is kind and sincere.

Next: Make it easy →
Principle 29 · Small fixes, big confidence

Use Encouragement. Make the Fault Seem Easy to Correct

In short

If you tell someone they have no talent for something, they stop trying. Make the problem sound easy to fix, and show you believe they can do it.

The big ideas

  • Discouragement kills effort. Telling someone they are hopeless at something gives them every reason to give up.
  • Make it sound simple. Present the mistake as one small, specific thing to change, not a fundamental flaw.
  • Show your faith. Express confidence that they can do it, and point to what they already do well.
  • Break it into steps. One easy improvement at a time is much less daunting than a list of everything wrong.
From the book

Carnegie recounts a friend who, at forty, was learning to dance and had been told by one teacher that everything he did was wrong. He lost all interest. A second teacher told him he had a natural sense of rhythm and that his mistakes were small and easy to fix. He knew she might be flattering him a little, but he danced better because of it, and kept going.

How it sounds

You

Your talk was really strong. The only fix is slowing down in the first minute, and that's an easy one.

Them

Okay, I can do that. Maybe I'll pause after the opening line.

In plain words

How you describe a problem shapes whether people try to fix it. "You're terrible at this" stops them. "You're nearly there; just change this one thing" keeps them going.

Next: Happy to do it →
Principle 30 · A request they're glad to say yes to

Make the Other Person Happy About Doing What You Suggest

In short

Frame your request so the other person sees what's in it for them and feels good about doing it, not merely obliged.

The big ideas

  • Be sincere. Don't promise anything you can't deliver. Focus on real benefits, not invented ones.
  • Know exactly what you want. Be clear about what you are asking before you ask. Vague requests are easy to resent.
  • Think about their wants. Ask what the other person really wants, and what benefit they will get from doing what you suggest.
  • Match the two. When you make the request, phrase it so they see how it connects to what they want. Titles, recognition and responsibility can mean a lot.
From the book

Napoleon created the Legion of Honour and handed out thousands of crosses to his soldiers, made generals "marshals", and called his army the "Grand Army". When he was criticised for giving "toys" to battle-hardened veterans, he replied that people are ruled by such toys. Carnegie notes that recognition and titles still work, when they are sincere.

How it sounds

You

You know the system better than anyone. Would you lead the training for the new starters? It would put you in front of the whole leadership team.

Them

I'd like that, actually.

In plain words

The best requests leave people feeling good about saying yes. Be honest, know what you are asking, and connect the request to something the other person genuinely wants.

About the editions

The original 1936 book had two extra parts, on writing letters that get results and on making home life happier. The 1981 revision, the one most people read now, dropped them and updated the examples. This guide follows the 30 principles in the revised edition.

An independent study guide to How to Win Friends and Influence People by Dale Carnegie (1936, revised 1981). The explanations, retold stories, example conversations and labs are written in our own words; the short names of the principles are the book's. Labs that show percentages are illustrative models, not research data.
