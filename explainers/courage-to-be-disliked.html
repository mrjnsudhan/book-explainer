A night-by-night guide to Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga's book

The Courage to Be Disliked,
Explained

A restless young man hears of a philosopher on the edge of town who claims that people can change, that the world is simple, and that anyone can be happy. He decides to visit and prove him wrong. The Courage to Be Disliked is the record of their five nights of argument, and through it, an introduction to the psychology of Alfred Adler. This guide follows the five nights in seventeen short sections, each with the youth's objection and a lab where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

Your past doesn't decide your life, other people's opinions are their business, and happiness is the feeling of being useful, available right now.

The first night

Deny Trauma

A young man who thinks the world is a messy, unfair place visits a philosopher who claims people can change and anyone can be happy. The youth means to tear the idea apart. The first night is about Adler's most startling claim: your past does not decide who you are. The goals you hold now do.

The idea behind this night

We are not driven by what happened to us. We choose the meaning we give it, and we can choose again.

Chapter 1 · The world is as simple as you see it

Why People Can Change

In short

The philosopher's opening claim is that the world is simple and life is too. It only looks complicated because of the way we see it. Change the way you look, and the same world looks different. That's why change is possible for anyone.

The big ideas

  • Subjective worlds. Nobody lives in the objective world. We each live in a world we give meaning to. Two people can stand in the same rain and one calls it miserable, the other calls it fresh.
  • Adler, the forgotten giant. Alfred Adler stands next to Freud and Jung as one of the founders of modern psychology, but his ideas are less famous. The book's philosopher has built his thinking on Adler's individual psychology.
  • Not easy, but simple. Simple doesn't mean easy. Seeing things differently takes courage, because it means giving up the old view you've grown comfortable in.
From the dialogue

The philosopher's first example is water from a well. Drawn up all year round, it stays at about the same temperature, around 18 degrees. Yet in summer it feels cool on your hands and in winter it feels warm. The thermometer doesn't change. You do. The youth grumbles that this is only a trick of the senses. That, says the philosopher, is exactly the point: we are always living inside how things feel to us.

The youth objects

YouthIsn't the world just objectively unfair?

PhilosopherThe world is the same for everyone. What differs is the lens. If you can't swap the world, you can still swap the lens.

In plain words

Most of what feels like a fact about the world is a fact about how you're seeing it. That's good news: the world is hard to change, but your view of it isn't fixed.

Chapter 2 · Goals, not causes

Trauma Does Not Exist

In short

Most psychology asks what caused you to be this way. Adler asks what you're using it for. He called the first view etiology and the second teleology. In his view, we aren't pushed by past events. We are pulled by present goals, and we pick experiences out of the past that suit them.

The big ideas

  • Causes or goals. "I can't go out because I'm anxious" puts the cause first. Adler flips it: "I create anxiety so that I don't have to go out." The anxiety is real, but it serves a purpose.
  • Meaning is chosen. The book's line is that we are not determined by our experiences but by the meaning we give them. The same childhood can make one person bitter and another kind.
  • No excuse in the past. If the past decided everything, nobody could change and therapy would be pointless. Denying trauma is not denying pain. It's refusing to let the past be the last word.
From the dialogue

The youth's test case is a friend who has shut himself in his room for years. He wants to go out, but as soon as he tries, his heart pounds and his hands shake. Surely, says the youth, something in his past caused this. The philosopher suggests a harder reading. Staying in means nobody can judge him, nobody can reject him, and his parents keep worrying about him. The anxiety isn't the cause of staying in. It is what he produces so that he can stay in.

The youth objects

YouthSo you're saying my friend's suffering is fake?

PhilosopherNot at all. His fear is real. The question is what job it's doing. Find that, and you've found what can change.

Worth knowing

Adler's claim is a philosophical stance, not a medical one. Trauma and post-traumatic stress are well studied today, and they respond to treatment. The useful part of the idea is the question it asks: what am I getting out of staying stuck?

In plain words

When you catch yourself saying "I can't, because of what happened", try asking "what does not doing it get me?" The answer is usually safety from being judged. Knowing that gives you a choice.

Next: Anger is a tool →
Chapter 3 · Emotions serve goals too

People Fabricate Anger

In short

We say anger "took over". Adler says we use it. Anger is a quick way to make someone back down. The proof is how fast it disappears when it stops being useful.

The big ideas

  • Anger has a purpose. Shouting at someone isn't a loss of control. It's a shortcut to getting them to give in, without the effort of explaining.
  • You can switch it off. If anger truly controlled us, we couldn't drop it in a second. Yet we do it all the time when the situation changes.
  • Words over force. Anger is a means of communication, but a poor one. Anything it achieves can be achieved more respectfully with words.
From the dialogue

The youth admits he recently shouted at a waiter who spilt coffee on his best jacket. He just lost it, he says. The philosopher offers another scene. A mother is screaming at her daughter when the phone rings. It's the child's teacher. Instantly her voice turns polite and calm. When she hangs up, the shouting starts again. Anger she couldn't control? She turned it off and on like a tap.

The youth objects

YouthI couldn't help it. It just came over me.

PhilosopherThen how did the mother stop the instant the teacher called? Anger is a tool you pick up. You can choose another one.

In plain words

Next time you feel anger rising, ask what you want the other person to do. Then try asking for it in words. You'll usually get more of it, and lose less.

Next: You choose your lifestyle →
Chapter 4 · Lifestyle, and the fear of change

Unhappiness Is Something You Choose

In short

Adler uses the word lifestyle for your whole way of seeing yourself and the world. You picked it, usually around age ten, and you can pick again. People stay unhappy not because they can't change, but because the familiar is safer than the unknown.

The big ideas

  • Nobody chooses harm. Following Socrates, the philosopher says no one wants what's bad for them. Even unhappiness is chosen because, in some way, it seemed good: safe, predictable, excusing.
  • Lifestyle is a choice. Your personality isn't set in stone. It's a lifestyle, and it can be traded for another one.
  • Change takes courage. Keeping your current lifestyle is easy. It's like an old car you know how to drive. A new one is scary, so you keep the old car and complain about it.
  • Decided here and now. Whatever happened before, your life from now on is decided by what you do here and now.
From the dialogue

The youth confesses he'd love to be like his friend Y, who is cheerful and gets on with everyone. Why can't he just be Y? The philosopher says the problem isn't that he can't be Y. It's that he has chosen not to be happy as himself. Wanting to be someone else is a way of not starting. He also mentions a young man who dreams of being a novelist but never finishes a story. As long as nothing's submitted, he can keep thinking he'd be brilliant "if only he had time". Finishing would mean finding out.

The youth objects

YouthIf I could change, I would. Why would anyone choose to be unhappy?

PhilosopherBecause the unhappiness you know is safer than the happiness you don't. You aren't lacking ability. You're lacking courage.

In plain words

If you've wanted to change for years and haven't, it's not that you're broken. Your current way of living pays you something, usually safety. Name it, then decide whether it's worth the price.

The second night

All Problems Are Interpersonal Relationship Problems

The youth comes back the next week with a fresh complaint: he dislikes himself. The philosopher uses it to show Adler's second big claim. Every worry we have, even the ones that look private, is at bottom about other people.

The idea behind this night

Take other people away and most of your problems vanish, but so does everything good. The work is learning to live among them without competing.

Chapter 5 · Every problem is about other people

Why You Dislike Yourself

In short

The youth lists everything wrong with himself. The philosopher says he focuses on his faults on purpose: if he dislikes himself, he can avoid other people, and if he avoids them, he can't be rejected. Adler's sweeping claim follows: all problems are interpersonal relationship problems.

The big ideas

  • Self-dislike has a goal. Picking at your flaws gives you a reason to hide. "Nobody could like me" is safer than finding out.
  • Problems come from people. Loneliness only exists because others exist. Even worries that seem private, like money or looks, are about how we stand with other people.
  • Fear of being hurt. The deeper fear is being hurt in relationships. Avoiding people avoids the pain, but it also avoids every good thing they bring.
From the dialogue

The philosopher once worked with a student who was ashamed of blushing. She wanted the blushing gone, because then she could tell a boy she liked him. He suggested that she actually needed it. As long as she blushed, she could say "if only I didn't blush, he'd like me", and never risk asking. Being turned down by the boy would be far worse than having a symptom to blame. The goal was to protect her from rejection, and the blushing did the job.

The youth objects

YouthI really do have these flaws. I'm not making them up.

PhilosopherI believe you. But of all the things you could notice about yourself, you've chosen these. Ask what they're for.

In plain words

Notice the "if only" sentences you use. "If only I were taller, funnier, braver..." Each one protects you from a risk. Try taking the risk while you're still exactly as you are.

Next: Inferiority is a story →
Chapter 6 · Feeling, complex and superiority

Feelings of Inferiority Are Subjective Assumptions

In short

Feeling inferior isn't a fact about you. It's a judgement you made by comparing. A healthy feeling of inferiority pushes you to grow. An inferiority complex uses it as an excuse. A superiority complex hides it behind boasting.

The big ideas

  • A value you assign. Being short or quiet or new has no meaning on its own. You gave it the meaning "less than".
  • Healthy inferiority. Everyone wants to improve, and comparing yourself with your ideal self is normal and useful. That is a feeling of inferiority, and it drives growth.
  • The complex. An inferiority complex is the line "A is true of me, so I can't do B": I didn't go to university, so I'll never succeed. It's an excuse wearing the clothes of a reason.
  • Bragging hides it. People who boast, name-drop or flaunt their misfortune are often covering a feeling of inferiority. Pretending to be superior is the same complex in disguise.
From the dialogue

The philosopher tells the youth he is 155 centimetres tall. For years he thought of it as a defect and wondered if he'd be happier a little taller. Then a friend said that his height was a gift: big men can seem intimidating, but he puts people at ease. Nothing about his height changed. Only the value he gave it did. Height was objective. Inferiority was a story he'd been telling.

The youth objects

YouthBut I really did fail where others succeeded.

PhilosopherComparing yourself with others is where it goes wrong. Compare yourself with who you want to be, and the same facts become a direction.

In plain words

Wanting to be better is fine. Watch for the moment it turns into "so I can't" or into showing off. Both are ways of avoiding the real work of improving.

Next: Life isn't a race →
Chapter 7 · A level plane, not a track

Life Is Not a Competition

In short

If you see life as a race, everyone else is a rival, and other people's success feels like your loss. Adler pictures it instead as a level plane where people walk at different paces in different directions. Some are ahead, some behind, and nobody is competing.

The big ideas

  • Rivals or comrades. Competing turns people into enemies. When others are enemies, the world feels dangerous and their happiness feels like your defeat.
  • Walk your own path. On the level plane you move forward from where you are, toward your ideal self. Being ahead or behind someone else stops mattering.
  • From fight to revenge. Insults are invitations to a power struggle. If you fight and win, the loser moves on to revenge. The way out is not to step into the ring.
  • Admitting fault isn't losing. Saying sorry or admitting a mistake feels like defeat only if it's a contest. Once it isn't, it's just honest.
From the dialogue

The youth says he can't bring himself to be pleased for a friend who's doing well. The philosopher says that's what competition does. While others are rivals, every friend's promotion is a loss for you, and you can never really be happy for anyone. He adds a second example: someone insults you in public. The urge is to fight back and win. But winning makes your opponent want revenge, and the fight goes on. Seeing that it's a power struggle lets you decline it.

The youth objects

YouthWithout competition, why would anyone try?

PhilosopherYou still try. You just measure yourself against who you were yesterday and who you want to be, not against the person next to you.

In plain words

When someone else wins, notice whether it stings. If it does, you're racing. Try asking where you are on your own path instead, and the other person becomes a fellow walker.

Next: The life tasks and the life-lie →
Chapter 8 · Work, friendship, love

Facing the Tasks of Life

In short

Adler names three tasks every adult has to face: work, friendship and love. Each goes deeper than the last and is harder. Avoiding them with excuses is what he calls the life-lie. What matters isn't what you were born with but what use you make of it.

The big ideas

  • Three tasks, rising depth. Work ties have a shared goal to hold them together. Friendships have no such goal, so they need more. Love, and family, is the closest and hardest of all.
  • Red string or chains. A close bond can be a red string of connection or a chain. It turns into a chain when one person tries to control the other. Love feels freest when you can be yourself.
  • The life-lie. Blaming someone or something for not facing a life task, "my boss is impossible" or "my parents ruined me", is a life-lie. It lets you off the task.
  • Use, not possession. Adler's psychology is a psychology of use. The question isn't what you were given but what you'll do with it.
From the dialogue

The philosopher describes a couple whose love has cooled. Suddenly everything about the partner grates: the way they talk, even the way they eat. It feels as if the faults came first. The philosopher suggests the order is backwards. One of them first decided to get out of the relationship, and then went hunting for faults to justify it. The faults are real enough, but they're evidence gathered for a verdict already reached. That's the life-lie at work.

The youth objects

YouthSome people really are impossible to get on with.

PhilosopherSome are hard. But notice when you started collecting their faults. Was it before or after you'd decided to stay away?

In plain words

Look at the one relationship you most avoid. Ask whether you dislike the person, or whether you decided to avoid the relationship first and found reasons later. Then ask what you could do with what you've got.

The third night

Discard Other People's Tasks

Two weeks later the youth is angry about the idea of not needing anyone's approval. The philosopher answers with the tool the book is best known for: the separation of tasks. Work out whose task something is, and stop carrying the ones that belong to someone else.

The idea behind this night

You can't control what others think of you. Accepting that, even if it means being disliked, is what freedom is.

Chapter 9 · You are not here to meet others' expectations

Deny the Desire for Recognition

In short

We're taught to seek praise and avoid blame, so we grow up living for other people's approval. Adler says to deny that desire. You don't live to meet anyone's expectations, and nobody else lives to meet yours.

The big ideas

  • Reward and punishment. When praise is the reason to do good, people stop doing good when nobody's watching, or get angry when nobody thanks them.
  • Others' expectations. If you live to satisfy others, you end up living their life, not yours. And you can never satisfy them all.
  • They don't live for you. The flip side matters too. If others don't act as you hoped, that's not a betrayal. They were never living to meet your expectations.
From the dialogue

The youth admits he has spent years trying to be the son his parents hoped for. Isn't wanting recognition just human? The philosopher points out where it leads. Someone who picks up litter only when people are watching, or is hurt when nobody thanks them, is doing good for the praise, not the good. A life built that way is lived by the expectations of others, and it never feels like your own.

The youth objects

YouthIf nobody recognises what I do, how do I know it has value?

PhilosopherThat's the trap. Value judged by others can always be taken away. You'll find a steadier source on the fourth night.

In plain words

Before a big choice, ask: would I still choose this if nobody ever knew? If not, you may be living to meet someone else's expectations.

Next: Separate the tasks →
Chapter 10 · Whose task is this?

How to Separate Tasks

In short

The book's most practical tool. For anything that worries you, ask: who will ultimately receive the result of this choice? That person owns the task. Do your own tasks, don't let others into them, and don't intrude on theirs.

The big ideas

  • One question. "Who ultimately receives the result?" sorts almost everything. If your child doesn't study, it's the child who lives with the result, so it's the child's task.
  • Help, don't take over. Separating tasks doesn't mean ignoring people. You can let them know you're there to help. You just can't do their task for them.
  • The horse and the water. You can lead a horse to water, but you can't make it drink. Offering is your task. Drinking is the horse's.
  • What others think is theirs. How others judge you is their task. You can do your best, but you can't control their verdict, and trying to is where most stress comes from.
From the dialogue

The youth asks about a child who won't study. Surely making a child study is a parent's job? The philosopher asks who lives with the result of not studying. The child does. Forcing them is stepping into their task, and it usually backfires. What the parent can do is make it clear they are ready to help whenever the child wants. Then the parent has done their own task fully, and left the rest where it belongs.

The youth objects

YouthIsn't that just being cold and leaving people to fail?

PhilosopherIt's the opposite of abandonment. You stay close and offer help. What you stop doing is forcing, because forcing is the thing that damages the relationship.

In plain words

When you're worrying about something, ask who receives the result. If it's someone else, offer help once, mean it, and then put the worry down. If it's you, act.

Chapter 11 · Cutting the Gordian knot

What Real Freedom Is

In short

If you never want to be disliked, you have to meet everyone's expectations, which is impossible. So freedom, in the philosopher's words, is being disliked by other people. Not trying to be, but accepting the cost of living your way.

The big ideas

  • The cost of freedom. Being disliked by some people is proof that you're living freely. It's the price, not the goal.
  • Cut the knot. Like Alexander slicing the Gordian knot instead of untying it, separating tasks cuts through the tangle of trying to please everyone.
  • You hold the cards. You can't change anyone else, but you can change yourself. That puts the power to change a relationship in your hands.
From the dialogue

The youth is shocked: surely nobody wants to be disliked? The philosopher agrees it's painful. But to never be disliked you'd have to agree with everyone, change your face for every audience and never say no. That's not freedom, it's a cage. He tells the old story of Alexander the Great and the knot no one could untie. Alexander didn't fuss with it. He cut it with his sword. Separating tasks is that cut. Later he quotes a Jewish teaching: of every ten people, one will criticise you whatever you do, two will become close friends, and the other seven are neither.

The youth objects

YouthSo I should go around upsetting people?

PhilosopherNo. Be kind, do your best. Just don't treat being liked by everyone as the condition for living your own life.

In plain words

Someone will always disapprove. You can spend your life trying to change that, or you can accept it, put your energy into the people who are with you and the work that's yours.

Next: A feeling of community →
The fourth night

Where the Centre of the World Is

If we separate tasks, are we all alone? The fourth night answers no. The aim of relationships, for Adler, is a sense that you belong to a community, and you get there by treating people as equals: no praise or blame from above, only encouragement side by side.

The idea behind this night

You are not the centre of the world, but you are part of it. You feel your worth when you feel useful to someone.

Chapter 12 · Community feeling

You Are Not the Centre of the World

In short

The goal of relationships, for Adler, is community feeling: a sense that you belong, that others are comrades, and that you have a place. People desperate for approval are actually self-centred. They see others only as an audience for themselves.

The big ideas

  • Holism. You are one undivided person, and you are also part of something bigger. Your problems and your belonging both come from that.
  • Approval-seeking is self-centred. Worrying constantly about how others see you looks humble. It's really putting yourself at the centre and treating others as people who exist to judge you.
  • A part, not the centre. You are a part of the whole, not its centre. Belonging comes from contributing to the whole, not from being served by it.
  • Listen to the larger community. If you're stuck in a small community, like a school or a company, remember it's not the only one. There are always wider circles where you belong.
From the dialogue

The youth jokes that he's not self-centred, he's practically invisible: he spends all his time worrying what others think. The philosopher says that's the giveaway. Everything is about how others see him. He then suggests a way out for anyone trapped in a small world, like a child who's miserable at school. School feels like everything, but it's one community among many. Beyond it are the town, the country, humanity, even the universe. When one circle rejects you, listen to the voice of a larger one.

The youth objects

YouthThe community has to give me a place first, doesn't it?

PhilosopherThat's the centre-of-the-world view. Ask instead what you can give it. A place is something you make by contributing.

In plain words

If a group you're in feels like the whole world, step back. It isn't. And the question to ask in any group is not "what will they give me?" but "what can I add?"

Chapter 13 · Horizontal, not vertical

Do Not Rebuke or Praise

In short

Praise and blame both come from above. They're judgements by someone with more power on someone with less. Adler wants horizontal relationships, where people are equal but not the same. Instead of praise, encourage: say thank you, say it helped.

The big ideas

  • Praise is vertical. "Good job" from a parent to a child or a boss to a worker carries a hidden message: I'm in a position to judge you.
  • Equal, not the same. A child and an adult differ in knowledge and experience, but they're equal as people. Horizontal relationships start there.
  • Encourage instead. Gratitude, respect and joy, "thank you, that really helped", tell someone they've contributed. That's what builds the courage to keep going.
  • Help, don't intervene. Intervening is doing someone's task for them from above. Assisting is supporting them, as an equal, to do it themselves.
From the dialogue

The youth objects that children need praise. The philosopher asks what a mother means when she says "well done, you're so clever" to a child who helped. It's the tone you'd use with someone who can't normally do much. Between two adults, the same words would sound patronising. Now picture a friend who helps you move house. You don't tell them "good boy". You say thank you. That simple word treats the helper as an equal who chose to contribute.

The youth objects

YouthIsn't praise how children learn what's right?

PhilosopherPraise teaches them to look for the next judge. Gratitude teaches them that they're useful. Only one of those lasts without an audience.

Worth knowing

Modern research agrees in part. Praising a fixed trait like "you're so smart" can make children avoid challenges, while specific, honest feedback about what someone did tends to help. The book goes further and drops praise altogether.

In plain words

Swap "good job" for "thank you" or "that helped me" for a week, with anyone: kids, colleagues, a partner. Notice how it changes the feel of the relationship.

Next: How to feel you have value →
Chapter 14 · Contribution at the level of being

How to Feel You Have Value

In short

People feel they have worth when they feel useful to someone. That's the answer to the youth's question about where value comes from, if not from approval. And usefulness doesn't depend on doing. Simply being there can be a contribution.

The big ideas

  • Worth through contribution. The feeling "I am of use to someone" is what lets a person feel they have value. It doesn't need anyone's praise.
  • Being, not only doing. A newborn, a grandparent in hospital, a friend going through depression: none can do much, but their existence matters to others. Value at the level of being is unconditional.
  • See others that way. Look at people for the fact that they exist, not for what they achieve. Then you can be glad they're there, and say so.
From the dialogue

The youth argues that some people really can't contribute anything: someone bedridden, or a baby. The philosopher asks him to imagine his own mother in hospital after an accident. Would he feel she had no value because she can't work or cook? Of course not. He'd be grateful she was alive. That's contribution at the level of being. And if we can see it in others, we can see it in ourselves too, on days when we achieve nothing at all.

The youth objects

YouthThen a useless person can just say they're valuable?

PhilosopherNobody is useless at the level of being. Doing is a bonus. Being here already matters to someone.

In plain words

On a day when you got nothing done, remember: if you'd be glad a friend was simply alive and here, the same holds for you. Then go and be useful to someone, in a small way.

The fifth night

To Live in Earnest in the Here and Now

On the last night the youth asks how to actually live this way. The philosopher ties it together: accept yourself, trust others, contribute to them, have the courage to be ordinary, and treat life as a series of moments rather than a road to somewhere else.

The idea behind this night

Happiness is the feeling of being useful to others. It is available now, not at the end of some long climb.

Chapter 15 · Three parts of one cycle

Self-Acceptance, Confidence in Others, Contribution

In short

Community feeling rests on three things, and each feeds the next. Accept yourself as you are. Trust others without conditions. Contribute to them. Contribution makes you feel worthwhile, which makes self-acceptance easier, which makes trust easier.

The big ideas

  • Acceptance, not affirmation. Self-affirmation tells yourself "I can do it" when you can't. Self-acceptance says "I'm at 60 points; how do I get closer to 100?" It's honest.
  • Affirmative resignation. Change what you can, accept what you can't. Not giving up, but seeing clearly which is which.
  • Confidence, not credit. Credit is trust with conditions, like a bank loan. Confidence is trusting someone without conditions. If they betray it, that's their task, not yours.
  • Contribution, not sacrifice. Contribution is not giving yourself up for others. It's acting as part of a group because you choose to, which also gives you a sense of your own worth.
From the dialogue

The youth says he feels so self-conscious at parties that he can't be himself. The philosopher says that's what happens when you can't accept yourself: you're too busy managing how you look to act freely. He describes the three pillars and why they come as a set. Trust without self-acceptance is fearful. Contribution without trust looks like sacrifice. Self-acceptance without contribution has nothing to stand on. Together they turn into a cycle that strengthens itself.

The youth objects

YouthIf I trust everyone unconditionally, won't I get used?

PhilosopherSome will betray you. That's their task. Your task is to decide what you'll do. Most people find that trust given is trust returned.

In plain words

You don't need to feel 100 points to start. Accept the 60, trust one person a little more than feels safe, and do one useful thing for them today.

Next: The courage to be normal →
Chapter 16 · Two paths to being special

The Courage to Be Normal

In short

People who want to feel special take one of two paths: be especially good, or, when that fails, be especially bad. Adler traces how misbehaviour escalates in stages. The way out is the courage to be ordinary, which is not the same as being inferior.

The big ideas

  • Especially good or especially bad. A child who can't stand out by being good may stand out by being bad. Both are bids for a special place.
  • The stages of misbehaviour. Adler's model runs from demanding admiration, to drawing attention, to a power struggle, to revenge, and finally to showing incompetence so that nobody expects anything.
  • The easy path. Being bad is a shortcut to attention. It skips the hard work of being useful.
  • Normal isn't a failure. Being ordinary doesn't mean being worthless. Accepting it frees you from the endless need to prove something.
From the dialogue

The philosopher describes how it starts. A child first tries hard to be praised: the best marks, the tidiest room. If praise doesn't come, they try getting noticed any way they can, being cheeky or clowning. Then come fights with parents and teachers. If those fail, they move on to revenge, and finally to acting hopeless so nobody bothers them. Each stage is a way to stay special. The cure, says the philosopher, is the courage to be normal.

The youth objects

YouthIsn't wanting to be special just ambition?

PhilosopherWanting to grow is healthy. Needing to be special, good or bad, is a way of avoiding the plain work of contributing.

In plain words

If you notice you need to be the best, or you find yourself getting noticed in ways you're not proud of, try being ordinary for a while. Do something useful that nobody will praise.

Chapter 17 · Dance, and shine a light on now

Life Is a Series of Moments

In short

We imagine life as a line, climbing toward some summit. The philosopher says it's a series of dots: moments. Live each one fully, like dancing, where every step is complete in itself. The greatest life-lie is not living here and now.

The big ideas

  • Kinesis and energeia. Borrowing from Aristotle: kinesis is movement toward an end, where it's incomplete until you arrive. Energeia is complete in each moment, like dancing. Life should be energeia.
  • Dots, not a line. If life is a line, you're always on the way and never there. If it's dots, each moment is already the destination.
  • Spotlight the present. Lighting up the past and the future dims the present. Put the spotlight on here and now, and the rest goes dark, as it should.
  • Your own meaning. Adler said life in general has no meaning; you give it one. The philosopher's guiding star is contribution to others. Steer by it, and you won't get lost.
From the dialogue

The youth says this feels like giving up on long-term plans. The philosopher says no. A mountain climber who only counts the summit has wasted the climb if bad weather turns him back. A dancer never gets anywhere, yet every moment of dancing is complete. If you live each moment as the dance, then wherever life stops, you're already there. By the end of the last night the youth has stopped arguing. He leaves resolved to start with himself, rather than waiting for the world to change first.

The youth objects

YouthWithout goals, won't I just drift?

PhilosopherKeep your guiding star, contribution to others, and you'll always know which way you're facing. You just don't have to wait to arrive before life counts.

In plain words

Plan, by all means. But don't put off living until the plan works out. Today, do the thing that's in front of you as if it were the whole point, because it is.

Afterword

Your guiding star

The philosopher says that if you keep one star in sight, contribution to others, you won't lose your way, whatever happens. Write down one task you'll hand back and one way you'll be useful this week. It stays in this browser only.

Night 1Goals, not causesThe past doesn't push you. You choose a meaning for it, and you can choose again.
Night 2It's all relationshipsInferiority is a story, life isn't a race, and avoiding the life tasks is a life-lie.
Night 3Separate tasksAsk who receives the result. Do yours, offer help with theirs, accept being disliked.
Night 4Belong as an equalYou're part of the world, not its centre. Encourage instead of praising, and see value in being.
Night 5Live here and nowAccept yourself, trust others, contribute, dare to be ordinary, and live each moment like a dance.
The starContributionHappiness is the feeling of being useful to someone. You can have it today.

An independent study guide to The Courage to Be Disliked by Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga (2013; English translation 2017). The explanations, retold scenes and labs are written in our own words; the five nights follow the book's structure, and section titles follow its headings loosely. Labs that show numbers are illustrative models, not figures from the book.
