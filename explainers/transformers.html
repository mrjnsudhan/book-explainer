Chapter 1 · The big picture

What is a transformer, really?

The short answer

A machine that reads a whole piece of text at once and, for every word, works out which other words matter to it. Do that many times over and it understands the text well enough to translate it, answer questions about it, or guess the next word.

The big ideas

  • It is a guessing machine. At heart, a chatbot built on a transformer does one thing: given some text, it guesses the next small piece. Write that piece down, feed the text back in, guess again. Whole essays come out of that loop.
  • The old way read like a relay race. Before 2017 the best language models (called RNNs) read one word at a time, passing a small "memory" from word to word. By the end of a long sentence, the start had mostly faded, and nothing could be done in parallel.
  • The new way is a round table. A transformer lays every word on the table at the same moment. Each word can look straight at any other word, however far away, through a step called attention.
  • All at once means fast. Because every word is handled at the same time, the work suits graphics chips, which do thousands of sums in parallel. That is why transformers could be trained on huge amounts of text, and why they got so good.
In plain words

Imagine reading a mystery novel through a keyhole, one word at a time, with only a sticky note to remember everything so far. That is the old way. Now imagine spreading every page across a big table so you can glance from the last line straight back to the clue on page one. That is a transformer.

Chapter 2 · Tokenization

How does it read text?

The short answer

It doesn't see letters or whole words. Text is first chopped into tokens: common words stay whole, rarer words are built from smaller pieces. Each token gets an ID number, and those numbers are all the model ever sees.

The big ideas

  • Letters are too small. Reading one character at a time makes sentences very long, and a single letter carries almost no meaning.
  • Whole words are too many. A dictionary of every word, name, typo and new slang term would never end, and any word missing from it couldn't be read at all.
  • Pieces are just right. Subword tokens sit in between. "unbelievably" becomes un + believ + ably. A fixed set of about 50,000 pieces can spell anything.
  • The pieces are learned, not chosen. A method called Byte Pair Encoding starts from single letters and keeps gluing together the pair that appears most often in real text. Try it in the second tab.
In plain words

Think of a big tub of Lego. Common words like "the" or "cat" come as one ready-made brick. A rare word like your own name gets built out of two or three smaller bricks. The model only ever handles bricks, never raw letters, which is why it can stumble on things like counting the letters in a word.

Chapter 3 · Embeddings

How do words become numbers?

The short answer

Each token ID is swapped for a long list of numbers called an embedding, which works like a location on a map of meaning. Words used in similar ways end up close together, and directions on the map come to mean things, like "male to female" or "country to capital".

The big ideas

  • An ID number means nothing. Token 9246 isn't "bigger" than token 3797. The model needs numbers where closeness means similarity.
  • Every token gets coordinates. The original Transformer gave each token 512 numbers. Big modern models use several thousand. We can only draw two at a time, so the map below is a flattened sketch.
  • Nobody writes the coordinates. They start random and get nudged during training until words that appear in similar company sit near each other.
  • Directions carry meaning. The step from "man" to "woman" is roughly the same as the step from "king" to "queen". You can do arithmetic with meaning.
In plain words

Picture a city where every word has a home address. Animals live in one neighbourhood, foods in another, places in a third. Walk "two blocks north" from man and you reach woman; walk the same two blocks north from king and you reach queen. The model never sees a dictionary, only addresses like these.

Chapter 4 · Positional encoding

How does it know word order?

The short answer

Attention on its own sees a bag of words with no order, so "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would look the same. To fix that, each position gets a unique fingerprint made of waves, and it is added to the word's numbers before anything else happens.

The big ideas

  • Reading everything at once loses order. The round table from chapter 1 has no head or foot. Something has to tell each word which seat it is in.
  • Waves make good seat numbers. The paper used sine and cosine waves at many speeds. Each position gets the pattern of where all those waves happen to be at that point.
  • Like the hands of many clocks. A fast hand tells nearby seats apart; slow hands tell far-apart seats apart. Read together, they give every seat a pattern no other seat has.
  • Added, not stuck on. The fingerprint is simply added to the word's own numbers, so one vector now carries "what the word means" and "where it sits".
In plain words

A cinema ticket says both the film and your seat. The embedding is the film; the positional encoding is the seat number printed on the same ticket. Later, when words look at each other, they can tell not only who is in the room but who is sitting next to whom.

Chapter 5 · Self-attention

How do words look at each other?

The short answer

Every word asks a question, every word offers a label, and every word holds some information. Each word compares its question with all the labels, then soaks up information mostly from the words that matched. That is attention, and it is how "it" figures out what "it" refers to.

The big ideas

  • Words change meaning with company. "Bank" by a river and "bank" with money are different things. A word's vector has to be updated using the words around it.
  • Three roles per word. Each word makes a query (what am I looking for?), a key (what do I have to offer?) and a value (what I'll pass on if picked).
  • Attention is a share-out. Each word splits 100% of its attention across all the words, itself included. A strong match gets a big share.
  • Every word does this at once. The result is a grid: one row per word saying how much it listens to every other word. That grid is the "attention map".
In plain words

Think of a busy party. Everyone wears a name tag (the key), has a question on their mind (the query), and has a story to tell (the value). You glance at every name tag, spend most of your time with the people whose tags match your question, and leave knowing a blend of their stories. Every guest does this at the same time.

Chapter 6 · Queries, keys and values

What's the math inside attention?

The short answer

Four small steps. Multiply the query with each key to get a match score. Shrink the scores a little. Turn them into percentages with softmax. Blend the values using those percentages. The blend is the word's new, context-aware meaning.

The big ideas

  • The dot product measures agreement. Two arrows pointing the same way give a big positive score; at right angles, zero; opposite ways, negative.
  • Softmax turns scores into shares. It makes every score positive and scales them so they add up to 100%. Bigger scores get a bigger slice, and the gap is exaggerated.
  • Why divide by √d. With 64 numbers per arrow, scores get large and softmax would hand nearly everything to one word. Dividing by √64 = 8 keeps the choice soft enough to learn. Play with the sharpness slider to see the effect.
  • Q, K and V are learned lenses. The query, key and value are made by multiplying the word's vector by three grids of numbers the model learns. Those grids decide what each word asks and offers.
In plain words

A search engine works the same way. Your query is compared with every page's title (the keys). The best matches get most of your attention, and what you learn is a blend of those pages' contents (the values). Attention does this for every word in the sentence, with no search box.

Chapter 7 · Multi-head attention

Why have several heads?

The short answer

One round of attention can only look for one kind of thing at a time. So the model runs several smaller attentions side by side, called heads, each with its own queries and keys. One might track grammar, another who-did-what, another which word describes which. Their findings are stitched back together.

The big ideas

  • Many questions at once. For the word "cross", it helps to know who is crossing, what is being crossed, and what came just before. Different heads can chase each question.
  • Split, then join. The paper used 8 heads. The 512 numbers per word were split into 8 slices of 64, each head worked on its own slice, and the 8 results were joined back into 512.
  • Same cost, more variety. Eight small heads cost about the same as one big one, but they can learn eight different patterns.
  • Nobody assigns the jobs. The heads specialise on their own during training. Researchers find heads that track the previous word, match pronouns, or follow sentence structure.
In plain words

Hand the same sentence to a panel of specialists: a grammar teacher, a detective, an art critic and a speed reader. Each underlines different links. Then you staple their notes together. That stapled bundle is richer than any one specialist's view.

Chapter 8 · The transformer block

What's inside one layer?

The short answer

One layer is a meeting followed by desk work. In the meeting (attention) words share information. At the desk (a small feed-forward network) each word thinks it over alone. Shortcuts carry the original along, and a tidy-up step keeps the numbers in range. Stack this layer 6, 12 or 100 times.

The big ideas

  • Attention gathers, feed-forward thinks. Attention is the only place words talk to each other. The feed-forward part then works on each word separately, and is thought to store much of what the model "knows".
  • Shortcuts keep the original. Each part adds its findings on top of its input instead of replacing it. These residual connections let information and learning signals flow through very deep stacks.
  • Normalise to stay steady. After each part, "layer norm" rescales each word's numbers to a standard size, like a volume knob that stops things getting too loud or too quiet.
  • Same shape in, same shape out. A layer takes 512 numbers per word and gives back 512 per word, so layers stack like identical floors of a building, each refining the last.
In plain words

Think of editing a shared document. In each round everyone first reads everyone else's paragraphs (attention), then goes back to their own desk to improve their paragraph (feed-forward). They never rewrite from scratch; they add sticky notes to what's there (the shortcut). After a dozen rounds, each paragraph reflects the whole document.

Chapter 9 · Encoder and decoder

How does translation work?

The short answer

The original Transformer had two halves. The encoder reads the whole English sentence and builds a rich understanding of it. The decoder writes the French one word at a time, and before each word it glances back at the English through cross-attention.

The big ideas

  • The encoder sees everything. Its words can attend in every direction, because the whole input sentence is already known.
  • The decoder can't peek ahead. When writing word 3, it may only look at words 1 and 2 of its own output. A mask blocks the future, so the model can't cheat during training.
  • Cross-attention is the bridge. The decoder's queries look at the encoder's keys and values. That is how "chat" finds "cat", even when the word order differs between languages.
  • It set records. On standard English to German and English to French tests, the 2017 Transformer beat every earlier system, and trained in a fraction of the time.
In plain words

Picture a human interpreter. First they listen to the whole English sentence (the encoder). Then they speak French one word at a time (the decoder), glancing at their notes before each word to check which part of the English they're on (cross-attention), and they can never take back or skip ahead of what they've already said (the mask).

Chapter 10 · Training and generation

How does it learn and write?

The short answer

In training it is shown real text with the future hidden, guesses every next word at once, and has all its numbers nudged to make the right word a little more likely. In generation it guesses one word, adds it to the text, and guesses again, with a temperature dial for how adventurous to be.

The big ideas

  • Training is a giant fill-in-the-blank. For every position in a passage, the model predicts the next token. The gap between its guess and the truth (the "loss") tells it which way to nudge millions or billions of numbers.
  • Training is parallel, writing is not. Thanks to the mask, one sentence gives a guess at every position in a single pass. Writing, though, has to go one token at a time, because each new token depends on the last.
  • The output is a menu of odds. The last layer gives a probability for every token in the vocabulary. The model then picks one, usually not always the top one.
  • Three family branches. Encoder-only models (like BERT) are good at understanding. Decoder-only models (like GPT, Claude and Llama) are good at writing. Encoder-decoder models (like the original and T5) are good at turning one text into another.

Training is like studying with flashcards where the answer is covered: guess, peek, adjust, repeat billions of times. Generation is your phone's autocomplete on repeat, with a dial for whether to always tap the safest suggestion or sometimes take a chance on something more surprising.
