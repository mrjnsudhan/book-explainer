A chapter-by-chapter guide to the song at the heart of the Mahabharata

The Bhagavad Gita,
Explained

Two armies face each other on the field of Kurukshetra. The archer Arjuna asks his charioteer, Krishna, to drive him into the gap between them, takes one look at the cousins, teachers and friends he is about to fight, and puts down his bow. The Bhagavad Gita, "the Song of the Lord", is the conversation that follows: 700 verses in 18 chapters on how to act when every choice hurts, who you really are, and how to live without being ruled by what you want or fear. This guide takes each chapter in turn, with a lab in every one where you can try the idea.

Do the work in front of you as well as you can, and let go of what you get for it.

Chapters 1 to 6 · karma yoga

The Path of Action

Commentators have long split the Gita into three sets of six chapters. The first six start with a man who can't act and end with a mind that can sit still. On the way Krishna gives the two ideas the Gita is best known for: you are not the body that dies, and you can act fully without clinging to what the action brings.

The idea behind this part

Running away from your duty doesn't free you. Doing it, with your attention on the work and not on the reward, does.

Chapter 1 · Arjuna Vishada Yoga

Arjuna's Despair

In short

The blind king Dhritarashtra asks his minister Sanjaya, who has been given far sight, to tell him what is happening on the battlefield. Conches sound, the armies are ready, and Arjuna asks Krishna to drive his chariot into the middle. He sees his own family and teachers on the other side, his body gives way, and he refuses to fight.

The big ideas

  • The setting is a crisis. The Gita doesn't open in a temple or a forest. It opens a few minutes before a war, with the best archer of his age shaking in his chariot.
  • Arjuna's reasons sound good. He doesn't want a kingdom bought with his family's blood. Killing kin is wrong, families will collapse, and nothing good can come of it. He would rather be killed unarmed.
  • It is grief dressed as virtue. His body tells the real story: dry mouth, trembling limbs, a mind that won't hold still. The rest of the Gita asks whether his refusal is wisdom or panic.
  • The question is everyone's. What should I do when every option hurts someone I love? That is why a battle scene from an epic became a book people read for their own lives.

I don't want victory, Krishna, or a kingdom, or pleasures. What use would a kingdom be to us, or even life itself?

Verse 1.32 · in our words
From the text

Arjuna says to Krishna: drive the chariot between the two armies so I can see who I have to fight. Krishna sets it down in front of Bhishma and Drona, the great-uncle who raised him and the teacher who taught him archery. Arjuna looks across and sees fathers, grandfathers, uncles, brothers, sons, grandsons and friends on both sides. His limbs go weak, his skin burns, his great bow Gandiva slides out of his hand. He lists his arguments, then sits down in the chariot, drops his bow and arrows, and stops. That is where the chapter ends: the hero has quit before the first arrow.

Worth knowing

Some readers, Gandhi among them, take the battle as an allegory for the war inside each person between better and worse impulses. Others read it as a real war and a real question about duty and violence. The text supports both readings, and this guide doesn't pick one.

In plain words

Before you decide anything important, notice whether your reasons are coming from a clear head or a shaking body. Arjuna's arguments were real, but they arrived after the panic, not before it.

Chapter 2 · Sankhya Yoga

The Yoga of Knowledge

In short

Arjuna asks Krishna to teach him, and the real Gita begins. Krishna's first answer is that Arjuna is grieving for the wrong thing: the self in every person is never born and never dies, only bodies do. His second is practical: do your duty, keep your mind level in success and failure, and you will reach a steadiness nothing can shake.

The big ideas

  • The self doesn't die. There was never a time when you, I or these kings did not exist, and there never will be. Weapons can't cut it and fire can't burn it. It moves on from a body the way you take off worn-out clothes.
  • Feelings come and go. Heat and cold, pleasure and pain arrive when the senses meet the world, and they pass. Bear them without being thrown.
  • Work, not wages. You have a right to your work, never to its fruits. Don't make the reward your motive, and don't let that push you into doing nothing.
  • The steady mind. Krishna describes the person of steady wisdom: like a tortoise pulling in its limbs, they can withdraw their senses, and like the ocean taking in rivers, they stay full and still.

Your right is to the work alone, never to what it brings. Don't let the result be your reason for acting, and don't fall in love with doing nothing.

Verse 2.47 · in our words
From the text

Arjuna admits he is lost: "I am your student, I have come to you for help, tell me what is right." Krishna smiles and starts from the very top. You are mourning people who are not lost, he says. A body goes from childhood to youth to old age, and then to another body, and the wise aren't confused by that. Later in the chapter he traces how a person falls apart: keep thinking about something you want and you grow attached; attachment turns into desire, desire blocked turns into anger, anger clouds judgement, clouded judgement forgets what it learned, and once reason is gone, so are you.

In plain words

Do the work as well as you can, and treat the result as something you hand over rather than something you are owed. If you notice your mind circling something you want, that's the first step of the slide. Catch it there.

Chapter 3 · Karma Yoga

The Yoga of Action

In short

Arjuna is confused: if a calm, knowing mind is the goal, why push him into a bloody battle? Krishna's answer is that nobody can stop acting, even for a moment. The choice is not between action and rest but between action that binds you and action done as an offering, for the good of the world.

The big ideas

  • You can't not act. Breathing, eating, keeping a body alive are all actions. Nature drives everyone. Sitting still while your mind races after things isn't renunciation, it's pretending.
  • Act as an offering. Work done as a sacrifice, for something bigger than yourself, doesn't bind you. Work done only for yourself does.
  • Others copy you. Whatever a respected person does, others follow. Even if you need nothing, act to keep the world together, as King Janaka did.
  • Desire is the enemy. What pushes people to do wrong against their will? Desire, which turns into anger when blocked. It covers wisdom like smoke covers a fire or dust a mirror.

Better your own duty done imperfectly than someone else's done perfectly.

Verse 3.35 · in our words
From the text

Krishna points to himself. There is nothing in the three worlds I need to do and nothing I don't already have, he says, and still I keep working. If I stopped, everyone would copy me and the world would fall apart. He describes a cycle that holds life together: creatures live on food, food comes from rain, rain comes from sacrifice, and sacrifice is made of action. Taking from the cycle without giving back, he says, is stealing. Then he names the thing that makes even a good person act badly: desire, born of restless energy, never satisfied, hiding in the senses, the mind and the intellect.

In plain words

You will act today whether you mean to or not. Pick one task and do it for the people it helps, not for the credit. Notice that it feels lighter.

Chapter 4 · Jnana Karma Sannyasa Yoga

Knowledge and the Renunciation of Action

In short

Krishna reveals that he taught this yoga long ago to the Sun, who passed it down a line of kings until it was lost. Arjuna objects that Krishna was born recently. Krishna explains that he has been born many times, whenever goodness declines, and goes on to the chapter's riddle: the wise see inaction in action and action in inaction.

The big ideas

  • He comes back when needed. Whenever right order collapses and wrong rises, Krishna says, I take form, to protect the good, end the wicked and set dharma on its feet again.
  • Same act, different weight. Two people can do the same thing. One is bound by it because they want something; the other is free because they don't. What counts is the state of the doer.
  • Knowledge burns karma. As a fire turns wood to ash, knowledge turns all actions to ash. Nothing purifies like it.
  • Learn by asking. Find someone who knows, approach them humbly, ask questions and serve. And cut your doubt with the sword of knowledge, because a mind full of doubt finds no peace.

Whenever goodness fades and wrong is on the rise, I bring myself into being, age after age.

Verse 4.7–8 · in our words
From the text

Arjuna takes Krishna literally: you were born long after the Sun, so how could you have taught it? Krishna says they have both been born many times; the difference is that he remembers all of his births and Arjuna remembers none. He is unborn and unchanging, yet he takes form through his own power. Then he turns to the kinds of offering people make: some offer food into fire, some offer their senses into restraint, some offer their breath, some offer study. All of these are good, he says, but the best offering is knowledge, because every action ends in it.

Worth knowing

This chapter also says Krishna created the four social orders (varna) according to people's qualities and work. Many modern readers, including reformers who rejected caste by birth, point to that wording to argue the Gita ties varna to nature and conduct, not birth. Others read it more traditionally. The verse is one of the most argued over in the text.

In plain words

Ask of anything you're about to do: would I still do this if nobody noticed and nothing came of it? If yes, it won't weigh on you. If no, you've found where the hook is.

Chapter 5 · Karma Sannyasa Yoga

The Yoga of Renunciation

In short

Arjuna asks for a straight answer: is it better to give up action or to act without attachment? Both lead to freedom, Krishna says, but acting without attachment is better, and in the end they are the same path. The free person still sees, hears, eats and sleeps, but knows that the senses are just moving among their objects.

The big ideas

  • Two paths, one place. Only children, Krishna says, think renunciation and action are different. Whoever really practises one reaches the goal of both.
  • The senses act, not you. Seeing, hearing, touching, breathing, talking, letting go, even opening and closing the eyes: the wise person sees the senses at work and thinks, "I'm not doing anything."
  • Untouched, like a lotus leaf. A lotus leaf sits in water and stays dry. Act while offering the work to something higher and wrongdoing doesn't stick to you.
  • Same eyes for all. The wise see the same thing in a learned priest, a cow, an elephant, a dog and an outcast. Pleasure born of contact has a beginning and an end, so they don't rest their happiness on it.

Whoever acts, laying their work before the highest and letting go of attachment, is not stained by wrong, as a lotus leaf is not wet by water.

Verse 5.10 · in our words
From the text

Krishna describes someone who has found peace. They are not happy because something nice happened, so they are not crushed when it goes. They hold off the push of desire and anger before the body falls, and that is happiness. Their delight is inside, their joy is inside, their light is inside. He ends the chapter with a short sketch of meditation, shutting out outside contact, fixing the gaze between the brows, evening out the breath, that the next chapter opens up.

In plain words

Things will land on you all day: praise, blame, good news, bad news. You can't stop the rain. You can stop soaking it up.

Chapter 6 · Dhyana Yoga

The Yoga of Meditation

In short

Krishna teaches how to sit and still the mind: a clean, quiet place, a seat neither too high nor too low, body and head straight, mind turned inward. Arjuna protests that the mind is as restless as the wind. Krishna agrees, and gives the two-part answer that every meditation teacher since has repeated: practice and letting go.

The big ideas

  • You are your own friend and enemy. Lift yourself up by yourself, and don't drag yourself down. To someone who has mastered their mind, it is a friend. To someone who hasn't, it behaves like an enemy.
  • Nothing in excess. Yoga isn't for someone who eats too much or too little, or sleeps too much or too little. Moderation in food, play, work and sleep is what makes it work.
  • Bring it back. Whenever the restless mind wanders, gently bring it back. And again. That is the practice.
  • No effort is wasted. What happens to someone who tries and fails? Krishna says nobody who does good ever comes to a bad end. They pick up next time where they left off.

As a lamp in a place with no wind does not flicker: that is the picture of a yogi's mind held steady in meditation.

Verse 6.19 · in our words
From the text

Arjuna is honest: I don't see how this steadiness can last, Krishna, the mind is restless, strong, stubborn, as hard to hold as the wind. Krishna doesn't argue. Yes, he says, it is hard to control and always moving. But it can be held by practice and by dispassion. Arjuna then asks what becomes of someone who has faith but slips before reaching the goal: do they fall between two worlds, like a cloud torn apart? Krishna's reply is one of the kindest in the Gita: they are reborn into a good home, or a family of yogis, and the progress they made comes back to them.

In plain words

Sit for five minutes and count how often your mind wanders. Each time you notice and come back isn't a failure, it's one repetition. That's how the flame steadies.

Chapters 7 to 12 · bhakti yoga

The Path of Devotion

The middle six turn from what you do to who you are doing it for. Krishna describes himself: the thread through every pearl, the taste in water, the best of every kind of thing, and finally a vision too vast to look at. Then he brings it back to something anyone can do: love, and offer what you have.

The idea behind this part

Behind everything there is one source, and the simplest way to it is devotion, not cleverness or ritual.

Chapter 7 · Jnana Vijnana Yoga

Knowledge and Realisation

In short

Now Krishna talks about himself. Everything in the world, earth, water, fire, air, space, mind, intellect and the sense of "I", is his lower nature. His higher nature is the life in all beings. Everything is strung on him like pearls on a thread, though most people only see the pearls.

The big ideas

  • Two natures. An eightfold material nature that changes, and a higher one, the life principle, that holds the whole universe up.
  • He is the essence. I am the taste in water, the light in the sun and moon, the sound in space, the strength of the strong, the intelligence of the intelligent.
  • The veil of the three strands. The world is bewitched by the play of nature's three qualities and doesn't see what is behind them. It is hard to get through, but those who turn to him do.
  • Four kinds of seekers. People turn to the divine when they are in trouble, when they want to know, when they want something, or because they understand. All are good; the one who understands is dearest.

There is nothing higher than me, Arjuna. All this is strung on me like pearls on a thread.

Verse 7.7 · in our words
From the text

Krishna says that among thousands of people, maybe one sets out to reach perfection, and among those who strive, maybe one really knows him. He isn't scornful about others' worship. Whatever form someone worships with faith, he says, I make their faith steady, and they get what they ask for, though it is limited, because they asked for something limited. What they miss is the thread itself: unborn, unchanging, hidden behind its own magic.

In plain words

Pick something ordinary today, water, sunlight, someone's skill, and look for what makes it what it is. The Gita says that essence is the divine hiding in plain sight.

Chapter 8 · Akshara Brahma Yoga

The Imperishable

In short

Arjuna asks a string of questions about the ultimate, the self and karma, and how a person can know Krishna at the moment of death. Krishna says whatever you remember at the end is where you go, so remember him at all times, and fight. Then he zooms out to cosmic time: whole universes appear at the dawn of a day of Brahma and dissolve at its night.

The big ideas

  • The last thought matters. Whatever state of being you remember when you leave the body, you go to. And what you think of at the end is what you thought of most in life.
  • So remember all the time. Remember me always, and fight. The point isn't a deathbed trick; it's a life spent aimed at one thing.
  • Time on a vast scale. A day of Brahma lasts a thousand ages, and so does his night. Beings come out at dawn and dissolve at nightfall, again and again.
  • Beyond the cycle. Behind the unmanifest that worlds come out of is another, eternal unmanifest that doesn't perish when everything else does. Reaching it, nobody returns.

Remember me at all times, and fight. With mind and understanding set on me, you will come to me without doubt.

Verse 8.7 · in our words
From the text

Krishna describes the moment of leaving the body for someone who has practised: the senses closed, the mind held in the heart, the breath drawn up to the head, chanting the single syllable Om. Then he describes the two paths the departing are said to take, one of fire, light and the bright half of the year, from which there is no return, and one of smoke, night and the dark half, which leads back to birth. A yogi who knows both paths, he says, is never confused by either.

Worth knowing

The Gita gives only the ratio: a day of Brahma is a thousand yugas. The year counts in this lab, 4.32 million years for one great age and 4.32 billion for a day of Brahma, come from the later Puranas, which worked the figures out in detail. They are traditional numbers, not measurements.

In plain words

What you think about most is where your life is headed, and at the end it's what you'll be left with. Notice what your mind returns to when it's idle.

Chapter 9 · Raja Vidya Raja Guhya Yoga

The Royal Secret

In short

Krishna calls this the king of knowledge and the king of secrets, and it turns out to be simple. Everything rests in him, yet he isn't caught in any of it. He is the same to everyone. And whoever offers him even a leaf, a flower, a fruit or a little water with love, he accepts.

The big ideas

  • In everything, bound by nothing. All beings rest in me as the wind rests in space, but I am not in them, and they are not limited to me. It is a puzzle meant to be held, not solved.
  • Love is the offering. The value of the offering isn't its price. A leaf given with devotion counts for more than gold given without it.
  • Make everything an offering. Whatever you do, eat, give, offer or endure, do it as an offering. Then there is no line between sacred and ordinary.
  • No one is shut out. Even someone who has done great wrong, if they turn to him with their whole heart, should be counted as good. Women, merchants and labourers, often shut out of ritual in the Gita's world, reach the highest too.

A leaf, a flower, a fruit or water: whatever is offered to me with love, I take, because it is offered from the heart.

Verse 9.26 · in our words
From the text

Krishna says he is the ritual and the offering, the herb and the chant, the butter and the fire. He is father, mother and grandfather of the world, its goal, support, witness, home and friend. He is immortality and death, being and non-being. People who perform rituals for heaven go there, enjoy it, and come back when their merit runs out, like spending a fortune. But people who simply think of him, with nothing else on their minds, he looks after himself: he brings what they lack and keeps what they have.

In plain words

Pick one ordinary act today, cooking a meal, finishing a report, and do it as if you were giving it to someone you love. The act doesn't change. You do.

Chapter 10 · Vibhuti Yoga

Divine Glories

In short

Arjuna asks how he can think of Krishna in daily life. Krishna answers with a long list: among lights I am the sun, among mountains Meru, among rivers the Ganga, among seasons spring, among letters the first one. The point isn't the list. It's that wherever something is at its best, you are seeing a spark of him.

The big ideas

  • Look for the peak. In every category, Krishna names the finest example as himself. Excellence is where the divine shows through most clearly.
  • Even the uncomfortable ones. Among deceivers, he says, I am gambling. Among things that cut down, I am death. The list doesn't only include pleasant things.
  • He is in the list too. Among the Vrishni clan I am Vasudeva, among the Pandavas Arjuna, among sages Vyasa. The speaker, the listener and the author all make the list.
  • A fraction is enough. There is no end to my glories, he says. I hold up this whole universe with a single fragment of myself.

Whatever is glorious, beautiful or powerful, know it comes from a spark of my splendour.

Verse 10.41 · in our words
From the text

Arjuna has just heard Krishna's teaching and is moved. He says: I believe everything you've said, but even the gods don't know your full nature. Tell me in what forms I should think of you. Krishna begins: I am the self seated in the heart of all beings, their beginning, middle and end. Then the list rolls on for twenty verses, from the sun to the ocean, from the lion among beasts to Garuda among birds, from the thunderbolt to silence, which he says he is among secrets.

In plain words

Today, when you see something done superbly, a perfect sentence, a kind act, a sunset, pause on it. The Gita says that's the closest look at the divine most of us get.

Chapter 11 · Vishvarupa Darshana Yoga

The Vision of the Cosmic Form

In short

Arjuna asks to see Krishna as he really is. Krishna gives him a divine eye, and Arjuna sees a form with countless arms, faces and eyes, brighter than a thousand suns, holding every god and world inside it. Then he sees the warriors of both armies rushing into its mouths. The vision terrifies him, and he begs Krishna to come back as his friend.

The big ideas

  • Ordinary eyes can't see it. Arjuna needs a gift of sight. The Gita is saying the whole truth is too large for normal perception.
  • Brighter than a thousand suns. If a thousand suns rose in the sky at once, that would hint at the brilliance of the form.
  • Time, the destroyer. Krishna names himself as time, grown vast, here to wind up the worlds. The warriors are already gone; Arjuna is only the instrument.
  • Love over awe. After the vision, Arjuna apologises for having joked with Krishna as a friend. Krishna returns to his human shape, and says only devotion lets anyone see what Arjuna saw.

I am time, grown mighty, the destroyer of worlds, come to sweep them away. Even without you, none of these warriors will survive.

Verse 11.32 · in our words
From the text

Sanjaya, narrating for the blind king, describes what Arjuna saw: the whole universe gathered in one place, in the body of the god of gods. Arjuna, hair on end, folds his hands and calls out what he sees: the gods, the sages, serpents, Brahma on his lotus; no beginning, middle or end anywhere. Then the vision turns dreadful. Bhishma, Drona and the enemy's champions, along with Arjuna's own side, pour into the gaping mouths like rivers into the sea, like moths into a flame. Arjuna begs to know who this is. The answer is: time. Be only the instrument, Krishna says. They are already slain.

Worth knowing

J. Robert Oppenheimer, who read the Gita in Sanskrit, said that after the first atomic test in 1945 he thought of verse 11.32 in the form "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." Most translators render the word (kala) as time rather than death.

In plain words

When something feels overwhelming, it helps to remember you are one instrument in a much bigger process. That isn't an excuse to stop caring. It's permission to stop carrying the whole thing.

Chapter 12 · Bhakti Yoga

The Yoga of Devotion

In short

The shortest chapter, and one of the most loved. Arjuna asks which is better: worshipping Krishna in a form, or the formless absolute. Both get there, Krishna says, but the formless is much harder for people with bodies. Then he gives a ladder that meets you wherever you are, and a portrait of the devotee he holds dear.

The big ideas

  • Form is easier. Loving a person is something people know how to do. Fixing the mind on something with no qualities at all is a steep road.
  • A ladder for everyone. Fix your mind on me. If you can't, practise. If you can't practise, do your work for me. If you can't even do that, just let go of the results of what you do.
  • Letting go of results brings peace. Krishna ranks giving up the fruits of action above even meditation, because peace follows it at once.
  • Who he loves. Someone who hates no one, is friendly and kind, free of "I" and "mine", the same in pleasure and pain, forgiving, content, steady, not troubled by the world and not troubling it.

The one by whom the world is not troubled, and who is not troubled by the world, free of excitement, envy, fear and anxiety, is dear to me.

Verse 12.15 · in our words
From the text

Krishna's ladder is unusual because it goes down rather than up: every rung is an easier option for someone who can't manage the one before. It ends with the most ordinary thing in the book. If you can't do anything else, give up the fruits of your actions. Then the chapter turns into a list of qualities, verse after verse ending with the same refrain, "that one is dear to me": the same to friend and enemy, to honour and insult, to heat and cold, quiet, content with whatever comes, at home anywhere.

In plain words

You don't have to start at the top. If meditation doesn't work for you, practise something smaller. If that's too much, do your job as service. If that's too much, just loosen your grip on how things turn out.

Chapters 13 to 18 · jnana yoga

The Path of Knowledge

The last six get analytical. They separate the body from the one who knows it, sort every thought, food, gift and kind of happiness into three strands of nature, and end with the whole teaching pulled together. Then Krishna steps back and tells Arjuna to think it over and choose.

The idea behind this part

See clearly what is you and what is nature moving around you, and the fear of acting goes away.

Chapter 13 · Kshetra Kshetrajna Vibhaga Yoga

The Field and Its Knower

In short

The body, Krishna says, is a field, and the one who knows it is the knower of the field. Everything you can observe, including your thoughts, desires, pain and pleasure, belongs to the field. The knower is what observes. Seeing the difference clearly is what Krishna calls knowledge.

The big ideas

  • The field is everything you can watch. The elements, the senses, the mind, desire and aversion, pleasure and pain, even the sense of "I": all of it is field.
  • The knower is what watches. In every field there is a knower, and Krishna says he is the knower in all fields.
  • Knowledge is a way of living. He defines knowledge not as facts but as humility, harmlessness, patience, honesty, service, steadiness, self-control and detachment.
  • Like space, like the sun. Space is in everything but nothing stains it. The one sun lights the whole world. So the knower lights up the whole field and isn't touched by it.

As the one sun lights up this whole world, so the knower of the field lights up the whole field.

Verse 13.33 · in our words
From the text

Krishna lays out two things that have both existed from the beginning: nature, which does all the acting and changing, and the conscious spirit, which experiences it. Nature is the cause of the body and the senses; the spirit is the cause of experiencing pleasure and pain. Trouble starts when the spirit, sitting inside nature, takes nature's qualities as its own. Someone who sees that all actions are done by nature alone, and that the self isn't the doer, he says, really sees.

Worth knowing

Some editions add a question from Arjuna at the start of this chapter, which brings the count to 701 verses. Most count 700 and leave it out, as this guide does.

In plain words

Next time a strong feeling comes up, try saying "this is in the field". Pain, a craving, a worried thought: they are things you are aware of. You are the one aware.

Chapter 14 · Gunatraya Vibhaga Yoga

The Three Gunas

In short

All of nature is woven from three strands, or gunas: sattva, which is clear and bright; rajas, which is restless and driven; and tamas, which is heavy and dull. They are always mixed, and one is always on top. Each one binds you in its own way, even the good one. Freedom is stepping behind all three.

The big ideas

  • Sattva: clarity. Light, calm, wanting to understand. It binds you through attachment to happiness and knowledge.
  • Rajas: drive. Desire, activity, restlessness, greed. It binds you through attachment to action.
  • Tamas: inertia. Dullness, confusion, laziness, too much sleep. It binds you through carelessness.
  • One always wins. Sattva rises by overpowering the other two, and so does each of the others. Notice which is in charge right now, and you understand a lot about your mood.

When someone sees that only the gunas act, and knows what is beyond them, they come to my being.

Verse 14.19 · in our words
From the text

Arjuna asks the obvious practical question: how do you recognise someone who has gone beyond the three gunas? How do they behave? Krishna's answer is a portrait. They don't resent light, activity or confusion when those appear, and don't long for them when they fade. They sit like someone unconcerned, unshaken, thinking "it's just the gunas moving". To them a lump of earth, a stone and gold are the same, praise and blame are the same, friend and foe are the same.

In plain words

A few times today, ask which strand is running you right now: clear, driven or heavy? Just naming it creates a little space between you and it.

Chapter 15 · Purushottama Yoga

The Supreme Person

In short

Krishna describes the world as an ashvattha, a sacred fig tree, with its roots above and its branches below. Its leaves are the sacred hymns, its branches are fed by the three gunas, and its new shoots are the things of the senses. You can't see where it begins or ends. Cut it down, he says, with the strong axe of detachment.

The big ideas

  • Roots in the sky. The tree grows down from its source. The world we live in is the branches, far from where it starts.
  • It keeps sprouting. Every sense object is a bud that turns into a new branch of wanting. The more you feed it, the more it spreads.
  • The axe is detachment. You can't climb out of the tree by grabbing more branches. You cut through with non-attachment and then look for the place from which there is no return.
  • The highest person. There are two kinds of being: the perishable, which is all creatures, and the imperishable, which is unchanging. Beyond both is the supreme self, Purushottama.

A fragment of me becomes the living soul in the world of the living, and draws to itself the senses and the mind.

Verse 15.7 · in our words
From the text

Krishna explains how the soul travels: as the wind carries scent from flowers, the self carries the mind and senses when it leaves one body and enters another. The confused don't see it come or go; those with the eye of wisdom do. Then he lists where he can be found: in the light of the sun, the moon and fire; in the earth holding beings up; in the plants, through the juice of the moon; as the fire of digestion inside every body; and in every heart, as memory and knowledge and their loss.

In plain words

Think of one thing you want that keeps sprouting new wants: the next upgrade, the next approval. You don't need to fight every new shoot. Cut the branch they grow from.

Chapter 16 · Daivasura Sampad Vibhaga Yoga

Divine and Demonic Natures

In short

Krishna sorts human qualities into two sets. The divine: fearlessness, purity, generosity, self-control, truthfulness, non-violence, compassion, forgiveness. The demonic: hypocrisy, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness. The first set frees you; the second binds you. And he names the three gates that lead to ruin: desire, anger and greed.

The big ideas

  • Qualities, not people. The chapter describes tendencies anyone can have. Arjuna is told not to worry: he was born with the divine ones.
  • The demonic worldview. Krishna sketches someone who thinks the world has no truth and no order, nothing but desire, and lives to pile up more: I got this today, I'll get that tomorrow, I beat that rival and I'll beat the next.
  • Three gates to ruin. Desire, anger and greed destroy the self. Leave all three, and you start to do what is good for you.
  • A guide, not a whim. Someone who throws out guidance and follows their cravings reaches neither happiness nor perfection.

Desire, anger and greed: these three are the gates to a self-destroying hell. So give up all three.

Verse 16.21 · in our words
From the text

Krishna's portrait of the demonic mind is uncomfortably modern. Such people don't know what to start and what to stop. They are full of insatiable desire and hypocrisy and pride. Caught in worry that only ends at death, they treat enjoying things as the highest goal, convinced that's all there is. Bound by hundreds of hopes, they try to pile up wealth by any means. "I'm the master, I enjoy, I'm successful, strong and happy. I'm rich and well-born. Who else is like me?"

In plain words

Pick one of the divine qualities you'd like more of, fearlessness, generosity, patience, and one of the three gates you know you walk through most often. Watch both this week.

Chapter 17 · Shraddhatraya Vibhaga Yoga

The Three Kinds of Faith

In short

Arjuna asks about people who don't follow the rules of scripture but still act with faith. Krishna says everyone's faith takes the shape of their nature: a person is made of their faith. Then he runs food, sacrifice, self-discipline and giving through the three gunas, so you can see which strand is driving each of them.

The big ideas

  • You are your faith. Whatever someone's faith is, that's what they are. Faith isn't just belief; it's the direction of a whole life.
  • Food in three strands. Sattvic food is fresh, nourishing and pleasant. Rajasic food is too hot, sour, salty or sharp, and brings pain. Tamasic food is stale, spoilt or left over.
  • Discipline of body, speech and mind. Speech that doesn't upset, that is true, kind and useful, is discipline. So is a calm, gentle, quiet mind.
  • Giving in three strands. A gift given because it's right, at the right time to someone who needs it, expecting nothing back, is sattvic. One given for return or grudgingly is rajasic. One given with contempt is tamasic.

Everyone's faith fits their nature. A person is made of their faith: whatever their faith is, so are they.

Verse 17.3 · in our words
From the text

The chapter ends with three words used at the start of every offering: Om Tat Sat. Om is the sound of the absolute. Tat, "that", is said by those who act without wanting a reward. Sat means both "what is real" and "what is good", and is used for good deeds and steady practice. Anything done without faith, Krishna adds, whether a sacrifice, a gift or a hardship, is called asat, "unreal", and is worth nothing here or after.

In plain words

Run one ordinary choice through the three strands today: what you eat, how you speak to someone, a gift you give. Is it clear, driven or dull?

Chapter 18 · Moksha Sannyasa Yoga

Liberation through Renunciation

In short

The longest chapter pulls everything together. Real renunciation isn't giving up action, it's giving up attachment to its fruit. Krishna lists five factors behind every action, and "I" is only one of them. He sorts knowledge, action, doers, understanding, resolve and happiness by the three strands. Then he says: I've told you everything; think it over and do as you choose. Arjuna picks up his bow.

The big ideas

  • Give up the clinging, not the work. Duties like sacrifice, giving and discipline shouldn't be dropped. Do them, and drop the attachment.
  • You are not the only doer. Every action has five causes: the body, the doer, the instruments, the many efforts, and the unseen hand of fate. Anyone who thinks "I alone did this" doesn't see.
  • Poison first, nectar later. Happiness that starts like poison and ends like nectar is sattvic. Happiness that starts like nectar and ends like poison is rajasic.
  • Your own nature will move you. If out of pride you decide not to fight, Krishna says, it won't last: your own nature will make you. Better your own path done imperfectly than someone else's done well.

Let go of every duty and take refuge in me alone. I will free you from every wrong. Do not grieve.

Verse 18.66 · in our words
From the text

After 700 verses, Krishna doesn't order Arjuna to fight. He says: I've given you knowledge more secret than any secret. Reflect on it fully, then do what you wish. He asks: have you heard this with full attention, Arjuna? Has your confusion gone? Arjuna answers: my delusion is gone. Through your grace I remember who I am. I stand firm, my doubts are cleared, I will do as you say. Back in the palace, Sanjaya tells the blind king: wherever Krishna and Arjuna stand together, there is fortune, victory, wealth and what is right.

Worth knowing

Verse 18.66 is called the "last word" (charama shloka) by some traditions and is read very differently by different schools: as a call to total surrender, as the end of ritual duty, or as giving up the sense of being the doer. It is probably the single most commented-on verse in the Gita.

In plain words

Do the work that is yours. When it goes well, remember you were one of five causes. When it goes badly, the same. Then pick up your bow again.

Afterword

Act, offer, let go

At the end of the eighteenth chapter Arjuna picks up his bow. Nothing about the battle has changed; what changed is how he sees it. The Gita's practice fits into an ordinary day: do the next right thing, do it well, and hand over the result. Keep a small log of what you did and how tightly you held the outcome.
