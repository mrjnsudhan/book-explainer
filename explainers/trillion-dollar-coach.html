A chapter-by-chapter guide to the book by Eric Schmidt, Jonathan Rosenberg and Alan Eagle

Trillion Dollar Coach,
Explained

Bill Campbell was a former college football coach who never wrote code, yet for decades he was the person Silicon Valley's leaders called when things got hard: Steve Jobs at Apple, the founders and executives at Google, and many more. When he died in 2016, three of the people he coached at Google set out to write down how he did it. Trillion Dollar Coach is the result: a playbook for managing people as a coach would, built on trust, candour, teams and, unusually for a business book, love. This guide takes it section by section, with a lab in each where you can try the idea.

The book in one line

The best managers are coaches: they win by making their people and their teams better, and they care about them as people while they do it.

Chapter 1 · The Bill problem

The Bill Problem

The book opens with a goodbye. Bill Campbell, the coach behind some of the most successful leaders in technology, has died, and the people he coached are trying to work out what exactly he did for them, and how anyone could do it now that he's gone.

The idea behind this chapter

Teams do better with a coach. There was only one Bill, so the answer is for managers themselves to learn to coach.

Section 1 · A football coach in Silicon Valley

Who Bill Campbell Was

In short

Bill Campbell was a college football coach who became a marketing executive, a CEO and then, for decades, the trusted coach of founders and chief executives. He never wrote code, but he understood people better than almost anyone the authors had worked with.

The big ideas

  • From the field to the boardroom. He played and later coached football at Columbia University, then moved into advertising and business, arriving at Apple in 1983 to run marketing.
  • An operator, not just an adviser. He was CEO of Claris, GO Corporation and Intuit, so his advice came from having done the job himself.
  • The coach of coaches. From the late 1990s he spent much of his time coaching leaders, including Steve Jobs, and from 2001 the leadership team at Google, often without taking pay.
  • Why "trillion dollar". The companies he helped build were together worth well over a trillion dollars. The title is the authors' nod to that, not a claim about Bill's own wealth.
From the book

In April 2016, a big crowd gathered on a school football field in Atherton, California, to say goodbye to Bill Campbell. Famous chief executives and founders stood alongside the parents and kids from the youth flag football team he had coached for years. The authors, who had worked with Bill at Google for well over a decade, realised that almost everyone there could tell a story about how Bill had helped them, and that his way of working had never been written down.

Try it this week

Think of the person who has done the most for your career. Write down one specific thing they did. That is the kind of thing this book is trying to teach.

In plain words

Bill Campbell was a former football coach who became one of the most trusted advisers in technology. The book is three of the people he coached trying to pass on what he taught them.

Section 2 · Every team needs a coach

The Bill Problem

In short

Teams of talented people still need someone whose job is to make the team work: to build trust, surface problems and keep people pulling together. Bill did that for many companies. The authors call it the Bill problem: he can't be replaced, so managers have to learn to do it themselves.

The big ideas

  • Talent isn't enough. A group of stars who don't trust each other will lose to a team that does. Bill's job was the bit between the people.
  • Coaching is for the best, not the struggling. Top athletes have coaches. Bill worked with people who were already successful and helped them get better.
  • The manager is the coach. You can't hire a Bill for every team, so the skills of coaching have to become part of every manager's job.
  • Coaching is learnable. What Bill did can be broken into habits: how to run a meeting, how to give feedback, how to show you care. That's what the rest of the book does.
From the book

When Eric Schmidt joined Google as CEO in 2001, board member John Doerr suggested he work with Bill Campbell. Eric, already an experienced chief executive, was not keen: he didn't think he needed a coach. Within a short time he had changed his mind. Bill didn't tell him how to run Google's technology. He helped him run the team, and the weekly conversations went on for fifteen years.

Try it this week

At your next team meeting, watch for the moments when people talk past each other or avoid each other. That gap is where a coach works.

In plain words

Good people don't automatically make a good team. Someone has to work on how they work together, and in most places that someone has to be the manager.

Chapter 2 · Your title makes you a manager

Your Title Makes You a Manager, Your People Make You a Leader

The longest chapter is the practical one: how Bill wanted managers to run one-to-ones and staff meetings, face the biggest problem in the room, make decisions, handle brilliant but difficult people, and pay and part with people decently.

The idea behind this chapter

A manager's first job is the success and well-being of their people. Good process is how you show it.

Section 3 · It's the people, starting with the 1:1

Your Title Makes You a Manager

In short

Bill's favourite line was that your title makes you a manager, but your people make you a leader. A manager's top priority is the well-being and success of their people, and the most important place that happens is the one-to-one.

The big ideas

  • People come first. Companies succeed because of their people. Helping them do their best work is the core of the manager's job, not a side duty.
  • Prepare for 1:1s. Bill took one-to-ones seriously and thought about them in advance. They were for the person, not for a status update you could read in an email.
  • Start with the human. He opened with small talk, about family or the weekend, to get to know the person before getting to work.
  • Four things to cover. Then he went through performance on the job, relationships with peers, how well they were leading their own team, and innovation and best practices. He'd ask the person to rate themselves and compare it with his own view.
From the book

The authors describe Bill's one-to-one framework almost like a checklist on a notepad. After the personal catch-up, he'd go through four areas: job performance (hitting the numbers, shipping the product), relationships with peers (which he thought were often neglected and very important), management and leadership (were they coaching their own people?) and innovation (were they moving forward and learning from others?). He liked to have the other person assess themselves first. The most useful conversation was usually where their view and his didn't match.

Try it this week

Before your next one-to-one, write down your view on each of the four areas. Ask the other person to do the same, then compare.

In plain words

Your job title gives you authority, but people choose whether to follow you. Show them they come first, and use the one-to-one to talk about their work, their peers, their team and how they're improving.

Next: Trip reports →
Section 4 · Make the staff meeting human

Start with Trip Reports

In short

Bill suggested starting staff meetings with something unexpected: trip reports. Before the numbers, people share what they did over the weekend or on a recent trip. It sounds trivial, but it gets people seeing each other as people, which makes the hard conversations later go better.

The big ideas

  • Warm up the room. A few minutes of personal stories relax people and connect them before the work begins.
  • People, not roles. Knowing that a colleague just ran a marathon or visited family makes it harder to treat them as an obstacle in a debate.
  • Agenda matters. Bill wanted staff meetings for the important, cross-team issues and real debate, not for updates that could be read beforehand.
  • Get everyone talking. Once people have spoken about something easy, they're more likely to speak up about something hard.
From the book

At Google, Eric Schmidt began his Monday staff meetings with trip reports, following Bill's advice. Eric would often share stories from his own travels, and others would describe their weekends, a family event or a conference. The authors say it set a tone: the people in the room were colleagues and friends, not rival heads of departments, and that made the debates that followed more honest.

Try it this week

Open your next team meeting with two minutes of "what did you do this weekend?" Go first, and keep it short.

In plain words

Before you get into the work, give people a moment to be people. It makes them more open, and that pays off when the meeting gets difficult.

Section 5 · Bring the elephant out

Run to the Biggest Problem

In short

Teams naturally talk about easy things and avoid the hard one. Bill did the opposite: he'd find the biggest problem, the elephant in the room, name it, and get the team working on it, without blaming anyone.

The big ideas

  • Find the biggest problem. Ask what's really holding the team back. It's often the thing nobody has put on the agenda.
  • Name it out loud. An elephant that isn't named keeps growing. Saying it plainly is a relief to everyone who already knew.
  • Make it the team's problem. Get the group around it together rather than pinning it on one person or one department.
  • Don't let it fester. Problems that are left alone usually get worse and more expensive.
From the book

The authors describe Bill turning up to a meeting, listening for a while, and then asking about the one thing nobody wanted to discuss: a product that was slipping, two leaders who weren't speaking, a strategy everyone privately doubted. He didn't do it to embarrass anyone. Once the problem was on the table, the team could stop circling it and start solving it.

Try it this week

Ask your team: "What's the one problem we keep not talking about?" Then put it at the top of the next agenda.

In plain words

Don't let meetings drift around the edges. Find the biggest problem, say it plainly, and get everyone working on it together.

Next: The best idea →
Section 6 · Decide from first principles

Get to the Best Idea, Not Consensus

In short

Bill didn't want everyone to agree for the sake of it. A manager's job is to make sure every view is heard and the best idea wins. When the team is stuck, the manager breaks the tie, guided by first principles: the basic truths about what the company is trying to do.

The big ideas

  • Consensus isn't the goal. Everyone agreeing often means a watered-down compromise. The goal is the best answer.
  • Hear every perspective. Make sure the quiet and dissenting voices are heard before deciding, so people trust the process even when they lose the argument.
  • Lead from first principles. When a choice is hard, go back to the basic truths of the business, like what's best for the user, and decide from there.
  • The manager breaks the tie. If debate doesn't settle it, the leader decides, explains why, and the team commits.
From the book

The authors describe Bill pushing leaders not to settle for the option that annoyed the fewest people. When a team was split, he'd ask what principle should decide it. At Google that was often the idea of focusing on the user. Once the principle was named, the decision usually became clearer, and the people who had argued for the other side could accept it because they understood the reason.

Try it this week

Next time your team is split, ask "what principle should decide this?" before anyone argues for their option again.

In plain words

Don't aim for everyone to agree. Hear every view, decide based on what you're fundamentally trying to achieve, and if you have to make the call, make it and explain why.

Next: The aberrant genius →
Section 7 · Brilliant, and difficult

Manage the Aberrant Genius

In short

Every company has a few people who are exceptionally good and also hard to work with. Bill's advice was to protect and support them, as long as their behaviour isn't unethical or abusive and the value they create outweighs the toll they take on everyone else.

The big ideas

  • Stars can be difficult. Some of the most valuable people are also the most demanding, impatient or eccentric. That alone isn't a reason to lose them.
  • Spend time on them. Bill invested in these people, coaching them and helping them see how their behaviour affected the team.
  • Weigh value against toll. Keep asking whether what they deliver is worth the cost to colleagues and to the manager's time.
  • There is a hard line. Unethical behaviour or abusing colleagues isn't a trade-off. No amount of talent outweighs it.
From the book

Bill had a lot of experience with brilliant, difficult people, including at Apple. The authors say his approach was patient but clear: he'd work hard to help a gifted person change the behaviours that hurt the team, and he'd defend them when others wanted them gone. But he was equally clear that if someone crossed an ethical line or mistreated people, their genius stopped mattering.

Try it this week

If you have a brilliant, difficult person on your team, write down two things: what they contribute that nobody else does, and what it costs everyone else. Then have an honest conversation about the second list.

In plain words

Don't throw away exceptional people just because they're difficult. Coach them and protect them, as long as they stay ethical and decent and are worth what they cost the team.

Next: Pay and parting →
Section 8 · Two moments that reveal how you value people

Money Isn't Just Money, and Let People Leave with Dignity

In short

Bill believed pay was about more than money: it tells people how much they are valued, and they feel it emotionally. He was just as careful when someone had to go. Be generous, be honest, and let them leave with their dignity and their story intact.

The big ideas

  • Pay is a message. Compensation signals respect and recognition. People compare it with peers and read it as a verdict on their worth.
  • Pay people well. Bill tended to argue for paying good people fairly and generously, because the emotional effect matters as much as the amount.
  • Leaving with dignity. When someone has to go, treat them well: be clear about why, be generous where you can, and let them shape how it's announced.
  • Everyone is watching. The people who stay see how you treat those who leave, and that tells them how they'll be treated.
From the book

The authors describe Bill coaching executives through the hardest part of the job, letting someone go. His advice was to take responsibility for the decision rather than hide behind process, to be generous, and to let the person save face, for example by letting them describe their departure in their own words. He knew the way you handle the exit stays with the person for years, and with the team that watches it.

Try it this week

Think about the last time someone left your team. Ask yourself whether they'd describe how it was handled as fair and dignified. If not, what would you change next time?

In plain words

How you pay people and how you part with them both send loud messages about how much you value them. Make those messages generous and respectful.

Next: Coach the coachable →
Chapter 3 · Build an envelope of trust

Build an Envelope of Trust

Everything Bill did rested on trust. The authors call it an envelope: a space where people know you are on their side, so they can hear the hard truth and take the bigger risk.

The idea behind this chapter

Coach only the coachable, listen with your full attention, tell the truth with care, and believe in people more than they believe in themselves.

Section 9 · Who is worth coaching

Only Coach the Coachable

In short

Bill was choosy about whom he coached. Coaching only works if the other person is willing to hear it. He looked for four qualities: honesty, humility, a willingness to persevere and work hard, and a constant openness to learning.

The big ideas

  • Honesty. You can't help someone who won't tell you the truth about what's going on, including their own mistakes.
  • Humility. People who think they already know everything don't change. Humility makes room for feedback.
  • Perseverance and hard work. Coaching asks people to change habits, which takes effort over time.
  • Openness to learning. The best leaders stay curious and keep looking for ways to get better, however senior they are.
From the book

The authors say Bill would sometimes decline to work with someone, even someone important, if he didn't think they'd listen. With those he did coach, honesty ran both ways: he expected people to bring him their real problems, not a polished version. The people who got the most from him were the ones who could hear "you're doing this wrong" and go and fix it.

Try it this week

Rate yourself honestly on the four qualities. Ask someone you trust to rate you too, and notice where you disagree.

In plain words

Coaching only works on people who are honest, humble, hard-working and keen to learn. Look for those qualities in others, and work on them in yourself.

Section 10 · Full attention, and stories not instructions

Practise Free-Form Listening

In short

When you were with Bill, he was entirely with you: no phone, no glancing at the door, no waiting for his turn to talk. He listened, asked questions, and rather than telling you what to do, he'd often tell a story and let you work out the answer.

The big ideas

  • Give full attention. Put away the phone and the laptop. People notice immediately whether you're really listening.
  • Ask, don't assume. Questions help you understand the real problem, which is often not the one first described.
  • Don't be prescriptive. Bill rarely gave orders. He'd share a story from his own experience and let you decide what it meant for you.
  • Let people own the answer. A decision someone reaches themselves is one they believe in and carry out.
From the book

Several people in the book recall the same thing about time with Bill: he never looked at his phone. His attention made people feel that their problem mattered. And when they asked him what to do, he'd often answer with a story: something that had happened at Apple or Intuit, or on the football field. The lesson was in there, but it was theirs to find, and so the decision stayed theirs too.

Try it this week

In your next one-to-one, leave your phone in another room, and don't give any advice for the first ten minutes. Only ask questions.

In plain words

Listen with your whole attention, ask questions, and resist telling people what to do. Share your experience and let them reach the answer themselves.

Section 11 · Complete candour inside the envelope

Be Relentlessly Honest, with Care

In short

Bill was blunt, often colourfully so, and he told people hard truths. It worked because they knew he was on their side. Honesty without care feels like an attack; care without honesty is useless. He combined both, and usually criticised in private and praised in public.

The big ideas

  • Tell the truth. Give people the real feedback, as soon as you can, even when it's uncomfortable.
  • Show you care. Feedback lands when the person knows you want them to succeed. Trust is what makes candour bearable.
  • Be specific. Say exactly what happened and what better looks like. Vague criticism is just discouraging.
  • Criticise privately, praise publicly. Hard feedback in front of others humiliates. Praise in front of others multiplies.
From the book

People who worked with Bill describe him swearing, hugging and telling them flatly when they'd messed up, sometimes all in one conversation. They didn't resent it, because they knew he cared about them and wanted them to win. The authors argue that the trust he built first is what made his bluntness valuable: inside the envelope of trust, people could hear things they'd have rejected from anyone else.

Try it this week

Think of feedback you've been putting off. Write it in two sentences: one that says exactly what happened, and one that says why you're telling them (because you want them to succeed). Then say it this week, in private.

In plain words

Tell people the truth, clearly and soon, and make sure they know you're on their side. Criticise in private, praise in public.

Next: Believe in people →
Section 12 · Be the evangelist for courage

Believe in People More Than They Believe in Themselves

In short

Bill pushed people to be braver than they'd be on their own. He believed in them more than they believed in themselves, and he said so, which gave them the courage to take on bigger jobs and bolder bets.

The big ideas

  • Courage is contagious. When someone you respect says "you can do this", you start to believe it.
  • Push for the bigger job. Bill encouraged people to take roles they felt unready for, and they usually grew into them.
  • Back bold moves. He encouraged leaders to aim higher and take risks, and he stood by them when it got hard.
  • It has to be real. Belief only works if it's genuine and grounded in what you've seen the person do.
From the book

A recurring scene in the book: someone is offered a big role, or thinking about a bold move, and has doubts. They talk to Bill, and he tells them, emphatically, that they're ready. Several of the people the authors interviewed said it was Bill's confidence in them, more than their own, that got them to say yes. Once they'd done it, the confidence became their own.

Try it this week

Pick one person on your team who is better than they think they are. Tell them specifically what you've seen them do, and what bigger thing you think they could take on.

In plain words

Many people underrate themselves. A coach sees what they could do and tells them, which gives them the courage to try.

Chapter 4 · Team first

Team First

Bill was a football coach first, and he saw companies as teams. This chapter is about choosing the right players, looking after the team before the problem, closing the gaps between people and winning the right way.

The idea behind this chapter

When something goes wrong, look at the team before the task. A strong team solves the problem; a fractured one can't.

Section 13 · Smart, hard-working, honest and team-first

Team First: Pick the Right Players

In short

Bill wanted teams of people who put the team first. When hiring or choosing, he looked for intelligence, hard work, integrity, grit and the ability to see around corners, and he valued what he called heart: people who care about the team more than about themselves.

The big ideas

  • Smart and hard-working. The basics: people who can learn fast and are willing to put in the effort.
  • Integrity is non-negotiable. However talented, someone who lies or cuts ethical corners damages the whole team.
  • Grit and heart. People who get back up after a setback, and who care about the team winning more than about their own credit.
  • Team first. Bill rated people who made others better over lone stars, because winning is a team result.
From the book

As a football coach, Bill knew that the most talented player isn't always the most valuable one. The authors say he brought the same view to companies: he'd ask whether someone would put the team first, whether they'd keep going when it got hard, and whether they could be trusted completely. A brilliant hire who didn't care about the team, he thought, could end up costing more than they gave.

Try it this week

Look at the last role you hired for or the last project team you formed. Did you weigh integrity and team-first attitude as seriously as skills? Add one question about them to your next interview.

In plain words

Build teams from smart, hard-working people with integrity and grit, who care more about the team than about themselves.

Next: Work the team →
Section 14 · Pair people and fill the gaps between them

Work the Team, Then the Problem

In short

When a problem came up, Bill's first question was about the team: are the right people on it, and are they working well together? He paired people up on projects to build relationships, and paid close attention to the small gaps between colleagues before they became big ones.

The big ideas

  • Team before task. Most stubborn problems are really team problems: the wrong people, or people who don't trust each other.
  • Pair people. Give two colleagues a joint project. Working side by side builds the relationship faster than any off-site.
  • Fill the gaps between people. Notice small frictions, misunderstandings and missing connections, and close them early.
  • Peer feedback. Ask team members how their peers help them, so people know collaboration counts.
From the book

The authors describe Bill noticing that two executives, each strong on their own, weren't working well together. Rather than lecture them, he'd suggest they take on a project jointly, maybe a joint review, a shared customer problem or a plan they had to present together. By the end, they had a working relationship. The team's next problem got solved faster, because the gap between them had been closed.

Try it this week

Find the two people on your team who work together least well and most need to. Give them one small joint project this month.

In plain words

Before you attack a problem, check the team. Pair people up so they build real working relationships, and close small gaps before they grow.

Next: Win right →
Section 15 · Compete hard, play fair

Win, but Win Right

In short

Bill hated losing. He wanted his teams to win and win big. But he cared just as much about how they won: with integrity, keeping their word, treating competitors and colleagues fairly, and building something they could be proud of.

The big ideas

  • Winning matters. Bill was intensely competitive. Being nice was never an excuse for losing.
  • How you win matters too. Cutting corners, misleading people or betraying trust might win today and cost everything tomorrow.
  • Keep your word. A team whose word is good wins partners, customers and talent over time.
  • Be gracious. Win with class and lose with class. People remember how you behaved.
From the book

Bill's football background gave him a fierce desire to win, and the authors say he never lost it. He also cared about playing within the rules and about the people on the other side of the deal. To him, a win built on broken promises or bad behaviour wasn't really a win: it damaged the team's reputation and its own sense of who it was.

Try it this week

Think of a recent win for your team. Would you be comfortable if every detail of how you got it were public? If not, what would winning right have looked like?

In plain words

Play to win, and play fair. A win you have to hide isn't worth it.

Chapter 5 · The power of love

The Power of Love

This is the part that surprises most readers. Bill hugged people, knew their families and built communities wherever he went, and the authors argue this wasn't a soft extra but a big part of why teams around him performed.

The idea behind this chapter

Caring about colleagues as whole people, openly, makes teams stronger. It's allowed at work, and it works.

Section 16 · Care about the whole person

The Power of Love

In short

This is the book's most unusual claim: love belongs at work. Bill cared about the people he worked with as whole people, their families, their health and their lives, and showed it openly, with hugs, calls and practical help. The authors argue this made teams stronger.

The big ideas

  • See the whole person. Colleagues have families, worries and passions. Knowing about them builds the bond that work alone doesn't.
  • Show it. Bill didn't hide his care. He hugged people, remembered names of children and turned up when life got hard.
  • Build community. He brought people together outside work, at games, events and dinners, and created places where they could connect.
  • Care is an advantage. Teams who feel cared for trust each other more, take more risks and stick together through hard times.
From the book

Story after story in this chapter shows Bill showing up for people: phoning when someone's family member was ill, helping find the right doctor, remembering the names of people's kids. He was a part-owner of the Old Pro, a sports bar in Palo Alto, which became a gathering place, and he organised trips to big games for groups of friends. He also coached youth flag football for years. The authors' point is that none of this was separate from his coaching. It was the foundation of it.

Try it this week

Learn one thing this week about a colleague's life outside work: a family member's name, a hobby, something they're dealing with. Remember it and ask about it next time.

In plain words

Care about your colleagues as people, not just as workers, and don't be shy about showing it. It builds teams that trust each other.

Next: The yardstick →
Chapter 6 · The yardstick

The Yardstick

The short closing chapter asks how to measure a life like Bill's. Not by titles or money, the authors say, but by the people he helped become better at their jobs and in their lives.

The idea behind this chapter

Measure yourself the way Bill would: by how many people you helped.

Section 17 · Count the people you helped

The Yardstick

In short

How do you measure a life like Bill's? The authors' answer is simple: by the people he helped. The yardstick of a coach, or any manager, is how many people became better at their jobs and in their lives because of them.

The big ideas

  • Not titles or money. Bill's legacy isn't a company or a fortune. It's the people he made better.
  • Helping compounds. People Bill helped went on to lead and coach others, so his effect kept spreading.
  • Make it your measure. Ask yourself at the end of the year: who did I help, and how?
  • Pass it on. The authors wrote the book so that Bill's way of working could keep spreading through the people who read it.
From the book

The book ends by returning to the crowd at Bill's memorial. Almost everyone there had a personal story: a call at the right moment, a hard truth that changed a career, a hug when it was needed. The authors suggest the right yardstick for Bill, and for the rest of us, is that crowd: how many people are better off because we were their coach.

Try it this week

Name three people you'll make a point of helping this quarter, and write down how. Check back at the end of it.

In plain words

Measure yourself by the people you help grow. That's the yardstick Bill lived by, and the one the authors want readers to adopt.
