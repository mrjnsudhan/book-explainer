A chapter-by-chapter guide to Anthony de Mello's talks

Awareness,
Explained

Anthony de Mello was an Indian Jesuit priest and psychotherapist who ran retreats around the world, and he had one message he never tired of: most of us go through life asleep. We run on conditioning, chase approval, and blame other people for feelings that start in us. Awareness is a record of those talks, published after his death, in 56 short, funny, often blunt sections. This guide groups them into nineteen chapters, with a lab in every one where you can try the idea for yourself.

The book in one line

You don't need to add anything to be happy. You need to wake up, and see that the things you thought you couldn't live without were never you.

Part one · waking up

Waking Up

De Mello opens his retreat by telling the audience they are asleep, that they don't really want to wake up, and that he can't wake them anyway. Then he goes after the things that keep people asleep: happiness with conditions, renunciation, and the comfortable belief that our good deeds are pure.

The idea behind this part

Spirituality means waking up. Nobody can do it for you, and the first step is admitting you'd rather stay asleep.

Chapter 1 · Most people are asleep

On Waking Up

The book's sections On Waking Up · Will I Be of Help to You in This Retreat?
In short

For de Mello, spirituality means one thing: waking up. Most people are born, grow up, marry, work and die without ever waking. And when they come for help, they don't want to be woken. They want their broken toys mended so they can go back to sleep.

The big ideas

  • Asleep in a nightmare. People live with fear, conflict and loneliness and call it normal. They never question the beliefs and habits running the show.
  • All is well, say the mystics. Mystics of every religion agree on one strange thing: in spite of the mess, all is well. The problem isn't the world. It's that we're asleep and can't see it.
  • Relief, not a cure. Most people want to feel better, not to change. Give me back my spouse, my job, my reputation, they say, and I'll be fine. That is asking for relief. A cure is painful, so they avoid it.
  • Nobody can wake you. De Mello tells his audience he doesn't expect to help or harm anyone. Whatever happens to you, you'll have done it. A talk is like rain: the same rain grows flowers in a garden and thorns in a marsh.
From the talks

De Mello starts with a scene from Spanish television. A father knocks on his son's door and tells Jaime to get up for school. Jaime says he won't go, and he has three reasons: school is boring, the kids tease him, and he hates it. The father says he'll give three reasons why Jaime must go. First, it's his duty. Second, he is forty-five years old. Third, he is the headmaster. Wake up, says de Mello. You've grown up. You're too big to be asleep.

In plain words

Next time you want something fixed, ask whether you want the toy repaired or whether you want to see why it mattered so much. The first gets you back to sleep. The second is the start of waking up.

Next: Happiness, provided that →
Chapter 2 · Happiness, provided that…

On the Proper Kind of Selfishness, and On Wanting Happiness

The book's sections On the Proper Kind of Selfishness · On Wanting Happiness
In short

The first step to waking up is honesty: you don't really want to be happy. You want to be happy provided you keep this person, get that job, are thought well of. And you've been taught that simply wanting to be happy is selfish.

The big ideas

  • Try the test. Picture someone you love and say silently, "I'd rather be happy than have you." Most people feel guilty saying it. That guilt shows how thoroughly we've been taught to tie happiness to other people.
  • Conditions, conditions. We don't want happiness, we want happiness on terms. Every condition you attach is one more way to be unhappy when life doesn't meet it.
  • Sacrifice can be selfish too. Love that asks you to be miserable as proof of it is also self-interest. It only feels nobler.
  • The most practical thing there is. Spirituality, meaning waking up, deals with fear, inner conflict and loneliness. Business, politics and science, for all their success, don't touch those.
From the talks

De Mello once heard a retreat speaker say that the test of love is sacrifice. He put the idea to a woman listening: would she want the man she loved to love her at the cost of his own happiness? Yes, she said. Then, said de Mello, you'll have two unhappy people instead of one. But long live love!

In plain words

Notice the small print on your happiness: "provided she calls", "provided the meeting goes well". You don't have to give up wanting those things. Just see that you put the conditions there, and you can take them off.

Next: Listen and unlearn →
Chapter 3 · Understanding, not giving up

Psychology, Renunciation, and Listening to Unlearn

The book's sections Are We Talking About Psychology in This Spirituality Course? · Neither Is Renunciation the Solution · Listen and Unlearn
In short

Psychology can ease the pain and renunciation can tighten your grip, but neither wakes you up. What does is understanding: seeing a thing for what it's really worth, so it drops away by itself. And to understand, you have to listen openly and be ready to unlearn.

The big ideas

  • Sick of being sick. Relief can keep you asleep. Sometimes people have to hit rock bottom, to be thoroughly fed up, before they'll look at what's really going on.
  • What you fight, you're tied to. Renouncing something keeps your mind fixed on it. De Mello tells of a guru whose visitors were a prostitute who talked only about God and a priest who talked only about sex.
  • See its true value. You don't have to give a thing up. See through it, see what it's actually worth to you, and it loses its hold, the way a child's toy does once the child grows.
  • Faith means openness. Belief gives you security. Faith risks it for the truth. Don't swallow what you hear and don't reject it on reflex: test it the way the Buddha said a goldsmith tests gold, by cutting, scraping and melting it.
From the talks

De Mello tells of a boy in a school for children with learning difficulties, modelling something from a lump of cow dung. His teacher asks what he's making. "A teacher." The principal comes by and asks. "A principal." Then the school psychologist asks, and the boy says, "Not enough dung." The school had labelled him slow. De Mello's point: what everyone agrees is normal isn't necessarily right, and every new idea starts as a minority of one.

In plain words

When you want to be rid of a habit or a craving, stop fighting it for a moment and look at it. What does it actually give you? What do you think it gives you? The gap between those two is where it loosens.

Next: The masks of charity →
Chapter 4 · Kinds of self-interest

The Masquerade of Charity, and What's on Your Mind?

The book's sections The Masquerade of Charity · What's on Your Mind?
In short

Most charity is self-interest in disguise. De Mello names three kinds: doing what pleases you, doing good so you can feel good, and the worst, doing good so you won't feel bad. Seeing this isn't cynical. It punctures pride, and it leaves room for the kind of goodness that doesn't notice itself.

The big ideas

  • Two kinds of selfishness. One is pleasing yourself. The other is pleasing yourself by pleasing others. The second feels like virtue, which makes it the harder one to see.
  • The worst kind. Doing good to avoid guilt or criticism. You do it, you resent it, and the other person usually feels it.
  • Call it what it is. A woman who volunteers because she needs to feel needed isn't doing wrong. De Mello just asks her to call it enlightened self-interest rather than charity.
  • Life is a banquet. Yet most people are starving, like castaways off the coast of Brazil who died of thirst on a raft, not knowing they were floating on fresh water from a great river.
From the talks

De Mello admits to his own worst kind of charity. Someone he'd rather not see turns up at his door in the evening, and he says "Come in", because saying no would make him feel like a bad person. Then he sits through the visit resenting every minute. Real goodness, he says, looks more like the people in the Gospel parable who are thanked for feeding the hungry and ask, puzzled, "When did we do that?" They hadn't noticed they were being good.

In plain words

You don't need pure motives to do good. You need honest ones. When you help, notice whether you're enjoying it, polishing your image or dodging guilt. Admitting it takes the pride out, and the help gets lighter.

Next: Nobody lets you down →
Part two · seeing yourself

Seeing Yourself

Once you admit everyone is self-interested, yourself included, two things change: other people stop disappointing you, and you can start to watch yourself honestly. These sections teach the core practice, self-observation, and the distinction at the centre of the book: the "I" who watches and the "me" it watches.

The idea behind this part

Watch yourself as if you were watching someone else. What you can watch, you are not.

Chapter 5 · Nobody lets you down

Good, Bad, or Lucky, and Our Illusion About Others

The book's sections Good, Bad, or Lucky · Our Illusion About Others
In short

Self-preservation is our deepest instinct, so everyone is self-interested, in refined ways or crude ones. You aren't better than the criminal, only luckier or more aware. Expect people to look after themselves and they can't disappoint you. Oddly, that's when you can start to love them.

The big ideas

  • Lucky, not virtuous. People who win the lottery say they were lucky, not that they earned it. De Mello says the same of virtue and awareness. There's nothing to be proud of.
  • At home with sinners. Someone outside Christianity once told de Mello that Jesus was at home with sinners because he saw he was no better than they were.
  • Disappointment is your illusion. A young man complains his girlfriend let him down. She didn't, says de Mello. You built her up and expected too much.
  • See through yourself first. Once you've seen your own self-interest, other people's stops shocking you. Then you can see them as they are, and love what you see.
From the talks

De Mello tells of an old man in a dream. He's paralysed, and he watches his old enemy climb the hill to his house carrying a gun. He can't run and he can't fight. Then it dawns on him: he can wake up. And he does. That's the book's whole idea in one scene. You don't have to defeat the nightmare. You have to see that it's a dream.

In plain words

When someone disappoints you, look at the picture of them you were holding. They didn't fall short of themselves. They fell short of your idea of them, and that idea was yours.

Next: Self-observation →
Chapter 6 · Watch yourself like someone else

Self-Observation, and Awareness Without Evaluating Everything

The book's sections Self-observation · Awareness Without Evaluating Everything
In short

The one thing nobody can do for you is self-observation: watching what goes on inside and around you as though it were happening to someone else. No judging, no justifying, no trying to fix. Most of us run on praise and blame like puppets on strings, and watching is how the strings show.

The big ideas

  • Observation, not self-absorption. Self-absorption is anxious preoccupation with yourself. Observation is calm watching from a little way off, the way the sky watches clouds pass.
  • "I am depressed" isn't true. Depression, anxiety and joy are experiences passing through. Say "there is depression" and you've already stepped back from it.
  • Understand, don't label. A dog trainer studies dogs to get tricks out of them. A scientist watches ants simply to understand them. Self-observation needs the scientist's attitude.
  • Change comes by itself. Awareness changes you without effort, the way a river makes its own banks. Forced discipline is a poor imitation.
From the talks

Visitors to de Mello's centre in India used to admire the trees and the climate, and he caught himself glowing with pride, as if he'd planted them. When people criticised India's poverty he felt ashamed of something he hadn't caused. Someone likes your shirt and you feel good. That's how mechanical we are. He jokes that the most liberating book title would be "I'm an Ass, You're an Ass": admit it openly and nobody can wound you with it.

In plain words

A few times today, catch yourself in the middle of a reaction and describe it as a reporter would: "He's pleased because someone liked his idea." Don't fix anything. Just watch, and notice who's watching.

Next: I and me →
Chapter 7 · I and me

The Illusion of Rewards, Finding Yourself, and Stripping Down to the "I"

The book's sections The Illusion of Rewards · Finding Yourself · Stripping Down to the "I"
In short

The most important question, says de Mello, isn't whether there's life after death but whether there's life before it. To find out, ask "Who am I?" Your body, name, job, religion, nationality, even your thoughts, are "me": things that change. The "I" is the one who notices them.

The big ideas

  • Life before death. Eternity isn't endless time later. It's timelessness, available now. Repentance means waking up, not weeping over the past.
  • Whose thoughts are these? Much of what you think, feel and believe came from your parents and your culture. Sit in any group and you can hear people's parents talking through them.
  • You aren't your labels. Change your party, your job or your country and you're still there. De Mello compares the body to Niagara Falls: the same name for water that's never the same twice.
  • Suffering is identification. The "I" is never hurt. It's the "me" that gets threatened, and suffering starts the moment you take the "me" to be you.
From the talks

Labels get people killed, says de Mello, and he tells it as a joke. In Belfast a man is stopped at gunpoint and asked, "Catholic or Protestant?" He thinks fast: "I'm Jewish." The gunman says, "Then I must be the luckiest Arab in Belfast." Neither man is a label. Both are ready to die or kill for one.

In plain words

Try describing yourself without a single label: no job, nationality, religion, role or adjective. It's surprisingly hard. Whatever is left when you run out of words is closer to the "I".

Next: No one can make you unhappy →
Chapter 8 · No one can make you unhappy

Negative Feelings, Dependence, and How Happiness Happens

The book's sections Negative Feelings Toward Others · On Dependence · How Happiness Happens
In short

A negative feeling about someone means you're caught in an illusion, and it's you who has to change, not them. Leaning on others for your happiness breeds fear and control. Happiness isn't a thrill you chase. It's what's left when you stop clinging, and trying too hard is what blocks it.

The big ideas

  • The feeling is in you. A hospital dietitian flinched every time someone at a staff meeting said the food stank. She'd identified herself with the food. The remark wasn't the problem; the identification was.
  • Love doesn't cling. Enjoy someone the way you enjoy a symphony: fully, while it plays, without demanding the orchestra never stop.
  • Loneliness isn't cured by company. It's cured by contact with reality. Distracting yourself with people only postpones it.
  • Thrills aren't happiness. Excitement is followed by a crash. Chasing the next high is addiction, whether the high is a drug, a win or a romance.
From the talks

De Mello retells a Chinese sage's poem about an archer. When he shoots for nothing, he has all his skill. When he shoots for a brass buckle, he gets nervous. When he shoots for a prize of gold, he goes blind, or sees two targets. His skill hasn't changed. The prize divides him, because now part of him is thinking about winning instead of shooting.

In plain words

When you feel bad about someone, look for what you were demanding of them. When you need someone to be happy, notice the fear that comes with the need. Enjoy people without holding on, and the fear has nothing to grip.

Part three · fear and labels

Fear and Labels

Here de Mello names what drives the sleep: fear of losing something, and the labels we cling to so we know who we are. He gives his most practical method, four steps for any negative feeling, and warns that even the wish to become a better person can be one more form of greed.

The idea behind this part

Behind anger is fear, and behind fear is a label you think you are. Understanding, not effort, sets you free.

Chapter 9 · Love or fear

Fear, Contact with Reality, and Good Religion

The book's sections Fear—The Root of Violence · Awareness and Contact with Reality · Good Religion—The Antithesis of Unawareness
In short

There are only two forces in us, de Mello says: love and fear. Fear is the root of violence, and behind every anger is a fear of losing something. Awareness means contact with reality as it is, and good religion should wake you up, not give you a comfortable substitute for being awake.

The big ideas

  • Anger hides fear. Think of the last time you were angry and ask what you were afraid of losing. There's almost always something.
  • The fearless are nonviolent. People who have nothing to lose have nothing to defend. Real nonviolence comes from fearlessness, not from gritted teeth.
  • A floodlight, not a spotlight. Concentration narrows to one thing. Awareness takes in everything, like a driver deep in an argument who still notices a door rattling.
  • Rules over people. Religion goes wrong when worship, rules and institutions matter more than love. A devotion that doesn't change you is a distraction.
From the talks

De Mello passes on a story he credits to Cardinal Martini. An Italian couple's wedding reception is rained out, so they ask the parish priest if they can hold it in the church: just a little cake, a little song. The party gets loud. The priest is beside himself. His assistant reminds him that Jesus went to a wedding. "I know," says the priest, "but they didn't have the Blessed Sacrament there."

In plain words

Next time you're angry, don't argue with the anger. Ask: what am I afraid of losing right now? Respect, control, being right, someone's love? Seeing the fear doesn't make it vanish, but it stops it driving.

Chapter 10 · Black paint in the air

Labels, and Obstacles to Happiness

The book's sections Labels · Obstacles to Happiness
In short

You don't find the truth by hunting for it, goes a Zen saying de Mello likes; you just drop your opinions. Identify with a label like success, failure or genius and you start clinging to it and dreading its loss. Suffering is the signal that an illusion has run into reality. Someone who doesn't identify is as untouched as the air you throw black paint into.

The big ideas

  • Drop your opinions. Truth isn't something you add. It appears when you stop insisting on what you already think.
  • Suffering is a signal. Pain tells you something's wrong in the body. Suffering tells you you're out of touch with reality, holding an illusion that's just collided with what is.
  • Who decides what success is? Success and failure are verdicts other people taught you to want or fear. They come and go; you don't.
  • The air stays clear. Throw black paint into the air and the air doesn't turn black. A person who hasn't identified with the labels is just as clean after praise or blame.
From the talks

De Mello describes a fifty-five-year-old small businessman sitting in a bar with a beer, comparing himself to the classmates who "made it": one runs a big corporation, another is a chief justice. He feels like a failure. De Mello is unimpressed by the list. Those are labels, he says, and the man is making himself miserable over a scoreboard someone else drew up.

In plain words

Notice which label you'd hate to lose most: clever, kind, successful, a good parent. That's where you're most easily hurt. You can still be all those things without needing to be.

Chapter 11 · Four steps for any bad feeling

Four Steps to Wisdom, and All's Right with the World

The book's sections Four Steps to Wisdom · All's Right with the World
In short

De Mello gives a plain method for any negative feeling. Name it. See that it's in you, not in the world. Don't identify with it. And understand that when you change, everything changes. Underneath sits the mystics' claim that all is well, even when you can't yet see it.

The big ideas

  • 1. Name the feeling. Most people don't even notice they're anxious or resentful; they just are. Notice it and give it a name.
  • 2. It's in you. No person or event has the power to make you unhappy. Reality provides the stimulus; you provide the reaction. The feeling comes from your programming, not from out there.
  • 3. It isn't you. Don't say "I am angry". Say "there is anger". It's a cloud passing, and you're the sky.
  • 4. When you change, everything changes. You don't have to fix the world first. Change the way you see, and the problem you were sure was out there looks different or disappears.
From the talks

De Mello's image for trying to fix your feelings by fixing the world: it's like changing your pen to improve your handwriting. You can buy a new job, a new partner, a new city, and the same hand writes the same way. Every time you're unhappy, he says, you have added something to reality that wasn't there.

In plain words

Try it on the next small irritation: name it, own it, step back from it, and see what happens to the situation when you're no longer fighting it. Small feelings are good practice for big ones.

Next: Don't try to change →
Chapter 12 · Don't try to change

Sleepwalking, Change as Greed, and A Changed Person

The book's sections Sleepwalking · Change as Greed · A Changed Person
In short

Wanting to change yourself out of dissatisfaction is more of the same greed: a spiritual ambition instead of a worldly one. De Mello's advice is not to push but to understand. Change that comes from awareness happens through you, not by you.

The big ideas

  • Sleepwalking. Most of the day we're on autopilot, reacting the way we were programmed to react, and calling it choice.
  • Change as greed. Trying hard to become a better, holier or calmer person is often the "me" wanting more for itself. The striving is part of the sleep.
  • Understand, don't change. It makes no sense to change what you don't understand. See a habit clearly enough and it changes on its own.
  • Others keep the old picture. A changed person may find the people around them still treat them as the old one. That's their image, not your reality.
From the talks

De Mello describes a priest who was lazy and tried system after system to make himself work harder, with no lasting success. What changed him in the end wasn't willpower but insight: he saw that the laziness went back to an old disappointment. Once he understood it, the effort he'd been forcing simply wasn't needed.

In plain words

Pick one habit you've been fighting. For a week, stop fighting and just watch it: when it shows up, what it gives you, what it protects. You may find you no longer need the fight.

Part four · dropping illusions

Dropping Illusions

The next run of sections takes apart the illusions that feel most like reality: that you must win the race, that you can't be happy without certain people and things, that words and concepts show you the world as it is, and that you need approval to live.

The idea behind this part

An attachment is a belief, not a fact. See through it and you keep the enjoyment without the fear.

Chapter 13 · Step off the treadmill

Arriving at Silence, Losing the Rat Race, and Permanent Worth

The book's sections Arriving at Silence · Losing the Rat Race · Permanent Worth
In short

Truth lies beyond words, so deep knowing ends in silence. The race for success and status burns the energy you need for living, and the awake person is untroubled by honour or disgrace. Your worth was never at stake in the race in the first place.

The big ideas

  • Words run out. Near the end of his life Thomas Aquinas stopped writing. After an experience he couldn't describe, he said everything he had written was straw.
  • The race has no finish. Whoever you compare yourself with moves too. Win and you find a new group to measure against.
  • Honour and disgrace. An awakened person is indifferent to both. Not cold, just not run by them.
  • You're not O.K. and you're not not O.K. Your worth isn't something others hand out or take back. You're simply you.
From the talks

Thomas Aquinas wrote some of the most influential theology ever produced. Then, the story goes, after an experience at Mass late in life, he stopped. When his secretary begged him to finish his great work, he said that everything he'd written seemed like straw next to what he had seen. De Mello loves the story: the best minds end up at a loss for words, because words aren't the thing.

In plain words

Notice whom you compare yourself with, and how that group changed the last time you moved up. You can keep working and keep improving. Just stop using the race to decide whether you're worth something.

Next: Attachment or preference →
Chapter 14 · Attachment and preference

Desire, Not Preference; Clinging to Illusion; Hugging Memories

The book's sections Desire, Not Preference · Clinging to Illusion · Hugging Memories
In short

An attachment is a belief that without something or someone you can't be happy. A preference is wanting it without that belief. Drop the attachment and keep the preference, and you can enjoy everything without being enslaved by it. Memories work the same way: we hug our picture of the past instead of what's here.

The big ideas

  • An attachment is a belief. "I can't be happy without this" isn't a fact about the world. It's a conviction you were taught, and convictions can be seen through.
  • Understood desire becomes preference. Don't suppress desire. Understand it, see its true worth, and it turns into a preference: welcome when it comes, not a disaster when it doesn't.
  • Clinging to illusion. We hold on to ideas of how things should be because letting go feels like losing ourselves. It's only losing the illusion.
  • Hugging memories. Meeting an old friend, you may be embracing your memory of them, not the person standing there now.
From the talks

De Mello imagines someone embracing a friend they haven't seen in years. The warmth is real, but whom is it for? Often it's for the friend of twenty years ago, the one in the memory. The person in front of you has changed, and if you only greet the memory, you miss them. The same happens with old hurts: what wounds us now is usually a memory being replayed, not anything happening today.

In plain words

List three things you're sure you can't be happy without. For each, ask: is that true, or is it something I was told? You can keep wanting all three. Just try wanting them without the "or else".

Next: Words are not the thing →
Chapter 15 · Words are not the thing

Getting Concrete, At a Loss for Words, Cultural Conditioning, and Filtered Reality

The book's sections Getting Concrete · At a Loss for Words · Cultural Conditioning · Filtered Reality
In short

Concepts freeze a flowing world into fixed pieces. Name a bird and you stop seeing it. Your culture told you whom to love and whom to fear before you could choose, and your filters decide what reaches you at all. Awareness means coming back to your senses and looking again.

The big ideas

  • Name it and stop seeing it. De Mello quotes Krishnamurti: once you teach a child the word "bird", the child may never really see a bird again. They see the word.
  • Even "God" can get in the way. Our idea of God can block the experience of God. De Mello says concepts point at reality; they never contain it.
  • Who taught you to hate? A baby born in America and raised in Russia would grow up feeling what Russians feel about Americans. National hatreds are learned, not discovered.
  • Filtered reality. Like a president who only hears what aides pass up, we receive a world already sorted by our fears, beliefs and habits.
From the talks

De Mello leans on Krishnamurti here. A child sees a bird for the first time and is absorbed: the colours, the hop, the cocked head. Then someone says "That's a sparrow," and something closes. Next time, the child sees "sparrow" and moves on. Concepts are useful, he says. They just aren't the thing, and we keep mistaking them for it.

In plain words

Pick something you see every day, a tree, a mug, a colleague's face, and look at it for a minute as if you had no word for it. Notice how much the word had been hiding.

Next: The approval drug →
Chapter 16 · The approval drug

Detachment, Addictive Love, More Words, Hidden Agendas, and Giving In

The book's sections Detachment · Addictive Love · More Words · Hidden Agendas · Giving In
In short

Society got you hooked early on a drug: approval, appreciation, attention. Once hooked, you live for the next dose and dread withdrawal, and you call the craving love. Real love is clear seeing and an honest response. Detachment isn't coldness; it's being free enough to love.

The big ideas

  • The drug called approval. As a child you were given a taste for it, then taught you'd be miserable without it. Now the people who supply it control you.
  • Addiction isn't love. If you can't be happy without someone, you need them, and need brings fear, jealousy and control. Love doesn't.
  • Hidden agendas. Before you speak or help, ask what you're really after. Often it's another dose.
  • Aloneness, not loneliness. Drop the need for people and you aren't left lonely. You're alone, free, and finally able to enjoy them.
From the talks

De Mello describes a man who realised, with some shock, that he didn't need his closest friend in order to be happy. He was afraid that meant he loved the friend less. It turned out the opposite way: without the need, there was nothing to protect and nothing to demand, and the friendship got easier and warmer.

In plain words

Notice the moments today when you're fishing: for a like, a thank-you, a sign you did well. You don't have to stop enjoying appreciation. Just see that you can live without it, and you'll stop being run by it.

Next: Through you, not by you →
Part five · love and reality

Love and Reality

The last sections bring it home. Dying to the "me", changing through insight rather than effort, meeting people without yesterday's images, and living fully in the present. Love, for de Mello, is what remains when fear and need are gone.

The idea behind this part

Love can't be defined, only uncovered. Drop what isn't love and what's left is it.

Chapter 17 · Through you, not by you

Assorted Landmines, The Death of Me, Insight and Understanding, and Not Pushing It

The book's sections Assorted Landmines · The Death of Me · Insight and Understanding · Not Pushing It
In short

Dying to self means dying to the "me", the bundle of labels and demands, not the body. Insight, not effort, is what changes you. Pushing a stalled car down the road is not the same as fixing its engine.

The big ideas

  • Watch for landmines. Some ideas feel like progress and keep you asleep: needing to be right, needing to be good, needing results from your awareness.
  • The death of the me. Imagine yourself lying in a coffin. From there, look at what you were so worried about. Most of it shrinks, and you start to live.
  • Insight isn't information. Knowing that something is bad for you rarely changes you. Seeing it, really seeing it, does.
  • Don't push. Change happens through you, not by you. You can't force awareness; you can only stop getting in its way.
From the talks

De Mello's image for effort without insight is a car that's broken down. You can get out and push it, and it'll move a little while you're pushing, and stop the moment you tire. Or you can look under the bonnet and find what's wrong. Fix the engine and you don't need to push at all.

In plain words

If you've been pushing yourself to change something for months, stop and ask what's actually broken. The answer usually isn't "not enough willpower".

Chapter 18 · Seeing people fresh

Getting Real, Assorted Images, Saying Nothing About Love, and Losing Control

The book's sections Getting Real · Assorted Images · Saying Nothing About Love · Losing Control
In short

You can't say what love is, only what it isn't: not need, not image, not control. Most of the time we relate to our pictures of people, built from yesterday, rather than to the people themselves. Love means dropping those images and seeing the person now, and giving up the control we try to keep over them.

The big ideas

  • Images, not people. Every past hurt, compliment and habit adds a layer to your picture of someone. Soon you're talking to the picture.
  • Love can't be defined. De Mello prefers to say nothing about love directly. Remove fear, need and illusion, and love is what's there.
  • Love is clear seeing. To love someone you have to see them as they are, not as you need them to be, and respond to that.
  • Losing control. Trying to control people, and life, is fear in action. Letting go isn't defeat; it's waking up.
From the talks

De Mello's pictures of love come from nature. A rose doesn't decide to give its scent to good people and hold it back from bad ones; it just smells sweet to whoever comes near. A lamp lights up the room for everyone in it. A tree gives shade to the one who waters it and the one who cuts it down. Love like that has no conditions, because it was never about getting something back.

In plain words

Next time you see someone close to you, try to meet them as if for the first time. Leave last week's argument and last year's label at the door. You may be surprised who's there.

Chapter 19 · Where's the fire?

Listening to Life, The End of Analysis, Dead Ahead, and The Land of Love

The book's sections Listening to Life · The End of Analysis · Dead Ahead · The Land of Love
In short

Life is something to listen to, not to analyse to death. Live each moment fully, dying to the past as you go. And remember what religion so easily forgets: the point was always the fire, never the shrine built to honour it.

The big ideas

  • Listen to life. Stop commenting and start hearing. Life is happening now, and most of us miss it narrating it to ourselves.
  • Analysis isn't awareness. Analysing your problems can become one more way to stay in your head. Awareness simply sees.
  • Die to the past. To live fully you have to let each moment go as it ends, without dragging it into the next.
  • The land of love. When fear and need are gone, what's left is love. Not a feeling you produce, but a place you arrive.
From the talks

De Mello's closing parable: a man discovers how to make fire and goes from tribe to tribe teaching people to cook and keep warm. He becomes so popular that the priests feel threatened and have him killed. Then, to keep the people's loyalty, they put his portrait on an altar, with the tools he used, and teach people to revere them. For centuries the worship goes on, with prayers and rituals. But there's no fire. "Where's the fire?" asks de Mello. That, for him, is the danger in all religion: honouring the tools and forgetting what they were for.

In plain words

Whatever you believe or practise, ask now and then whether it still gives you fire: more awareness, more love, more freedom. If it's only a beautiful altar, light the fire again.
